\title{Chaos, growth and firm life cycles in a scale-invariant Lotka--Volterra economy}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
We study a generalised Lotka--Volterra model of firms on a heterogeneous interaction network, in
which interactions are measured in units of the mean firm size.  The economy then grows
exponentially at a self-consistent rate while its composition follows a disordered replicator
equation.  Heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory gives the phase diagram.  For an
uncorrelated network the unique stationary composition loses stability at a disorder
$\sigma_c\simeq\sqrt2\,[1-\tfrac12\Var\kappa\,(1-\mu^2/2\pi)]$, to first order in the degree
variance; above it, trapping equilibria coexist with chaos, and the boundary between growing and
shrinking economies is nearly independent of the network.  In the chaotic, growing phase each
firm is an independent logistic unit driven by a Gaussian fitness whose mean and amplitude are set
by its degree.  The one-firm equation is solvable for any fitness path: a firm exits on the
first fitness dip whose area exceeds an explicit threshold, which yields its lifetime law.  Under
the unbalanced panel used for firm data, a firm's volatility is its log range over its lifetime,
and the multiscaling of the size--volatility relation follows from the growing spread of
lifetimes with size.  The model reproduces the empirical shape of the multiscaling but not the
level of the size--volatility exponent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but not by as much as independent subunits
would imply.  If $v_i$ is the volatility of firm $i$'s growth rate and $\bar S_i$ its mean size,
the conditional moments scale as $\E[v^q\mid\bar S]\sim\bar S^{-\zeta_q}$ with $\beta=\zeta_1\approx0.2$
\cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002}, and the ratio curve $\zeta_q/\zeta_1\approx2.0$, $2.6$, $2.9$
for $q=2,3,4$ bends below the line $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=q$ of a volatility law of fixed shape
\cite{moran2024}.  Granular explanations attribute both to the internal structure of firms
\cite{wyartbouchaud2003,moran2024}; network explanations to the propagation of shocks between them
\cite{acemoglu2012,herskovic2020}.

Here we ask what an economy of interacting firms does when nothing in it is tuned to grow.  We use
the generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV) model, the reference model of disordered interacting
populations \cite{may1972,bunin2017,galla2018,roy2020}, with one change: interactions and
self-limitation are measured in units of the mean firm size.  The model becomes scale invariant.
The aggregate grows exponentially at a rate the dynamics selects, and the composition of the
economy, the shares of the firms, obeys a disordered replicator equation \cite{diederich1989}.
Simulations on a power-law interaction network reproduce fat-tailed growth rates, a
size--volatility law and multiscaling.

We give the theory of this behaviour in three steps.  Heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory
(HDMFT) \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} gives the phase diagram, including a closed form for the
stability line (Sec.~\ref{sec:phase}).  In the chaotic phase the network reduces to independent
firms driven by a Gaussian fitness of known law (Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  The one-firm problem is
solvable, and under the empirical protocol the size--volatility statistics are those of firm life
cycles (Sec.~\ref{sec:life}).  We then compare with data (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}).  Derivations, checks
and further results are in the Supplemental Material (SM).

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

Firm $i=1,\dots,N$ has size $x_i$, and $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ is the mean size.  The relative GLV is
\begin{equation}
  \dot x_i=x_i\Bigl[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\frac1m\sum_jW_{ij}x_j\Bigr]+\lambda(m-x_i),
  \label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with $W_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$ on a configuration-model graph of degree law
$p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and mean degree $C$, independent Gaussian $z_{ij}$, and $\mu<0$ for
competition.  The immigration term $\lambda(m-x_i)$ keeps firms above a floor and acts as entry.
We use $N=8000$, $C=N/40$, $\lambda=10^{-3}$, $\alpha\in[2.2,6]$, and the operating point
$(\mu,\sigma)=(-1.76,1.75)$.

The right-hand side is homogeneous of degree one in $x$.  With relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\dot m}{m}=g(t)=\langle SF\rangle,\qquad
  \dot S_i=S_i\bigl[F_i-g(t)\bigr]+\lambda(1-S_i),
  \label{eq:replicator}
\end{equation}
with $F_i=1-S_i+(WS)_i$: the scale grows at the share-weighted mean fitness, and the composition is
a replicator equation.  Writing $\Phi_i=1-g+(WS)_i$ for the \emph{fitness} of firm $i$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\ln S_i}{dt}=\Phi_i(t)-S_i+\lambda\bigl(S_i^{-1}-1\bigr),
  \label{eq:firm}
\end{equation}
a logistic equation with a moving carrying capacity.  Averaging over any stationary stretch gives
$\bar S_i=\bPhi_i$ up to the floor term: a firm's mean size is its mean fitness.

\section{Mean-field theory and phase diagram}
\label{sec:phase}

For $C\to\infty$ the field on a firm of relative degree $\kappa=k/C$ becomes Gaussian.  The HDMFT
replaces Eq.~\eqref{eq:firm} by one stochastic process per degree class, closed by
self-consistent averages over neighbours, which are drawn in proportion to degree.  For an
uncorrelated kernel the fitness is
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(t)=1-g(t)+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
  \label{eq:fitness}
\end{equation}
with $\E[\xi(t)\xi(t')]=Q(t-t')$,
and $\bar B$ and $Q$ degree-weighted averages of $S$ and $S(t)S(t')$.  The structural cutoff of
the hubs is handled with a soft-configuration kernel \cite{patil2026}.

\paragraph{Stability line.}
If the composition relaxes, the stationary abundances are clipped Gaussians and the unique fixed
point loses stability when the Perron root of $\sigma^2\varphi_pK_{pr}$ reaches one, with $\varphi_p$
the survival fraction of class $p$ and $K_{pr}$ the connection kernel.  On a regular graph the
mean interaction is a uniform shift that cancels in the replicator, and
$\sigma_c=\sqrt2/(1+\gamma)$ for coupling reciprocity $\gamma$, independent of $\mu$.  On a
heterogeneous, uncorrelated graph the stability operator has rank one, and the threshold reduces
to two scalar equations: with $\Delta_\kappa=A\kappa^{-1/2}+M\kappa^{1/2}$ and $w_n(\Delta)=\int_{-\Delta}^\infty
(\Delta+z)^n\,\phi(z)\,dz$,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{gathered}
  \sigma_c^{-2}=\bigl\langle\kappa^2\PhiN(\Delta_\kappa)\bigr\rangle,\\
  \bigl\langle\kappa^2\Delta_\kappa w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\bigr\rangle=0,\qquad
  M=\mu\bigl\langle\kappa^{3/2}w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\bigr\rangle .
  \end{gathered}
  \label{eq:sigmac}
\end{equation}
To first order in the degree variance,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_c(\mu)\simeq\sqrt2\Bigl[1-\frac{\Var\kappa}{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\mu^2}{2\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr].
  \label{eq:sigmac-v}
\end{equation}
Degree heterogeneity lowers the threshold, because hubs feel noise of amplitude $\propto\sqrt\kappa$.
A mean interaction of either sign raises it; beyond first order competition raises it more,
because it removes the noisiest firms.  Equation~\eqref{eq:sigmac} agrees with the full HDMFT to
$0.002$ at $\alpha=6$ and $0.03$ at $\alpha=3.5$ (SM).  Heavy-tailed graphs are the least stable at weak
competition and the most stable at strong competition (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}b).

\paragraph{Growth.}
Since $g^*=1-a$ for the fixed-point closure, the zero-growth line follows by fixing $a=1$.  On a
regular graph it is $\mu^{(0)}=-g_0(\sigma)$, with $g_0=1-\Delta/w_1(\Delta)$ and $\sigma^2w_2(\Delta)=1$.
Heterogeneity shifts it by $c(\sigma)\Var\kappa$, with $c$ running from $0$ at weak disorder to
$5/4-\pi/2\simeq-0.32$ at $\sigma_c$.  The coefficient is at most a third of that of $\sigma_c$, which is
why the growth boundary hardly depends on the topology.  Its continuation above $\sigma_c$ tracks the
simulated contour to $0.03$ in $\mu$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}a).

\paragraph{Above the line.}
Below $\sigma_c$ three different initial conditions freeze into the same state.  At $\sigma_c$ no
simulated run is chaotic.  Above it, runs are either trapped in one of many stable equilibria or
chaotic with a persistent amplitude, and which one depends on the realisation: at $\mu=-1.97$ the
chaotic fraction of ten graphs is $0.2$, $0.1$, $0.8$ and $1$ at $\sigma-\sigma_c=0.025$, $0.05$, $0.1$
and $0.2$, and at $\mu=-1.05$ chaos takes over only by $\sigma_c+0.2$.  (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}c).  This is the
multiple-attractor regime of the disordered GLV.  A simulated ``freezing line'' that classifies
cells by the churn of their growth rates lies $0.1$--$0.3$ above $\sigma_c$ and is a statistic of this
coexistence region, not a second transition.  The region of failed integrations at weak
competition and strong disorder is a numerical artefact of the solver tolerance: there the
economy is strongly concentrated and grows fast, but stays bounded.

\section{Firms as independent units}
\label{sec:onefirm}

At the operating point the composition never settles: over a few hundred time units most firms
fall to the floor and climb back.  Equation~\eqref{eq:fitness} predicts the fitness of every firm.
Within a firm, $\Phi_i=\bPhi_i+f_i\eta_i(t)$ with $\eta_i$ a smooth unit-variance process of
correlation time $\tau$, and at fixed degree
\begin{equation}
  \bPhi\mid\kappa\sim\mathcal N\bigl(r_\kappa,\;\sigma^2\kappa Q_T\bigr),\qquad
  f_\kappa=\sigma\sqrt{\kappa(Q_0-Q_T)},
  \label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
with $r_\kappa=1-\bar g+\mu\kappa\bar B$ and $Q_T$ the part of $Q$ that is static over a window.
Five numbers and the degree law fix the fitness of every firm.  Measured firm by firm in the full
model, the static field is Gaussian once its predicted mean and spread are removed, the amplitude
grows as $\sqrt\kappa$ over the whole degree range, and the strong skew of $\bPhi$ on heavy-tailed
graphs comes from the mixture over degrees (Fig.~\ref{fig:fitness}).

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fitness_law.png}
  \caption{The fitness law against the full model.  Top: per-firm mean fitness, measured and sampled
  from Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.  Bottom: fitness amplitude against relative degree (median and quartiles),
  with $f\propto\sqrt\kappa$.}
  \label{fig:fitness}
\end{figure*}

Replacing the network by independent copies of Eq.~\eqref{eq:firm}, each driven by a Gaussian
fitness with its firm's measured $(\bPhi_i,f_i)$, reproduces the size--volatility exponent of the
full model to $0.02$ on every graph and its ratio curve to $0.25$ (SM).  The size--volatility
statistics are therefore properties of a single firm and of the distribution of fitness across
firms; the network enters only through Eq.~\eqref{eq:law}.

\section{Life cycles}
\label{sec:life}

Firm data form an unbalanced panel: every firm contributes over its own listed life
\cite{moran2024}.  In the model a firm is listed while $S>\Sthr$, and each listed spell counts as one
firm.  Its volatility is the MAD estimate $v=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\overline{|g_t-\bar g|}$ of its growth rates
$g_t=\Delta\ln S$ over $n$ steps.  For a spell that enters and leaves at $\Sthr$, exactly,
\begin{equation}
  v=\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}\;\frac{2\ell+V_p}{n},\qquad\ell=\ln\frac{S_{\max}}{\Sthr},
  \label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
with $V_p\ge0$ the variation in excess of one rise and fall.  Spells shorter than about $8\tau$ have
$V_p\simeq0$: a spell is a single hump in $\ln S$, and longer spells are a few humps merged by
fitness dips that did not end them.  A firm's volatility is thus essentially its log range over
its lifetime.

\paragraph{Exact solution and kill rule.}
At $\lambda=0$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:firm} is linear in $1/S$.  With $\mathcal A(t)=\int^t\Phi$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac1{S(t)}=\int_{-\infty}^te^{-[\mathcal A(t)-\mathcal A(t')]}\,dt' ,
  \label{eq:exact}
\end{equation}
so the size is a soft minimum over drawdowns of the integrated fitness.  Near a downcrossing of
$\Phi$ with slope $-s$ the solution is an inverse Mills ratio, and during a dip
$S\simeq\sqrt{s/2\pi}\,e^{-[\mathcal A(t_0)-\mathcal A(t)]}$.  A spell therefore ends at the first
negative excursion of the fitness whose area exceeds
\begin{equation}
  \ell_c(s)=\ln\frac{\sqrt{s/2\pi}}{\Sthr},
  \label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}
with no fitted constant; the rule classifies $96$--$98\%$ of dips correctly (Fig.~\ref{fig:life}a).

\paragraph{Lifetime law.}
Dips occur at Rice's rate $\nu(u)=e^{-u^2/2}/2\pi\tau$, with $u=\bPhi/f$, and end the spell with
probability $P_{\rm kill}(u)$, a level-crossing statistic of the Gaussian fitness computable from
the Slepian model.  The mean lifetime is
\begin{equation}
  \bar L(u)=\frac{\PhiN(u)}{\nu(u)\,P_{\rm kill}(u)},
  \label{eq:lifetime}
\end{equation}
which matches the one-firm process to $3\%$ for $u\ge1$ with no simulation input
(Fig.~\ref{fig:life}b,c).  For $u\gg1$ dips are short parabolae and
$-\ln P_{\rm kill}\simeq\tfrac12(3\ell_c/2f\tau)^{2/3}u^{4/3}$, so lifetime grows faster than any power of
size: the size--volatility exponent of the life-cycle part is an effective exponent that increases
along the size range.

\paragraph{Multiscaling.}
Write $v=A(\bar S)X$ with $X$ random at fixed size.  If $c_n$ are the cumulants of $\ln X$ at fixed
$\bar S$, then exactly
\begin{equation}
  \zeta_q-q\,\zeta_1=-\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{q^n-q}{n!}\,\frac{dc_n}{d\ln\bar S},
  \label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}
so at second-cumulant order the ratio curve bends below $q$ if and only if $\Var(\ln X\mid\bar S)$
grows with size.  For life cycles $X=\ell/n$ and the spread is that of $\ln n$.  Small firms live
through a single fitness excursion, of fairly regular length; large firms exit on rare,
memoryless kills, for which $\Var\ln n=\pi^2/6$.  In the one-firm process $\Var(\ln n\mid\bar S)$ rises
from $0.29$ to $0.65$ across the larger half of spells, and $\Var\ln L$ reaches $\pi^2/6$ at $u\simeq1.5$.
The bend is therefore a crossover between two kinds of exit.  On heavy-tailed graphs the spread
of fitness amplitudes, $f\propto\sqrt\kappa$, adds lifetime dispersion at fixed size and flattens the
ratio curve further; making $f$ homogeneous restores the plain life-cycle bend (SM).

\section{Comparison with data}
\label{sec:data}

On the spell panel the binned volatility has a hump at small sizes, made of spells that barely
clear the listing threshold, and a flat top that makes a peak-started fit unstable.  We fit all
exponents on the larger half of spells by size, which fixes $\beta$ to $\pm0.03$ between
realisations.  Figure~\ref{fig:data} and Table~\ref{tab:data} give the full model.

The shape of the multiscaling is robust: the ratio curve does not depend on the fit range, and on
weakly heterogeneous graphs it is close to the data.  Its bend is the life-cycle mechanism of
Sec.~\ref{sec:life}.  The level of $\beta$ is not robust.  It depends on the fitted size range,
because the decline branch steepens with size, as the lifetime law implies (Fig.~\ref{fig:data}a),
and on the entry size, which sets the rise term $\ell$.  No graph gives $\beta\approx0.2$ together
with a data-like ratio curve: light tails give the right curve with $\beta=0.55$, heavy tails
$\beta\approx0.2$ with a curve flattened by amplitude heterogeneity.  Model firms enter at about
$10^{-2}$ of the mean size, while real firms enter the data at their IPO size, so the model
probably overstates the rise-and-fall share; the steepening of the model's curve where the data
flatten is the main quantitative mismatch.

The theory makes three predictions for firm data.  Restricting a panel to firms present
throughout should raise $\beta$, since such firms are observed only on their plateau (in the model,
$0.41$ against $0.19$ at $\alpha=2.5$).  Equation~\eqref{eq:identity} gives a model-free lower bound
on each firm's volatility from its entry, peak and exit sizes and its lifetime.  And removing each
firm's entry and exit years, or conditioning on age, should move $\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ towards $q$.

\section{Discussion}

Measuring interactions in units of the mean size turns a disordered Lotka--Volterra community
into a growing economy whose composition is a replicator.  Its phase diagram is controlled by
the stability of the unique stationary composition, which degree heterogeneity lowers and
competition raises, while the growth boundary is almost blind to the network.  In the chaotic
phase each firm is a logistic unit tracking a Gaussian fitness, and the size--volatility
statistics measured on an unbalanced panel are those of firm life cycles: entry when the fitness
has risen enough, exit on the first dip of large enough area, and a volatility equal to the log
range over the lifetime.  Multiscaling is then a crossover in the spread of lifetimes rather than
an asymptotic law, in line with its reading as a subleading effect \cite{moran2024}.  Open
problems are a controlled growth line in the chaotic phase, which needs the two-time theory, the
fate of trapping as $N\to\infty$, a sparse cavity theory for the heaviest tails, where a firm's
field is carried by a few neighbours, and a theory of the spell exponents that includes the
fluctuations of the largest firms.

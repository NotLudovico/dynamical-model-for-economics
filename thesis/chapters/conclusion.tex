\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis asked whether a dynamical model of interacting firms can produce the
firm-growth anomalies by itself, without the heavy-tailed internal composition that the
granular models assume and that \citet{moran2024} show the data reject. A Generalized
Lotka--Volterra network, with no external heterogeneity, no injected
noise, and no built-in correlations, recovers the fat-tailed growth-rate distribution, a
size--variance relation that falls with the empirical sign, and every size-conditioned
test by which the data reject the granular picture: the anomalous multiscaling of the
higher volatility moments, the collapse of the rescaled volatility distribution with a
bounded tail, and the persistence of fat tails in the growth of the largest firms. All
come out as stationary properties of an economy that grows while its composition keeps
fluctuating.

The model is one change to the standard Generalized Lotka--Volterra system.
Self-limitation and interactions act on a firm's size relative to the average firm rather
than on its absolute size, which makes the dynamics scale-invariant. Total output then grows
exponentially, without the finite-time blow-up of the absolute model, and in the strongly
disordered regime the composition of the economy never settles but keeps churning
chaotically. The couplings themselves are entirely standard: the usual dilute
normalization of the disordered Lotka--Volterra literature, on a scale-free graph whose
mean degree scales with the number of firms.

The central finding is where the phenomena come from, and it splits cleanly in two.
Switching the disorder off and on shows the fluctuations and the fat-tailed growth
distribution to be made by the disordered interactions; they appear on every topology,
including a fully connected one. Swapping the degree distribution of the graph, with
everything else held fixed, shows the size-conditioned structure --- the low
size--variance exponent and the multiscaling --- to be made by the degree heterogeneity.
Under the standard normalization a firm's noise variance grows with its connectivity, so
the graph's degree distribution is printed onto the firm-level fluctuations. On
homogeneous graphs the multiscaling vanishes exactly, the conditional volatility keeping
a fixed shape at every size; as the degree distribution broadens the ratio curve bends
toward the data, and only the scale-free graph reaches it. The size-dependent
correlations that \citet{moran2024} name as the missing element of the granular picture
are generated here between firms, by the network. That is where they locate them too: the
correlations they invoke are attributed to ``supply chain effects affecting inputs or
customers common to all sub-units'', which are relations between firms rather than inside
one. A network account of the \emph{level} of firm volatility, resting on customer
concentration, already exists \citep{herskovic2020}; what the present model adds is that
the \emph{size-conditioned} statistics, the exponent and the multiscaling, follow from the
heterogeneity of the interaction graph.

Three qualifications bound the claim. First, neither the dynamics nor the construction is new,
and Chapter~\ref{chap:background} places both in their lineage: the fluctuating
regime is the chaotic phase of the disordered Lotka--Volterra model \citep{diederich1989,bunin2017,galla2018,roy2020},
and the scale invariance and mean-coupling are those of the econophysics models of wealth and
firm sizes \citep{bouchaudmezard2000,malcai2002}. What is
specific here is the combination: self-limitation and disordered interactions made relative
together, so that a network of competing firms grows endogenously and fluctuates from the
interactions alone, and the finding that the graph's heterogeneity generates the discriminating
firm-growth statistics. Second,
the quantitative match is partial, and the way it falls short is informative. The
multiscaling ratios and the conditional tests --- the shape of the size-conditioned
statistics --- are reproduced and are converged in system size. The absolute scale is
not: the size--variance exponent stands at $\beta \approx 0.45$ at our largest sizes
against the empirical $0.15$--$0.20$, still drifting slowly upward, and the growth
tent's kurtosis falls slowly with size while its shape holds. What the model predicts
robustly is the shape of the statistics; their scale is a finite-size quantity. Third,
the growth is near-critical: the operating point sits in the growing band but not far
from its boundaries, and stronger disorder or a lighter-tailed graph loses most
realizations. None of this undoes the qualitative result, but each qualification shows
how far it still is from a quantitative theory.

The next steps follow from these limits. The exponent's slow upward drift asks for its
limit, and for the size distribution of the
model's firms, whose tail would tie the exponent's scale to the Zipf law the data pair
it with. The plateau the immigration floor imposes on the smallest firms is worth a second
look as well, now that \citet{fontanelli2025} report an empirical size--volatility relation
that steepens for small firms and flattens for large ones rather than following a single
power law. The correlation structure matters more than
either: the match shown here is in the marginal facts and their moments, whereas the
size-dependent correlations that \citet{moran2024} call for are generated directly by
the model and can be measured firm by firm, on the network. That measurement is the
model's distinctive prediction, that the correlations live between firms rather than
inside them. Further out,
replacing the schematic disordered graph with couplings closer to the real economy, its
input--output linkages, supply networks, and shared markets, would take the model from
reproducing the stylized facts to saying something about the macroeconomic dynamics
behind them.

\chapter{Finite-size scaling of the exponents across topologies}
\label{app:scaling}

Section~\ref{sec:growing-finite-size} reported that the multiscaling ratios are converged in
system size while the size--variance exponent $\beta$ drifts, and gave the drift for the
power-law-$2.5$ graph. Because the drift is the sharpest quantitative caveat on the model, this
appendix documents it for every topology of Section~\ref{sec:growing-graph}, so that what is a
finite-size transient and what is a converged prediction can be told apart at a glance.
Figure~\ref{fig:scaling-topology} and Table~\ref{tab:scaling} give the size--variance exponent
and the highest multiscaling ratio $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ against $N$, at the fixed dilution
$C = N/40$, for each degree distribution. The fully-connected graph is dense and cannot be
carried past $N = 16000$ on the available hardware; the regular graph, homogeneous but sparse,
provides the homogeneous control at the largest sizes.

Two patterns separate cleanly. The multiscaling ratio (right panel) is flat in $N$ for every
topology to within its error above $N \approx 8000$: the homogeneous graphs sit on the
fixed-shape value $4$ at every size, and each heterogeneous graph holds its own value
($\zeta_4/\zeta_1 \approx 3.4$ for the power-law-$3.5$ graph, $\approx 3.1$ for power-law-$2.5$,
$\approx 2.7$ for power-law-$2.2$) across the range $N = 8000$ to $32000$. Carried down to
$N = 2000$, the sparse graphs tell the rest of the story: below $N \approx 8000$ their ratios
converge toward one another near the fixed-shape value (the power-law-$2.5$ graph rises from its
$3.1$ plateau to $3.3$ at $N = 4000$ and $3.8$ at $N = 2000$, and the regular and power-law-$3.5$
graphs bunch nearby), so the separation of the plateaus by degree distribution is itself a
large-$N$ effect that finite size washes out. The multiscaling is
thus a genuine thermodynamic property of each ensemble, set by the degree distribution and not
by the system size, and the empirical value $2.9$ is bracketed between the power-law-$2.5$ and
power-law-$2.2$ graphs at every $N$ where the plateaus have separated.

The size--variance exponent (left panel) behaves differently. It creeps upward with $N$ for
every topology across the whole range $N = 2000$ to $32000$, but the drift and its limit are
organized by the degree distribution. On the homogeneous graphs $\beta$ is already near $1$ and
nearly flat: the fully-connected graph sits at $\approx 1.02$, the regular graph creeps from
$0.73$ at $N = 2000$ to $0.99$ at $32000$ and levels off. The heterogeneous graphs climb further but level off more slowly:
the power-law-$3.5$ graph runs from $0.61$ to $0.80$ and the power-law-$2.5$ graph from $0.32$ at
$N = 2000$ through $0.43$ at $8000$--$16000$ to $0.49$ at $32000$. The step in $\beta$ per doubling
of $N$ shrinks along each curve, the signature of a finite-size approach to a limit rather than
an unbounded drift, and extrapolating in $1/N$ (the form that pins the regular graph at
$\beta_\infty = 1.00$, $R^2 = 0.999$) sends every topology to a finite value: $\beta_\infty
\approx 0.8$ for power-law-$3.5$ and $\approx 0.5$ for power-law-$2.5$, though for the heavier tail
the limit is only loosely constrained by these sizes. The power-law-$2.2$ graph converges
fastest and is already flat over the measured range: $0.16$ at $N = 8000$, $0.22$ at $16000$,
$0.23$ at $32000$, the last two agreeing within their errors, its exponent essentially settled
just above the empirical band $0.15$--$0.20$. (It is not carried below $N = 8000$: the most
heterogeneous graph is also the most fragile, and at $N = 2000$--$4000$ most of its realizations
freeze, leaving a survivor-biased handful with anomalously high exponents.) Every topology thus
appears to approach a finite large-$N$ exponent set by its degree tail; only for the heavy-tailed
graphs is that limit still loosely pinned at the sizes reached here. What the sweep establishes is
therefore not a single matched exponent but a monotonic control: the degree-tail exponent sets
where $\beta$ lands, spanning the whole range from $1$ down through the empirical band, while it
leaves the multiscaling ratio fixed. The size-conditioned \emph{shape} is the converged
prediction of the model; the absolute exponent is a finite-size quantity whose large-$N$ value
the topology tunes toward, and in the most heterogeneous case into the neighbourhood of, the
data.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{meandeg_scaling_topology.png}
    \caption{Finite-size scaling of the exponents at fixed dilution $C = N/40$, for the five
    degree distributions of Section~\ref{sec:growing-graph} (7--20 economies per point), with the
    fully-connected, regular, power-law-$3.5$ and power-law-$2.5$ curves carried down to
    $N = 2000$ to show the finite-size approach; the power-law-$2.2$ graph freezes at these small
    sizes and is not carried below $N = 8000$.
    \panel{Left}: the size--variance exponent $\beta$ against $N$; it drifts upward for every
    topology, but the drift shrinks and the value falls as the degree distribution broadens,
    from $\beta \approx 1$ on the homogeneous graphs to $\beta \approx 0.23$, converged, on the
    power-law-$2.2$ graph, just above the empirical band. \panel{Middle}: the highest
    multiscaling ratio $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ against $N$; flat in $N$ above $N \approx 8000$ for every
    topology, set by the degree distribution, with the empirical value bracketed between the
    power-law-$2.5$ and power-law-$2.2$ graphs. \panel{Right}: the same exponents plotted against
    $1/N$, where the infinite-size limit is the intercept at $1/N = 0$ (stars); the dashed lines
    are linear fits, which send every graph to a finite $\beta_\infty$ (the same fit pins the
    regular graph at $1.00$, the power-law-$3.5$ at $0.79$ and the power-law-$2.5$ at $0.47$),
    evidence that $\beta$ approaches a finite topology-set limit rather than drifting without
    bound. The fully-connected graph, dense, is not run
    beyond $N = 16000$.}
    \label{fig:scaling-topology}
\end{figure}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:intro}

The natural baseline for firm growth is Gibrat's law of proportionate effect, which takes the
growth of a firm over a period to be a random
shock independent of its current size \citep{gibrat1931}, so that a firm's log-size follows a
random walk and the average growth rate and its spread are the same at every size. Much of the empirical
firm-growth literature is the study of where this baseline breaks \citep{sutton1997}, and it
breaks in two definite ways that we take as our starting point.

The first is in the volatility of the growth rate. Measured year over year,
a firm's growth volatility falls with its size as a power law,
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(S) \propto S^{-\beta},
  \label{eq:intro-sizevar}
\end{equation}
with a small exponent $\beta \approx 0.15$--$0.20$ that is stable across datasets
\citep{stanley1996, amaral1997}. Gibrat's law makes the volatility independent of size,
$\beta = 0$; a firm assembled from many independent parts would instead average them down to
$\beta = 1/2$; the measured exponent lies between the two.

The second is in the shape of the growth distribution. Independent multiplicative shocks would
make it Gaussian, but the demeaned growth rate is instead sharply peaked and fat-tailed, close to
the Laplace form \citep{stanley1996, bottazzi2006}, and it keeps that same shape within every
size class of firms. We will refer to these two throughout as the
size--variance law and the growth tent. A model of firm growth has to produce both, and the
difficulty is to produce them together.

\section{The granular explanation and where it fails}
\label{sec:intro-granular}

\subsection{The granular model}
\label{sec:intro-granular-model}

The standard explanation treats a firm as an aggregate of many smaller units, each carrying
its own idiosyncratic shock. A firm made of $K$ sub-units has size
$S = \sum_{i=1}^{K} s_i$, and over a year each sub-unit grows by an
independent random shock, $s_i \to s_i(1 + \eta_i)$, with the $\eta_i$ of mean zero and common
variance $\sigma_0^2$. The firm's growth rate is then the share-weighted average of these
shocks,
\begin{equation}
  g = \frac{\Delta S}{S} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} w_i\, \eta_i,
  \qquad w_i = \frac{s_i}{S},
\end{equation}
and, the shocks being independent, its variance is
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^2(S) = \sigma_0^2 \sum_{i=1}^{K} w_i^2 = \sigma_0^2\, H,
\end{equation}
set entirely by the Herfindahl index $H = \sum_i w_i^2$ of the sub-unit shares.

If the units are of comparable
size, then $w_i \approx 1/K$ and $H \approx 1/K$, and with $K$ proportional to
$S$ we obtain $\sigma(S) \propto S^{-1/2}$, that is $\beta = 1/2$, well above the measured
exponent, so the shares cannot be even. $H$ has to decay more slowly than $1/S$,
and this happens only when a few large units carry a finite share of the firm and their shocks
dominate the sum.

\citet{gabaix2011} established this mechanism at the level of the whole economy, with the
units being firms and the aggregate GDP. Firm sizes follow a
Zipf-like law \citep{axtell2001}, a heavy tail $P(s > x) \sim x^{-\nu}$ with $\nu$ close to one, and for such a
tail the Herfindahl index does not vanish as $1/N$ but much more slowly,
\begin{equation}
  H \sim N^{-2\left(1 - 1/\nu\right)}, \qquad 1 < \nu < 2,
\end{equation}
because the sum of squared sizes is carried by the largest few. Their shocks do not cancel,
and aggregate fluctuations instead decay as $N^{-(1 - 1/\nu)}$.
\citet{sutton2002} and \citet{wyartbouchaud2003} carried the argument back inside a single
firm. With $K \propto S$ sub-units whose sizes carry the same heavy tail,
$\sigma(S) \propto S^{-(1 - 1/\nu)}$, so
\begin{equation}
  \beta = 1 - \frac{1}{\nu},
  \label{eq:intro-beta-nu}
\end{equation}
which lands anywhere between $0$ and $1/2$ as the tail runs from Zipf ($\nu \to 1$) to thin
($\nu \to 2$). A heavy-tailed distribution of sub-unit sizes thus delivers both starting facts
from a single assumption. It
gives a variance that falls too slowly with size, and a growth distribution that stays
fat-tailed because the sum in $g$ is carried by a handful of dominant units. A tail slightly
above the Zipf value, $\nu \approx 1.2$, lands the size--variance exponent in the
measured range. On the two facts we began with, the granular model works.

\subsection{Where it fails: conditioning on size}
\label{sec:intro-granular-fail}

Those two facts are \emph{marginal}: each averages over all firms of a given size, reporting a
typical volatility and a pooled growth distribution. \citet{moran2024} sharpen the test by
conditioning on size and asking about the whole distribution of growth and volatility within
each size class, not merely its typical value. The granular composition makes definite
predictions there, and on three of them the data break it.

First, a firm's growth rate divided by its own
volatility is an average of the firm's
independent shocks, which by the central limit theorem is closer to Gaussian the more
sub-units the firm has, so the largest
firms should be the most Gaussian. In the data the rescaled growth
stays fat-tailed at every size.

Second, a firm's
volatility divided by the typical volatility of firms its size has, in the granular model, a
size-independent distribution, which the data confirm. The model, however, also predicts an
excess of anomalously volatile large firms, those built from only a few sub-units, whose
volatility does not shrink with size; this would appear as a hump in the rescaled
distribution, and the data show none.

Third, each higher moment of the volatility
distribution falls with size as its own power law,
$E[\sigma^q \mid S] \sim S^{-\zeta_q}$, the first exponent being the size--variance law again,
$\zeta_1 = \beta$. The granular
model predicts the exponents to be independent of the order $q$. The poorly diversified
firms of the previous test keep a volatility of order $\sigma_0$ whatever their size, so
they contribute $\sigma_0^q$ times their fraction among firms of size $S$ to the $q$-th
moment. Only that fraction decays with $S$, and $\sigma_0^q$ carries no size dependence,
so every moment beyond the first is dominated by this contribution and the exponents
collapse to a single value, $\zeta_q = \zeta_{q'}$ for all $q, q' \ge 2$. The measured exponents instead keep rising with the order,
$\zeta_1,\dots,\zeta_4 \approx 0.20,\,0.39,\,0.51,\,0.58$ \citep{moran2024}. We call this
$q$-dependence the multiscaling of the size-conditioned volatility: the volatility at a given
size is governed not by one scale but by a distribution of them, whose shape changes as the firm
grows.

A conditional
volatility with a single scale (a fixed distributional shape whose level alone slides with
size) gives exponents locked to the first, $\zeta_q = q\,\zeta_1$, a straight line in $q$,
while the granular tail flattens them to a constant beyond $q = 1$. The data lie
between these two reference behaviours, rising but sublinearly; \citet{moran2024} attribute the shortfall below the
straight line to subleading corrections from the incipient tail of their volatility
distribution. Whatever its asymptotic fate, the measured $q$-dependence is the observable that
separates the data from the granular composition, and it is the one this thesis sets out to
generate.

From the three failures \citet{moran2024} conclude that the granular models lack a
correlation the data possess: that the shocks hitting a firm become ``more and more correlated
as its size increases, due either to supply chain effects affecting inputs or customers common
to all sub-units''. The missing ingredient is a correlation that grows with firm size, and its
proposed sources point at relations between firms rather than inside them.

\section{A model of interacting firms}
\label{sec:intro-move}

Those sources (shared customers, common suppliers) are precisely the premise of the
production-network account of the macroeconomy, and of the one existing network model of firm
volatility (Chapter~\ref{chap:background}). The granular models cannot reach them: with no
relation between firms at all, they are, in the phrase of \citet{moran2024}, ``island models''.
We take the between-firm premise one level down, to the firm-growth statistics themselves,
treating the firms as the interacting parts and the economy as the aggregate, and asking
whether the interactions between firms can generate the size-conditioned statistics.

Concretely, we model the economy as a network of interacting firms, each node a company and
each edge a signed competitive or cooperative coupling, drawn at random into a disordered
interaction matrix. The dynamics are those of the Generalized Lotka--Volterra system from
theoretical ecology, each firm growing logistically and coupled to its neighbours through that
matrix. Even in the disordered regime where its dynamics stay
chaotic, firm sizes in this system remain bounded, so on its own it cannot represent a steadily
expanding economy. In the model of this thesis the self-limitation and the
interactions instead act on each firm's size relative to the average firm,
which makes the dynamics scale-invariant. Scale invariance under a rescaling of all sizes,
and the mean-coupled form it takes, are inherited from the econophysics models of wealth and
firm sizes (Chapter~\ref{chap:background}); those models drive the fluctuations with an
injected external noise, while here they are generated by the disordered
interactions themselves, in a persistently fluctuating state from which we read off the growth
statistics of the firms. The model also adds a small immigration term, a
firm-entry rate that keeps firms from reaching exact extinction.
Total output then grows exponentially without diverging, and in the strongly disordered regime
the composition of the economy keeps changing chaotically. It is in this
state that the two facts appear together, and switching off the disorder removes both, so their
origin lies in the interactions. The model recovers the
anomalous multiscaling of the higher moments, the test the granular picture fails, and passes
the other two size-conditioned tests of \citet{moran2024} as well; the size--variance exponent
comes out with the right sign as a clean power law, its value set by the graph's degree tail,
which brackets the empirical $0.15$--$0.20$ between $\beta \approx 0.45$ at tail exponent $2.5$,
where the multiscaling ratios sit on the data, and $\beta = 0.21$ at exponent $2.2$, where the
exponent reaches the band while the ratios run a few percent low.
The thesis's sharpest finding is that the size-conditioned statistics are
generated by the degree heterogeneity of the network. Under the standard dilute normalization a
firm's noise variance grows with its connectivity, so the graph's degree distribution is printed
onto the firm-level fluctuations; on homogeneous graphs the multiscaling vanishes exactly, and
only a scale-free network reproduces the empirical curve. The network is the explanatory
ingredient, the production-network premise carried down to the
firm-growth statistics.

The rest of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} places the
model in the two bodies of work it draws on, the compositional and scale-invariant models
of firm growth on one side and the disordered Lotka--Volterra systems of theoretical
ecology on the other, and states what it takes from each.
Chapter~\ref{chap:growing} sets out the model.
It introduces the relative GLV and its scale-invariant growth, says why the obvious GLV will
not do and what the relative interaction fixes, and locates the chaotic, growing regime in
which the firm-growth statistics are measured. Chapter~\ref{chap:firmgrowth} measures the two
facts in the fluctuating state, sets out the estimator that measurement across many
realizations requires, recovers the multiscaling and the other two conditional tests that
separate the data from the granular models, and then dissects the mechanism: swapping the
degree distribution of the graph while holding everything else fixed shows the heterogeneity
to be what generates the size-conditioned statistics. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} draws
the results together and points to what would tie the model more tightly to the data, in
particular a direct measurement of the correlations between firms' growth rates that it
predicts. Six appendices hold the supporting material: a dynamical mean-field theory of the
relative model (Appendix~\ref{app:dmft}); finite-size scaling of the exponents across topologies
(Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}); the correlation analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:correlations}); survival
and re-entry dynamics (Appendix~\ref{app:survival}); the stationary firm-size distribution
(Appendix~\ref{app:size-distribution}); and the numerical methods and measurement protocol
(Appendix~\ref{app:numerics}).

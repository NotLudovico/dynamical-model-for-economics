\title{Interaction heterogeneity and the multiscaling of firm growth volatility}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The growth volatility of firms falls with size as $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q}$, and the exponents grow
more slowly than $q$.  We propose an economy of interacting firms: generalised Lotka--Volterra dynamics on a
scale-free network, with birth--death noise and turnover, and five parameters.  We score it on the
size--volatility observables of Moran, Secchi and Bouchaud.  Tuned to $\zeta_1\simeq0.2$, it gives ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ below $q$, tent-shaped growth rates, and rescaled growth rates that stay fat-tailed among the
largest firms.  It fails on the range: volatility falls over two orders of magnitude of size rather than four, by
a factor $2.8$ rather than $5.5$, and firm sizes have no Zipf tail.  Its lives are short, and its aggregate
volatility is too large.  We explain this behaviour with one firm in a moving field.  The decline runs from the
newborn size to the top of a thin-tailed size distribution, and from the noise of a newborn to a floor that the firm cannot average
away.  The ratios bend below $q$ because the floors of firms of equal size differ: entry and exit add large
growth rates that weigh more in a short life than in a long one, and a firm with more links feels a more volatile
field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but much more erratically than a sum of independent
parts would.  Let $v$ be the volatility of a firm's growth rate and $\Sb$ its mean size over its life.  In
Compustat the conditional moments scale as
\begin{equation}
\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q},
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
with $\zeta_q=0.20(1)$, $0.39(2)$, $0.51(3)$, $0.58(4)$ for $q=1,\dots,4$, over four orders of magnitude of size
\cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002,moran2024}.  The ratios $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.95$, $2.55$, $2.90$ lie
below $q$ \cite{moran2024}.

The bend has a simple meaning.  If a firm's volatility were a function of its size times a random factor
whose law does not depend on size, the ratios would be exactly $q$.  Ratios below $q$ therefore say that firms
of equal size differ more in volatility the larger they are.

Three further facts come with the bend.  Growth rates have tent-shaped tails, and they stay fat-tailed when
each firm's growth is rescaled by its own volatility, even among the largest firms \cite{moran2024}.  The
growth of a firm correlates more with the aggregate, and with firms of similar size, the larger the firm
\cite{moran2024}.  And firm sizes follow Zipf's law \cite{axtell2001}.

Granular models make a firm a sum of subunits with independent shocks \cite{wyartbouchaud2003,gabaix2011}.
They give $\zeta_1$ close to $0.2$ for suitable subunit sizes \cite{sutton2002}.  Moran, Secchi and Bouchaud
(MSB) tested them against the other facts \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms give $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$,
and firms made of a few subunits give ratios close to $1$; the data lie between.  The rescaled growth of a
granular firm becomes Gaussian, and independent parts do not co-move.  MSB conclude that granular mechanisms
alone cannot account for the data, and point to correlations that grow with size as the missing ingredient
\cite{moran2024,herskovic2020}.

Other models get parts of the picture.  Customer--supplier networks make volatilities co-move
\cite{herskovic2020}, proportional growth of products gives tent-shaped growth \cite{fu2005}, and agent-based
models of firm formation give Zipf's law \cite{axtell2016}.

Firms interact: they compete for the same customers, and they buy from and sell to each other
\cite{acemoglu2012,carvalho2019}.  Interactions make firms co-move.  They keep the environment of a firm
changing, so even a large firm cannot average its volatility away.  And they make firms enter and exit.  We
therefore ask whether an economy of interacting firms, with nothing about a single firm put in by hand, can
produce the facts, and where it fails.  We take the simplest such dynamics, the generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV) model, whose
strongly interacting phase keeps fluctuating \cite{roy2020,mallmin2024,arnouxbunin2024}.  Firms grow, limit
themselves, and gain or lose from the firms they are linked to on a scale-free network.  We add birth--death
noise and turnover: a firm that reaches zero exits, and a newborn takes its place.

Section~\ref{sec:economy} presents the model and its phase diagram.  Section~\ref{sec:data} scores it on the
observables of MSB: what it reproduces, what it reproduces in part, and where it fails.  Section~\ref{sec:theory}
explains both sides from one firm in a moving field: why volatility falls with size, why the decline spans only
two orders of magnitude, and why the ratio curve bends.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the limits and the
open problems.  A notebook of the companion code prints every number \cite{code}, and Table~\ref{tab:notation}
lists the symbols.

\section{An economy of interacting firms}
\label{sec:economy}

\paragraph{Model.}  We consider $N$ firms with sizes $x_i$, and $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ is the mean firm.  The firms sit
on the nodes of a configuration-model graph with degree law $p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and mean degree $C$.  Their
sizes evolve as
\begin{equation}
dx_i=x_i\Big[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\sum_jJ_{ij}\frac{x_j}{m}\Big]dt+\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}\;dW_i ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with independent Wiener processes $W_i$.  The couplings are $J_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$, with
$A$ the adjacency matrix and $z_{ij}$ independent standard Gaussians; $z_{ij}$ and $z_{ji}$ are uncorrelated.  A
firm that reaches zero exits, and a newborn of size $Dm$ takes its place on the same node.  The model has five
parameters: $\mu$, $\sigma$, $\alpha$, $C$ and $D$.

\paragraph{Reading the equation.}  The bracket is the growth rate of firm $i$.  The $1$ is the growth rate of a
firm alone.  The term $-x_i/m$ is self-limitation: a firm slows down as it saturates its own market.  The sum is
the effect of the linked firms.  A negative $J_{ij}$ means that firm $j$ takes growth from firm $i$, as a
competitor does; a positive one means that it feeds firm $i$, as a customer or a supplier does.  With $\mu<0$,
competition dominates on average.

The last term is birth--death noise, as for gains and losses of indivisible pieces of business of size $Dm$.  It
adds $Dm/x_i$ per unit time to the variance of the growth rate, so it matters only for small firms.  It is the
only granular, $1/\text{size}$ effect in the model.  Every term is homogeneous of degree one in the sizes, so the
economy has no preferred scale.

\paragraph{A logistic unit in a moving field.}  In relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$,
\begin{equation}
dS_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i+O(N^{-1/2}),
\label{eq:onefirm}
\end{equation}
with the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\bar f(t),
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
where $\bar f$ is the growth rate of the mean firm, entry dilution included (Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).
Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a logistic equation whose carrying capacity moves in time.  If the field stays at
$\Phi>0$, the firm relaxes to the plateau $x_i=m\Phi$.  If $\Phi<0$, the firm shrinks until it exits.

The field is the rate at which a firm would grow, relative to the mean firm, if it were very small.  Two firms
differ only through their fields, and the neighbours of a firm set its field.  The rest of the paper rests on
this reduction: the statistics of volatility follow from one firm and the statistics of its field.

A rescaling by $1-\bar f$ maps the relative sizes onto a standard GLV model on the same graph
(Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  On the complete graph it is the GLV model with demographic noise
\cite{altieri2021}, with a newborn in place of each firm that exits.  With immigration in place of the noise, the
strongly interacting phase of that model shows the chaotic turnover of Refs.~\cite{roy2020,mallmin2024}.

\paragraph{Phase diagram.}  The model has two regimes (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}).  For weak interactions the economy
relaxes: every field settles to a constant, and only the birth--death noise moves sizes.  For strong interactions
the fields keep moving, because the firms that make them keep rising and falling.  The facts need the second
regime, because the floor of a firm comes from the motion of its field.  The instability line $\sigma_c(\mu)$
separates the two regimes.  The zero-growth line separates economies whose mean firm grows from those where it
shrinks.

\paragraph{Theory of the phase diagram.}  For $C\to\infty$, heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory (HDMFT)
replaces the network by one process per degree class \cite{aguirrelopez,park2024,poley2025,patil2026}.  A node
of relative degree $\kappa=k/C$ feels
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)=1-\bar f+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
\label{eq:hdmft}
\end{equation}
with $\bar B$ the degree-weighted mean size and $\xi$ a Gaussian process (Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}).  Under
competition a better connected firm has a lower mean field, because it competes with more firms.  It also has
a field of larger amplitude, because it sums more random terms.

In the relaxing regime $\xi$ is nearly static (Table~\ref{tab:phase}), and the theory reduces to a static
self-consistency.  Each node then follows the exact stationary law of one firm at constant field, with noise and
turnover (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  The static solution loses stability at $\sigma_c(\mu)$.  Without noise
this line is known \cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026}; noise raises it almost linearly in $D$, as on the
complete graph \cite{altieri2021}.  The same solution gives the zero-growth line.

\paragraph{Test in simulation.}  In simulation the onset of the fluctuating regime follows the predicted line,
with nothing fitted (Fig.~\ref{fig:instability}).  The predicted zero-growth line is close to the simulated one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}a).  Our reference point is $\alpha=2.5$, $\sigma=1.70$, $D=0.1$, $C=200$, $\mu=-1.76$.  It
lies in the fluctuating phase ($\sigma_c=1.497$), close to the line on which the mean firm stops growing.

\section{The model against the MSB observables}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  We integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} in relative form and record sizes every $\Delta t=0.5$,
which we take as one year (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  A \emph{life} runs from a birth to the next exit at the
same node.  Lives cut by the observation window are kept, as in MSB's unbalanced panel.  A life with $n$ growth
rates $g_t=\ln S_{t+1}-\ln S_t$ has volatility and size
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|g_t-\bar g\big|,\qquad
\Sb=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}S_t,
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
the factor making $v$ the standard deviation for Gaussian growth.  Exponents are least-squares slopes of
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ against $\ln\Sb$ over $25$ equal-count bins (Fig.~\ref{fig:fits}).

\paragraph{Samples.}  We compare with data on lives with at least five growth rates, the cut that matches MSB's
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  The comparison uses seven economies with $N=32000$.  The theory tests use lives with at least $20$ growth rates, long enough to estimate each
life's floor, in two economies with $N=16000$.

\paragraph{Bursts.}  In a few economies one firm runs away to hundreds of times the mean firm and drives the
aggregate.  We exclude one of the eight production economies for this reason (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\paragraph{What is fitted.}  One fact is fitted: we place the reference point at the depth that gives
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$.  The other entries of Table~\ref{tab:facts} are outputs.

\paragraph{Where the facts hold.}  The reference point is one point of a band (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).  Near the
zero-growth line, $\zeta_1$ and the ratio curve depend on one variable: the depth $\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu)$ of the
economy in the fluctuating phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}b).  The deeper the economy, the more its field moves, and
the flatter the decline.  The band that gives $\zeta_1=0.15$--$0.25$ is about $0.07$ wide in $\sigma$.  It holds
economies that compete moderately and grow slowly (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).  Across the band the ratio curve stays below $q$, but $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ stays between about
$3.0$ and $3.5$, above Compustat's $2.90$.  The model reaches $2.90$ only deeper in the fluctuating phase, where
$\zeta_1$ is about $0.13$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}c and Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

\paragraph{What works.}  The decline is monotone, and the ratio curve lies below $q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}a,b).
Growth rates have tent-shaped, exponential tails (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}a and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).
Rescaled by each firm's volatility, they stay fat-tailed among the largest firms, with the excess kurtosis of
Compustat in the largest fifth (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}b--d); granular models predict Gaussian tails there
\cite{moran2024}.

\paragraph{What works in part.}  The higher exponents are too large: $\zeta_4=0.685$ against $0.58(4)$, so the
curve bends less than in Compustat.  The bend also depends on the minimum life length.  With all lives the ratios
lie above $q$, and they fall below $q$ from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}); MSB's results do not
change with the cut.  The rescaled growth rates of small firms have tails that are too thin, and most of their
kurtosis comes from the rescaling of short lives (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}d).  The model's tails are asymmetric, the
negative one fatter (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}b).  Multi-year persistence is confined to the largest firms
(Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}f): small firms are moved by near-Gaussian, mean-reverting birth--death noise, large firms by
rare and persistent dips of their field.

Co-movement rises with size (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}e), but we do not count it as a result.  The growth rates in
these correlations include the growth of the mean firm, and the model's aggregate volatility is several times too
large.  A common shock added to growth whose own volatility falls with size makes both correlations rise with size
by itself.  We have not separated this common mode from the effect of the interactions.

\paragraph{What fails.}  The decline spans two orders of magnitude of size rather than four, and volatility falls
by a factor $2.8$ against $5.5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}a); Section~\ref{sec:range} explains both numbers.  The tail
of the volatility distribution is too thin, and thinner for longer lives (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}c).  With all lives
the model gives $b=4.4$, but a null with iid Laplace growth gives $4.3$, so that match is an artefact of short
lives.  The size distribution has no Pareto tail (Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}a): sizes are capped near the highest
plateaus.  Lives are too short.  Newborns have extreme infant mortality, $38\%$ of the lives with at least two
growth rates have exactly two, and small established firms shrink on average; MSB's firms have $46$ growth rates
on average.  Aggregate volatility is $0.088$ per year, several times too large, and it does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$
over the sizes we simulate (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

\paragraph{The year is a choice.}  At a step of $2$ time units instead of $0.5$, the bend survives, but $\zeta_1$
for lives with at least five growth rates falls to $0.15$.  The serial correlation of growth depends on the step:
at our step it rises with size, as reported for French firms \cite{coad2007}, and at a step of $2$ it vanishes at
every size (Fig.~\ref{fig:limits}b and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\section{Theory of the model}
\label{sec:theory}

This section explains Table~\ref{tab:facts} from Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}.  One firm in a given field reproduces
the life panel and gives a volatility law (Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  The law explains the range and the fall of
the decline (Sec.~\ref{sec:range}).  A general criterion says which dispersion bends the ratio curve
(Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), and the last part finds where that dispersion comes from in the model
(Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).

\subsection{One firm in its field}
\label{sec:onefirm}

Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} describes each firm as one logistic unit driven by its field.  We check that the field
can be taken as given, find which statistics of the field matter, and split the growth variance of a firm into the
part from its field, the floor, and the part from its own noise.


\paragraph{The field can be taken as given.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} assumes that a firm's own noise does
not feed back on its field.  To test this, we drive independent firms by the stored fields of the reference
economies, with fresh noise.  Their life panels agree with the full model to $0.01$ in $\zeta_1$ and $0.06$ in the
ratios, at every minimum life length (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}).  The size--volatility statistics are
therefore a one-firm problem.

\paragraph{Three statistics of the field.}  Three statistics of the field are enough: the mean field of a node,
its temporal amplitude, and one correlation time.  Hubs have a lower mean field and a larger amplitude.  A
Gaussian field with these statistics reproduces the panel of the full model (Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).  The
bend comes from two spreads among firms of equal degree: the spread of the mean field and the spread of the
amplitude.  If we set both to their class values, the ratios go back towards $q$.

HDMFT, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, gives the form of these statistics.  At $C=200$ it gets the temporal amplitude
$20$--$30\%$ low and misses its spread at equal degree, because a few neighbours dominate the field of a firm
(Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).  We therefore take the field statistics from simulation.

\paragraph{The volatility law.}  In logs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} reads
$d\ln S=[\Phi-S-D/(2S)]\,dt+\sqrt{D/S}\,dW$.  Write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$, where $y_F$ solves the noise-free
equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ from the same initial size.  To linear order, $\delta$ is an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck
process that relaxes at rate $S$ and is driven by noise of variance $D/S$.  The variance of a firm's yearly
growth over its life is then
\begin{equation}
\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$, the floor, is the growth variance of $y_F$.  The law reproduces the measured volatility to within
$5\%$ above the lowest decile of size and $9\%$ in it (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}a, Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}).

\paragraph{Two parts.}  The floor is nearly flat with size, because a firm does not follow the fast part of its
field.  The demographic part $B$ falls with size, and the two parts are equal at $\Sb\simeq1.6$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}a).  Equation~\eqref{eq:law} is therefore a two-part law, with a floor $u=V_F$ and
a part $B$ that falls with size; Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} uses this form.  The panel does not show two separate
regimes, and $\zeta_1$ is the effective slope of the whole range.

\subsection{Why the decline spans two orders of magnitude}
\label{sec:range}

The model fails on the range of the decline and on the size of the fall (Table~\ref{tab:facts}).  Both follow
from Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} and the volatility law.

\paragraph{The ends of the range.}  The bins hold equal numbers of lives, so the range is the spread of life-mean
sizes between the smallest and the largest $4\%$ of lives.  The smallest lives are newborns that survive a few
years: in the production run the smallest bin sits at $\Sb=0.032$, that is $0.32D$.  The largest lives sit on high
plateaus, since a firm in a field $\Phi$ relaxes to $S=\Phi$, and the largest bin sits at $\Sb=3.2$.  The range is
therefore
\begin{equation}
R\simeq\log_{10}\frac{\Sb_{\rm top}}{0.3\,D},
\label{eq:range}
\end{equation}
with $\Sb_{\rm top}$ the mean size of the largest lives: two orders of magnitude at $D=0.1$.  Compustat's bins run
from about $10^{-3}$ to $10$ times the mean firm \cite{moran2024}.  The model therefore loses one and a half orders
of magnitude at the bottom, set by the newborn size, and half of one at the top, set by the thin tail of sizes.

\paragraph{The ends of the fall.}  A small firm is far below its plateau, so its self-limitation is negligible and
$dS=\Phi S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW$.  In units of the newborn size, $s=S/D$, this reads $ds=\Phi s\,dt+\sqrt s\,dW$, and $D$
drops out.  Growth rates are differences of logarithms, so the volatility of the smallest lives does not depend on
$D$: it is $1.11$ at the reference point.  At the other end the volatility of the largest lives approaches the
floor: $0.39$, against $\sqrt{V_F}\simeq0.37$ in the largest decile of long lives (Table~\ref{tab:law}).  The
fall is the ratio of the two, $2.8$, and the exponent is close to the logarithm of the fall over the range,
\begin{equation}
\zeta_1R\simeq\log_{10}\frac{v_{\rm newborn}}{\sqrt{V_F}} :
\label{eq:fall}
\end{equation}
$0.42$ against $\log_{10}2.85=0.45$.  The least-squares slope weighs the middle of the range, which is why the two
differ.

\paragraph{What it would take.}  The bottom of the range moves with $D$: a newborn about $30$ times smaller would
reach Compustat's smallest bin.  The top needs a fatter tail of sizes, which is the failure on Zipf's law.  The
fall needs a lower floor: at the model's newborn volatility, Compustat's fall of $5.5$ needs a floor of about $0.2$,
half the model's.  We have not tested whether a smaller $D$ keeps the other facts.

\subsection{When the ratio curve bends}
\label{sec:criterion}

\paragraph{What the exponents measure.}  The exponent $\zeta_1$ says how fast the typical volatility falls with
size.  The higher exponents say how the whole distribution of volatilities at a given size changes with size.
If that distribution only changes its scale, $v=A(\Sb)\,X$ with the law of $X$ the same at every size, then
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$ for every $q$.  High moments weigh the most volatile firms of each size.  Ratios below $q$
therefore mean that, among large firms, some stay much more volatile than the rest.

We make the statement precise in two steps.  The first is an identity that holds for any panel.  The
second assumes that a firm's growth variance has two parts, and finds which dispersion bends the curve
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\paragraph{Dispersion at fixed size.}  Write a firm's volatility as its conditional mean times a rescaled
volatility, $v=\bar v(\Sb)\,X$ with $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$, and let $K_q(\Sb)=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln\bar v+K_q$, and since $K_1=0$,
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
$K_q$ is convex in $q$ and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$.  For $q>1$ it measures the dispersion of $X$, with more
weight on the upper tail as $q$ grows.  The ratio curve lies below $q$ where this dispersion grows with size, on
$q$ where it stays constant, and above $q$ where it shrinks.

For a lognormal $X$,
with $\sigma^2(\Sb)$ the variance of $\ln X$, $K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$ and
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\,\frac{d\sigma^2}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general-lognormal}
\end{equation}
Granular models show both limits \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms keep the law of $X$ fixed, and
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.  Firms made of a few subunits stretch the upper tail of $X$ so fast that $\zeta_q$ stops
depending on $q$ for $q\ge2$, and the ratios fall towards $1$.  A model of the bend has to say why the dispersion grows, and by how much.

In Compustat, $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$ is negative and falls with $q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bend}b, MSB's
uncertainties taken as independent).  A lognormal spread whose variance grows by $0.035$ per unit of $\ln\Sb$
fits it by Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}.  The dispersion at fixed size therefore grows with size, and
the collapse of the rescaled volatilities that MSB report can only be approximate.  The exponents do not
resolve whether the whole distribution spreads or only its upper tail.

\paragraph{A floor and a falling part.}  Suppose, as the volatility law of Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm} suggests,
that a firm's squared volatility is a floor $u_i$ plus a part $B(\Sb)$ common to all firms of the same size, up
to an estimation factor $\epsilon_i$:
\begin{equation}
v_i^2=\big(u_i+B(\Sb_i)\big)\,\epsilon_i^2 .
\label{eq:twopart}
\end{equation}
Let $b=-d\ln B/d\ln\Sb$ be the local slope of the falling part and $w=B/(u+B)$ its share of the variance.
If $\epsilon$ is independent of $u$ and of size, differentiating $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ gives
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\qquad
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\,w\mid\Sb\big]}{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb\big]},
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
where the composition term $c_q$ vanishes when the law of the floor at fixed size does not change with size.
The exponent is the slope of the falling part times its share of the variance, weighted by $v^q$.

\paragraph{The criterion.}  With $Y=u+B$, $\langle w\rangle_q=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$.  Since
$\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is convex in $q$, $\langle w\rangle_q$ does not increase with $q$, and it is constant only if
$Y$ is the same for all firms of equal size.  When $c_q=0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q,
\label{eq:bend}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if the floor is the same for all firms of equal size.  When $c_q\neq0$, a floor
that is the same for all firms of a size still gives $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.  The bound is local.  A least-squares
slope over the bins is an average of the local slopes with positive weights, so $\zeta_q\le q\zeta_1$ also holds
for the fitted exponents when $b>0$.

If $\ln Y$ is Gaussian at fixed size with variance $s^2$,
\begin{gather}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}\simeq q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\nonumber\\
s^2=\Var\big(\ln(u+B)\mid\Sb\big)\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb),
\label{eq:lognormal}
\end{gather}
the last form for a narrow floor.  As the falling part shrinks with size, the floors show through: $w$ falls
and $s^2$ grows.  A spread of floors therefore bends the curve only among the large firms.

\paragraph{Estimation noise.}  The factor $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratios only if its law does not depend on
size.  Its variance falls as $1/n$ with the number of growth rates, and is several times larger for the shortest
lives (Fig.~\ref{fig:bend}c).  Where small firms live shorter, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and, by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}, pushes the ratio curve above $q$.  Short lives can therefore hide a bend or
reverse it (Table~\ref{tab:cases}).  In firm data the ratio curve should be reported with its minimum life
length.

\paragraph{The size of the bend.}  In the model $K_q$ first falls with size, as short lives drop out, and then
rises steeply among the largest firms (Fig.~\ref{fig:bend}a).  The net growth gives $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$ close to
Compustat's, with a fourth moment that grows somewhat less (Fig.~\ref{fig:bend}b).  The rise is weakest for
$q=2$, so the bend sits in the higher moments.  Measuring $K_q(\Sb)$ by size in firm data would compare the
shapes directly.
\subsection{Where the floors differ}
\label{sec:floor}

Every life has a growth variance $V_F+B$.  Here we ask what the floor is made of, why it differs between lives of
equal size, and whether the criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} accounts for the panel.  The lives have at
least $20$ growth rates, in the two reference economies, and the volatility is the growth standard deviation
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\paragraph{The floor is mostly entry and exit.}  A typical life begins as a newborn at $S\simeq D$, climbs to a
plateau, follows the field there, and collapses in a final dip.  Split the noise-free path into the climb, the
core and the collapse:
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
with $V_{\rm core}$ the growth variance on the plateau and $E_{\rm in}$, $E_{\rm out}$ the sums of squared
growth rates over the climb and the collapse.  For a logistic climb from $S_0$ to a plateau $h$,
$E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$.  For a collapse in a field that has fallen to $-\varphi$,
$E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$.  Both grow with the plateau.

The core falls with size.  The entry-and-exit part grows with size, because large firms climb further and
collapse from higher.  Their sum is the nearly flat floor (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\paragraph{Lives end in dips of the field.}  An established firm dies when its field dips below zero; most exits
happen in a dip, at every size.  Inside the dip the firm is small, and it is a Feller branching process.  Its
extinction probability is exact:
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm kill}=\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{D\,J}\Big),\qquad J=\int\frac{dt}{S_{\rm det}(t)},
\label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}
with $S_{\rm det}$ the noise-free path through the dip.  Over $446\,554$ dips, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill} predicts a kill
fraction of $0.725$ against $0.722$ observed, with no fitted parameter.  Successive dips are nearly independent,
so lives are close to memoryless.

\paragraph{Floors differ with life length and degree.}  By Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}, the same climb and collapse are
spread over lives of very different lengths.  The lengths are broad because lives end at memoryless dips.  At
fixed size, the spread of life lengths and the spread of floors both grow with size (Table~\ref{tab:fifths}).
Among the larger firms, the life length explains more of the spread of floors than the degree does.

The degree acts through both parts of the floor.  A better connected firm feels a field of larger amplitude,
which keeps its core up.  It also meets more killing dips, which shorten its life and raise its entry-and-exit
part.

\paragraph{The criterion on the panel.}  With size, the noise share $\langle w\rangle_1$ falls and the dispersion
$s^2$ grows (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}b).  Within size bins, $\langle w\rangle_4/\langle w\rangle_1$ follows the
lognormal estimate of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lognormal} (Table~\ref{tab:fifths}).  In the largest fifth the composition
term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:identity} also acts, because the largest lives have the largest degrees.

To isolate the floor, we keep each life's own floor and replace $B$ by its bin mean.  The ratios are then
$1.85$, $2.55$, $3.07$, against $1.75$, $2.30$, $2.70$ measured (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}c).  The floor alone
carries about two thirds of the bend.  The rest comes from the life's own demographic part, which is larger where
the floor is larger, because low excursions raise both (Table~\ref{tab:perlife}).  The opposite test keeps each
life's own $B$ and the bin mean of the floor.  Its ratios lie above $q$ (Table~\ref{tab:mslaw}): without the
spread of the floor, only estimation noise is left.  Most of the bend comes from the climb and the collapse,
through the spread of life lengths (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\paragraph{Three panels.}  Table~\ref{tab:cases} sets three panels of the model against the criterion.  On a random
regular graph every firm has degree $C$.  Its floor has the same make-up, but it is small and falls with size.  Even
the largest lives stay dominated by their own noise, $s^2$ stays small, and the ratio curve is closer to $q$.  The

When all lives are kept, the shortest lives are mostly small firms, and the estimation variance falls steeply
with size.  The ratios then lie above $q$.  Dropping the shortest lives restores the bend.  One criterion
accounts for a bend, its absence and its reversal: what differs is how the dispersion at fixed size changes with
size.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We have proposed an economy of interacting firms and scored it on the size--volatility observables of MSB.  With
one fitted exponent, it gives a ratio curve below $q$, tent-shaped growth rates, and rescaled growth that stays
fat-tailed among the largest firms.  Its higher exponents are too large, and its bend needs lives of at least five
growth rates.  It fails on the range of the decline, the size of the fall, the tail of the volatility distribution, the
size distribution, the length of lives and the aggregate volatility.  Co-movement rises with size, but we have not
separated the interactions from the common mode of an aggregate shock that is too large.

One firm in a moving field explains both sides.  The self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also
thins the tail of sizes: the decline runs from the newborn size to the top of that tail, and volatility falls from the noise of a
newborn, which does not depend on $D$, to a floor that the firm cannot average away.  The ratio curve bends below
$q$ exactly when the dispersion of volatilities at fixed size grows with size.  In the model this dispersion comes
from floors that differ between firms of equal size.  A floor contains the climb after entry and the collapse
before exit, spread over a life whose length is broad because lives end at memoryless dips.  A better connected
firm also feels a field of larger amplitude.  Short lives work the other way, through the noise of estimating a
volatility.

Granular models sit at the two ends of the criterion.  Well-diversified firms keep the law of the rescaled
volatility fixed, on the boundary.  Firms made of a few subunits stretch its upper tail so much that the ratios
fall towards $1$ \cite{moran2024}.  The corrections that MSB invoke would act through the same growth of $K_q$, but
they give the length of a life no role beyond estimation noise.  Appendix~\ref{app:tests} turns our mechanism into
tests on firm data: the dispersion at fixed size should grow with size, life length and exposure to other firms
should explain a growing share of it, and trimming the years after entry and before exit should move the ratio
curve towards $q$.

Anomalous moment scaling has been derived for GLV communities before.  With multiplicative environmental noise
and migration, abundances obey Taylor's law with one exponent for every order \cite{alhiyasat2026}, through the
Bouchaud--M\'ezard wealth model \cite{bouchaudmezard2000}.  That law concerns the moments of an abundance.  Ours
concerns the moments of the growth volatility at a given size; its exponents bend below $q\zeta_1$, and it
depends on the network and on turnover.

Three problems are open.  First, at finite connectivity a Gaussian closure predicts neither the amplitude of the
field's motion nor its spread between firms of equal degree, which carries part of the bend.  A closure that keeps
this spread, such as the cavity method solved by population dynamics \cite{tarabolo2025,tonolo2025}, is the
natural next step.  Second, the model needs a dynamics
that decouples the decline of volatility from the size cap, both for the range and for Zipf's law.  Third, a theory
of co-movement in a finite economy would separate the common mode from the effect of the interactions.

\section{Relative sizes and the one-firm equation}
\label{app:relative}

This appendix goes from the model, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, to the equation of one firm in its field,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}, and to the standard model it maps onto.  Throughout, $S_i=x_i/m$ is the relative size, so that
$\sum_iS_i=N$; $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is an average over degree classes or nodes, $\E$ an average over the disorder or the
noise, and an overbar a time or life average; $\phi$ and $\PhiN$ are the standard normal density and distribution
function, and $\phi_0=\phi(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

\subsection{The mean firm}

Write $F_i=1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j$, so that the drift of $x_i$ is $x_iF_i$ (because $x_j/m=S_j$).  Summing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} over $i$ and dividing by $N$,
\begin{equation}
\dd m=\frac1N\sum_jmS_jF_j\,\dd t+\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{D\,m\cdot mS_j}\,\dd W_j
=m\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],
\label{eq:app-dm}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\fb_0=\frac1N\sum_jS_jF_j,\qquad \dd Z=\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{DS_j}\,\dd W_j,\qquad
(\dd Z)^2=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_jDS_j\,\dd t=\frac DN\,\dd t ,
\label{eq:app-Z}
\end{equation}
where the last equality uses $\sum_jS_j=N$.  The common noise $Z$ has quadratic variation of order
$1/N$.

\subsection{It\^o's rule for the quotient}

For $S_i=x_i/m$ It\^o's rule for $f(x,m)=x/m$ gives
\begin{equation}
\dd S_i=\frac{\dd x_i}{m}-\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}+\frac{x_i\,(\dd m)^2}{m^3}-\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}.
\end{equation}
Term by term:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x_i}{m}&=S_iF_i\,\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i,\\
\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=S_i\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],\\
\frac{x_i(\dd m)^2}{m^3}&=S_i\,(\dd Z)^2=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t,\\
\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i\cdot\frac1N\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t ,
\end{align}
where the last line uses $\dd W_i\dd W_j=\delta_{ij}\dd t$.  The two It\^o corrections cancel exactly, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[F_i-\fb_0\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-S_i\,\dd Z .}
\label{eq:app-rel}
\end{equation}
This is exact between entries.

\paragraph{Conservation.}  Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel},
$\sum_i\dd S_i=\big(\sum_iS_iF_i-N\fb_0\big)\dd t+\sum_i\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-N\,\dd Z=0$
by the definitions of $\fb_0$ and $Z$.  So $\sum_iS_i=N$ is preserved between entries.

\paragraph{Size of the common-noise term.}  $S_i\,\dd Z$ has variance $S_i^2D\,\dd t/N$, against $DS_i\,\dd t$
for the own noise: relative size $S_i/N$.  It is of order $N^{-1/2}$ in amplitude and is dropped in the
one-firm equation.  It is the only place where the finite size of the economy enters the single-firm
dynamics directly.

\subsection{Entry dilution}

An exit at $S_i=0$ removes nothing from $m$.  The newborn adds $Dm$ to $\sum_jx_j$, so
$m\to m'=m(1+D/N)$, every incumbent's relative size is multiplied by $1/(1+D/N)$, and the newborn's
relative size is $D/(1+D/N)=D+O(1/N)$.  If entries happen at a rate $N\nu$ per unit time ($\nu$ per
node), then over $\dd t$
\begin{equation}
\frac{m(t+\dd t)}{m(t)}=(1+D/N)^{N\nu\,\dd t}\big[1+\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big]
\;\Rightarrow\; \fb=\fb_0+D\nu ,
\end{equation}
and each incumbent picks up the drift $-D\nu S_i$.  So the growth rate of the mean firm is
\begin{equation}
\fb(t)=\frac1N\sum_iS_iF_i+D\nu(t),
\label{eq:app-fbar}
\end{equation}
and in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel} $\fb_0$ is replaced by $\fb$.  Here $\nu(t)$ is the empirical entry rate, which fluctuates at finite $N$.  (At the reference point $D\nu\simeq0.06$.)

\subsection{The field and the one-firm equation}

Collect everything except the firm's own self-limitation into the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=F_i+S_i-\fb=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\fb(t).
\label{eq:app-field}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i+O(N^{-1/2}),}
\label{eq:app-onefirm}
\end{equation}
with exit at $S_i=0$ and entry at $S_i=D$.  Up to the common-noise term this is exact as a rewriting; the content of the
``one firm is enough'' result is that $\Phi_i$ may be treated as an external input, i.e.\ that firm $i$'s own
noise does not feed back on $\Phi_i$ through its neighbours.  The feedback is of order
$J_{ij}J_{ji}\sim A_{ij}(\mu^2/C^2+\sigma^2\E[z_{ij}z_{ji}]/C)$, so at zero reciprocity its mean is $\mu^2/C^2$ per
neighbour and $O(1/C)$ in total after summing over $k\sim C$ neighbours.  The products $z_{ij}z_{ji}$ have zero mean but do
not vanish, so the total differs from node to node by $O(C^{-1/2})$.  The approximation is controlled in $C^{-1/2}$ and is tested in Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}.

\paragraph{Plateau.}  At constant $\Phi>0$ the noise-free equation $\dot S=S(\Phi-S)$ has the stable fixed
point $S=\Phi$ ($\partial_S[S(\Phi-S)]=\Phi-2S=-\Phi<0$ there) and relaxes to it at rate $\Phi$.  For
$\Phi<0$, $S=0$ is the only non-negative fixed point and is attracting.  In absolute units the plateau is
$x_i=m\Phi$.

\subsection{The standard model}

In the stationary state of a large economy $\fb$ is constant.  Write $a=1-\fb$ and assume $a>0$.  The drift
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} is $S_i[a-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j]$.  With $S_i=a\,y_i$ and $\tau=a\,t$, so that
$\dd W_i(t)=a^{-1/2}\dd W_i(\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
\dd y_i=y_i\Big[1-y_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}y_j\Big]\dd\tau+\sqrt{\frac{D}{a^2}\,y_i}\;\dd W_i(\tau) .
\end{equation}
This is the standard GLV model with unit growth rate and unit self-limitation, on the same graph and with
the same couplings, with demographic noise of strength $D/a^2$; a firm that reaches zero restarts at
$y=D/a$.  The constraint $\sum_iS_i=N$ fixes $a=1/\langle y\rangle$: the relative sizes are the abundances
of the standard model divided by their mean, $S_i=y_i/\langle y\rangle$.  On the zero-growth line $a=1$
and the two models coincide.

\subsection{Logarithmic form and ``size is mean field''}

By It\^o, $\dd\ln S=\dd S/S-\tfrac12(\dd S)^2/S^2$ with $(\dd S)^2=DS\,\dd t$:
\begin{equation}
\dd\ln S_i=\Big[\Phi_i-S_i-\frac{D}{2S_i}\Big]\dd t+\sqrt{\frac{D}{S_i}}\,\dd W_i .
\label{eq:app-log}
\end{equation}
Integrate over a window $[t_1,t_2]$ of length $T$ and divide by $T$:
\begin{equation}
\Sb_i=\bar\Phi_i-\frac D2\,\overline{S_i^{-1}}+\frac1T\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{D/S_i}\,\dd W_i-\frac1T\ln\frac{S_i(t_2)}{S_i(t_1)} .
\end{equation}
Without noise, and for a window that ends at the size at which it starts, $\Sb_i=\bar\Phi_i$ exactly: a firm's
mean size is its mean field.  With noise the stochastic integral has zero mean for a window fixed in advance,
but not for a window chosen by the path (a life selected for its length).  A life also ends below the size at
which it starts, and $\int\dd t/S_i$ diverges at an exit, so on a life $\overline{S_i^{-1}}$ is taken over the
samples and the last ones before the exit dominate it.  The identity therefore holds per life only to about 5\%.

\section{Heterogeneous mean-field theory}
\label{app:hdmft}

This appendix summarises the mean-field description of the network, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, in the
notation we use.  It follows Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026}, where the static solution without
noise and its stability are derived; Appendix~\ref{app:constant} adds the birth--death noise and the turnover.

\subsection{Degree classes and kernels}

Group the nodes into classes $p$ with population fractions $\rho_p$ and degrees $k_p$.  Let $P_{pr}$ be the
probability that a given node of class $p$ is linked to a given node of class $r$.  The expected number
of class-$r$ neighbours of a class-$p$ node is $N\rho_rP_{pr}$, and the degree constraint is
$\sum_rN\rho_rP_{pr}=k_p$.  Define
\begin{equation}
K_{pr}=\frac{N\rho_rP_{pr}}{C}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sum_rK_{pr}=\kappa_p .
\end{equation}
\emph{Uncorrelated (bilinear) kernel:} $P_{pr}=k_pk_r/(NC)$, so $K_{pr}=\rho_r\kappa_p\kappa_r$; it satisfies
the constraint because $\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r=1$.  It is a probability only if $k_pk_r\le NC$; at $\alpha=2.5$
the largest degrees violate this (structural cutoff $k\sim\sqrt{NC}$).  \emph{Soft configuration} \cite{patil2026}:
$P_{pr}=h_ph_r/(h_ph_r+C/N)$, always in $[0,1]$, with hidden degrees $h_p$ chosen so that the constraint
holds; for $h_ph_r\ll C/N$ it reduces to the bilinear form with $h_p\propto k_p$.

\subsection{The mean part of the interaction field}

The interaction term on node $i$ in class $p$ is
$\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j=\frac\mu C\sum_jA_{ij}S_j+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt C}\sum_jA_{ij}z_{ij}S_j$.
The first sum has $k_i\sim C$ terms; by the law of large numbers over neighbours drawn with the kernel,
\begin{equation}
\frac\mu C\sum_jA_{ij}S_j\;\to\;\frac\mu C\sum_rN\rho_rP_{pr}\,\E[S_r]=\mu\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r]\equiv\mu B_p ,
\end{equation}
with fluctuations of relative order $k^{-1/2}$, i.e.\ an absolute correction $O(C^{-1/2})$.

\subsection{The random part: cavity argument}

Remove node $0$ (the cavity) and let the rest of the system evolve; the neighbours' sizes $S_j^{\setminus0}(t)$
are then independent of the couplings $z_{0j}$.  Reinserting node $0$ has two effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Direct field.}  $\eta_0(t)=C^{-1/2}\sum_jA_{0j}z_{0j}S_j^{\setminus0}(t)$ is a sum of $k_0$
  independent zero-mean terms conditional on the cavity trajectories, so by the central limit theorem it is a
  Gaussian process with covariance
  \begin{equation}
  \E[\eta_0(t)\eta_0(t')]=\frac1C\sum_jA_{0j}S_j(t)S_j(t')\;\to\;\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')]\equiv q_p(t,t') .
  \end{equation}
\item \emph{Reaction.}  Node $0$ perturbs each neighbour $j$ by $J_{j0}S_0$; the response of $j$ is
  $\chi_j J_{j0}S_0$ and feeds back on $0$ through $J_{0j}$.  The total is
  $\sum_jJ_{0j}J_{j0}\chi_jS_0$, whose mean is $\sum_jA_{0j}(\mu^2/C^2+\sigma^2\E[z_{0j}z_{j0}]/C)\chi_j=O(\mu^2/C)+0$
  at zero reciprocity, and whose spread between nodes is $O(\sigma^2C^{-1/2})$.  It vanishes for $C\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
So for $C\to\infty$ the field on a class-$p$ node is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_p(t)=1-\fb(t)+\mu B_p(t)+\sigma\eta_p(t),\qquad \E[\eta_p(t)\eta_p(t')]=q_p(t,t'),
\label{eq:app-effproc}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B_p(t)=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)],\qquad q_p(t,t')=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\label{eq:app-closure}
\end{equation}
Each class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} with this field; Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} closes the problem, and
$\fb(t)$ is fixed by $\sum_p\rho_p\E[S_p(t)]=1$ (which is $\sum_iS_i=N$, not an extra approximation).
This is the standard dynamical cavity derivation
\cite{bunin2017,galla2018,aguirrelopez} with degree classes.

\subsection{Bilinear kernel: the field law}

With $K_{pr}=\rho_r\kappa_p\kappa_r$, both closures factorise:
\begin{equation}
B_p=\kappa_p\bar B,\quad \bar B=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r];\qquad q_p=\kappa_pQ,\quad Q(t,t')=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\end{equation}
$\bar B$ and $Q$ are degree-weighted averages (a random neighbour is drawn in proportion to its degree).
Writing $\eta_p=\sqrt{\kappa_p}\,\xi$ with $\E[\xi(t)\xi(t')]=Q(t,t')$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Phi_\kappa(t)=1-\fb(t)+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t).}
\label{eq:app-fieldlaw}
\end{equation}
Under competition ($\mu<0$, $\bar B>0$) the field's mean falls linearly in $\kappa$; its amplitude grows as
$\sqrt\kappa$.  (On the reference graph the bilinear kernel is not a probability for the hubs,
so the factorisation is approximate there; the measured amplitude exponent is $0.54$ against $0.5$.)

\section{One firm with birth--death noise at constant field, and the instability line with noise}
\label{app:constant}

With noise a firm in a constant field no longer sits at a fixed size: it fluctuates around its plateau, or fails
and is replaced.  This appendix solves that problem exactly, uses the solution in place of the clipped Gaussian
of the noise-free theory \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} to obtain the phase lines with noise, and tests the instability line in simulation.
Local stationary laws with demographic noise have been derived on sparse graphs with immigration, which keeps
every species present \cite{machado2025}; here zero is absorbing, and a newborn replaces the firm that exits.

\subsection{Scale function, speed measure and boundaries}

At constant $\Phi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} is the one-dimensional diffusion
$\dd S=b(S)\,\dd t+\sqrt{a(S)}\,\dd W$ with $b(y)=y(\Phi-y)$ and $a(y)=Dy$, generator
$\mathcal L=\tfrac12Dy\,\partial_y^2+y(\Phi-y)\partial_y$.  The scale density and speed density
\cite{karlintaylor1981} (in this appendix $m(y)$ is the speed density, not the mean firm) are
\begin{equation}
s'(y)=\exp\Big[-\int_0^y\frac{2b(u)}{a(u)}\dd u\Big]=\exp\Big[-\frac2D\Big(\Phi y-\frac{y^2}2\Big)\Big],\qquad
m(y)=\frac{2}{a(y)s'(y)}=\frac{2}{Dy\,s'(y)},
\end{equation}
since $2b/a=2(\Phi-u)/D$.  Let $s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,\dd u$.  Properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item Near $0$, $s(y)\simeq y$ and $m(y)\simeq2/(Dy)$; $\int_0s(y)m(y)\,\dd y<\infty$, so $0$ is reached in finite time
  (an exit boundary in Feller's classification).
\item $s'(y)\sim e^{y^2/D}$ for large $y$, so $s(\infty)=\infty$: $\infty$ is never reached, and the process started anywhere
  hits $0$ with probability one.  Every life ends, so at a node with constant field the lives form a proper
  renewal sequence.
\item $\int^\infty m(z)\,\dd z<\infty$ (Gaussian decay).
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Mean exit time}

$T(x)$ solves $\mathcal LT=-1$ with $T(0)=0$ and no flux at infinity.  Since $s''/s'=-2b/a$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd y}\Big(\frac{T'}{s'}\Big)=\frac{T''+(2b/a)T'}{s'}=\frac{2\,\mathcal LT}{a\,s'}=-\frac{2}{a\,s'}=-m(y).
\end{equation}
Integrating from $y$ to $\infty$ with $T'/s'\to0$ at infinity, $T'(y)=s'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z$, and
\begin{equation}
T(x)=\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z\,\dd y=2\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty\frac{\dd z}{Dz\,s'(z)}\,\dd y .
\label{eq:app-T}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Green's function and the renewal law}

Exchanging the order of integration in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} (Fubini; all integrands positive),
\begin{equation}
T(x_0)=\int_0^\infty m(z)\int_0^{\min(x_0,z)}s'(y)\,\dd y\,\dd z=\int_0^\infty\underbrace{s(\min(x_0,z))\,m(z)}_{G(x_0,z)}\dd z .
\end{equation}
$G(x_0,z)$ is the Green's function: the expected time spent near $z$, per unit $z$, before absorption, starting at $x_0$.

\paragraph{Renewal--reward.}  With replacement at $D$, the successive lives at a node are i.i.d.\ copies of the
process started at $D$.  For any set $\mathcal A$, the long-run fraction of time spent in $\mathcal A$ equals
$\E[\text{time in }\mathcal A\text{ during one life}]/\E[\text{life length}]$.  So the stationary density of sizes at a node with constant field $\Phi=h$ is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{p(x\mid h)=\frac{G(D,x)}{T(D)}=\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)\,T(D)},\qquad T(D)=\int_0^\infty G(D,x)\,\dd x,}
\label{eq:renewal}
\end{equation}
and by the elementary renewal theorem the turnover rate is $1/T(D)$ per node per unit time.

\paragraph{Shape.}  For $x>D$, $s(\min(x,D))=s(D)$ is constant and
$1/(xs'(x))=x^{-1}\exp[(2/D)(hx-x^2/2)]=x^{-1}e^{h^2/D}e^{-(x-h)^2/D}$: a Gaussian factor of variance $D/2$ around
$h$ times $x^{-1}$.  For $x<D$, $x\ll D/|h|$ and $x\ll\sqrt D$, $s(x)\simeq x$ and $s'(x)\simeq1$, so $p\simeq2/(DT(D))$: flat.


\subsection{Two limits of the exit time}

Two limits are used below.  For a negative field, $-\Phi\gg\sqrt D$, the rescaling $y=D\tilde y$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} and the limit $D\to0$ give the exit time of a newborn as the Feller value
$T(D)\to|\Phi|^{-1}\int_0^{2|\Phi|}e^tE_1(t)\,\dd t$, with $E_1$ the exponential integral: it does not depend
on $D$.  For a firm on its plateau, $\Phi>0$, Laplace's method on the peak of $m(z)$ at $z\simeq\Phi$ gives
$T\simeq\sqrt{\pi D}\,\Phi^{-2}e^{\Phi^2/D}$ to exponential order, for $\Phi^2\gg D$.  At $D=0.1$ the
quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} from the plateau gives $13\,900$ time units at $\Phi=1$ ($12\,300$ from
the Laplace form), $200$ at $\Phi=0.7$, $38$ at $\Phi=0.5$ and $13$ at $\Phi=0.3$
(\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).  A firm on a plateau well above $\sqrt D$ therefore exits
through dips of its field rather than through its own noise.  On a plateau of $0.5$ or less its own noise
would end it within $76$ growth rates or fewer, a time comparable with the lives we measure; even there
most exits happen in dips (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\subsection{The static closure with noise}

When the field is static, each node follows the renewal law \eqref{eq:renewal} at its own $h=a+\mu B_p+\sigma\sqrt{q_p}\,z$, with $a=1-f^*$, $f^*$ the stationary growth rate of the mean firm, and $z$ a frozen standard Gaussian.
Let $\langle S\rangle_h=\int xp(x\mid h)\,\dd x$.  The static closure is Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} with time averages:
\begin{equation}
B_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r},\qquad q_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r}^2,\qquad \sum_p\rho_p\E\langle S\rangle_{h_p}=1,
\end{equation}
with $\E$ the Gaussian average over $z$.  Using $\langle S\rangle^2_h$ in $q_p$ counts only the static part of the neighbours'
sizes in the field covariance, i.e.\ the temporal fluctuations of $S$ produced by the noise are taken as not
feeding the field.  (Using $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ instead, ``as if quenched'', moves the lines by less than $0.015$.)

\subsection{The susceptibility and the instability line}
\label{app:susceptibility}

The response argument of Refs.~\cite{opperdiederich1992,bunin2017,aguirrelopez,patil2026} carries over with one change.  Shift the growth rate of every
node by a small static amount $\delta h_i$, independent between nodes, of zero mean and variance $\varsigma^2$.  The
time-averaged size of a node responds to the shift of its own field and to the shifts of its neighbours' mean
sizes,
\begin{equation}
\delta\langle S_i\rangle=\chi_i\Big(\delta h_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}\,\delta\langle S_j\rangle\Big),\qquad
\chi=\frac{\partial\langle S\rangle_h}{\partial h},
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i$ is evaluated at the node's own field.  By the cavity argument the sum is a Gaussian of variance
$\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r$ for a node of class $p$, independent of $\chi_i$, with $Y_r=\E[\delta\langle S\rangle_r^2]$.
Squaring and averaging,
\begin{equation}
Y_p=\E[\chi_p^2]\Big(\varsigma^2+\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r\Big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Y=\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2\,\varsigma^2 .
\end{equation}
The mean squared response per unit variance of the shift is the spin-glass susceptibility,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\chi_{\rm SG}=\frac1{\varsigma^2}\sum_p\rho_pY_p=\rho^{\sf T}\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2 .}
\label{eq:chisg}
\end{equation}
It is finite while the Perron root $\Lambda$ of $\sigma^2{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K$ is below one and diverges as
$\Lambda\to1$, which defines $\sigma_c(\mu,D)$.  Without noise $\langle S\rangle_h=[h]_+$, $\chi=\theta(h)$, $\chi^2=\chi$ and
$\E\chi^2=\Pr(h>0)$, the fraction of surviving firms: the deterministic criterion of Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} is recovered.  With noise $\chi$ is a smooth function of $h$ in place of a step, and the line moves up, almost linearly in $D$ (below).

\paragraph{Numerical solution.}  We tabulate $\langle S\rangle_h$, $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ and $\ln T(D;h)$ once per $D$ on a grid of fields,
by quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:renewal} in logarithms, with a logarithmic grid below $D$, and obtain $\chi$ by
differentiation.  The closure is iterated with damping on $(B_p,q_p)$, the normalisation being solved for $a$ at
every step, with $60$ degree classes of equal population built from the realised degree sequence and the
soft-configuration kernel.  The instability line is the root in $\sigma$ of $\Lambda=1$, and the zero-growth line the
root of $a=1$.

\paragraph{Turnover.}  $\sum_p\rho_p\E[1/T(D;h_p)]$ per node per unit time.  Nodes with $h<0$ dominate it, and by
the first limit above their newborns' lives are nearly $D$-independent: turnover is nearly independent of the noise.

\subsection{The phase lines}

Noise raises the instability line almost linearly with $D$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaselines}a): from $1.460$ to $1.577$ at
$\alpha=2.5$ and from $1.399$ to $1.551$ at $\alpha=6$ between $D=0.003$ and $0.3$, at $\mu=-1.76$; at $D=0.003$ it agrees
with the deterministic line to $0.002$ ($1.399$ against $1.400$ at $\alpha=6$, $1.413$ against $\sqrt2$ on the complete
graph).  Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows the phase diagram in the plane of the mean interaction and the
disorder.  On the reference graph competition raises $\sigma_c$ strongly, from $0.83$ at $\mu=0$ to $1.68$ at
$\mu=-2.5$, because its degrees are the most spread, and with them the mean field across classes; on the
complete graph the line does not depend on $\mu$, and at $\alpha=6$ it barely does.  Under strong
competition the zero-growth line is almost the same on the three graphs, within $0.05$ for $\mu\le-1.25$;
under weak competition it is lower on the reference graph ($0.50$ against $0.63$ and $0.64$ at $\mu=0$).  The
reference point, $\sigma=1.70$ against $\sigma_c(0.1)=1.497$, lies in the fluctuating phase, close to
the static estimate of the zero-growth line ($1.72$ at $D=0.1$).  The closure treats each node's field as
frozen, so above the instability line it is a continuation and not a controlled solution.  In
simulation the zero-growth line nevertheless lies within $0.04$ of the static estimate for $\mu$ from $-0.5$ to $-2.5$, and
the region where the facts of Sec.~\ref{sec:data} hold is a band along it (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).

\paragraph{Turnover and the motion of the field.}  The same theory predicts the turnover rate to $1\%$ below
$\sigma_c$ and to about $2\%$ above it (Table~\ref{tab:phase}, $\alpha=6$).  The share of the field variance that moves in
time grows from $0.025$ at $\sigma=1.2$ to $0.138$ at $\sigma=1.7$; the static closure underestimates it below $\sigma_c$, by a
factor of about $2$ at $\sigma=1.2$ and $5$ at $\sigma_c$, because it omits the collective amplification of slow field
fluctuations (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaselines}b).  The two-time closure includes it, but at $C=200$ only in part
(Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).

\subsection{The instability line in simulation}
\label{app:linetest}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:chisg} can be measured directly.  For each point we run three economies with
the same couplings and the same realisation of the noise ($D=0.1$, total time $700$, the first $200$ discarded,
$N=4000$, $C=100$): a base run; a run in which the growth rate of every firm is shifted by $\varsigma\eta_i$, with
$\eta_i$ independent standard Gaussians and $\varsigma=0.01$; and a run from other initial sizes.  The mean squared
difference between the time-averaged sizes of the first two, divided by $\varsigma^2$, is $\chi_{\rm SG}$; using the
same noise removes most of the sampling error.  The third run tells whether the composition is slaved to the
noise.  Below the line two economies with the same noise forget their initial sizes and follow each other;
above it the composition has a motion of its own and they separate.  We measure their distance
$d=\overline{(S_i^A-S_i^B)^2}/(2\Var S)$ over the last $100$ samples, which is $1$ for two economies with nothing in common; the frozen part that the same couplings give to both keeps it below $1$.  For
each graph we take nine values of $\sigma$ around the predicted line: $\alpha=2.5$ at $\mu=0$, $-0.5$, $-1$, $-1.76$ and

\section{Two-time closure}
\label{app:closure}

\paragraph{Field statistics at the reference point.}  A node's mean field falls with its degree, from $0.5$ at
$\kappa\simeq0.4$ to $-2.3$ at $\kappa\simeq2$.  Among nodes of equal degree the mean field has a spread of
$2.66\sqrt\kappa$ in standard deviation, close to Gaussian (excess kurtosis $0.3$).  Around its mean the field
moves with amplitude $2.15\sqrt\kappa$ (as $\kappa^{0.54}$ across degree classes); this amplitude differs between
nodes of equal degree by about $30\%$, and the field decorrelates over about $4$ time units.  Driving each firm
by a Gaussian field with its node's time mean, its temporal amplitude and one correlation function gives
$\zeta_1=0.23$ and ratios $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model.
Setting the amplitudes to their class value raises $\zeta_1$ and weakens the bend
(\todo{$0.28$; $1.84$, $2.51$, $3.03$}); setting the means to their class value as well gives ratios $1.93$,
$2.79$, $3.56$.  A Gaussian spread of the means with the same variance gives the same panel
(\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The closure.}
Each degree class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with a Gaussian field closed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure}; at
zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.  We solve the closure in the stationary state at the reference
point by sampling the effective process \cite{eissfeller1992,roy2019} (\texttt{scripts/two\_time\_dmft.py}),
with $31$ degree classes.  Evaluated on the measured sizes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} gives the temporal
and frozen parts of the measured field to within $4\%$ and $6\%$.  Solved self-consistently, it gets the
mean field, but its temporal amplitude is $0.71$--$0.81$ of the measured one in every class, and its life
panel has $\zeta_1=0.34$ and ratios $1.85$, $2.53$, $3.07$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$.

The cause is the finite connectivity.  At $C=200$ a few strongly fluctuating neighbours dominate the
temporal field of a node, so its amplitude varies between nodes of equal degree, with a coefficient of
variation of $0.24$--$0.36$, and the closure replaces this spread by its mean.  Started from the measured
state of one economy, one step of the iteration returns $0.955\pm0.01$ of the temporal field variance.  A
loss of $4.5\%$ per step that halves the self-consistent variance means a loop gain of about $0.9$: the
temporal field is set close to marginal amplification.  With the temporal field taken from simulation, an
amplitude $2.15\sqrt\kappa$ and an exponential correlation of $1/e$ time $4.5$, the panel has $\zeta_1=0.26$
and ratios $1.85$, $2.54$, $3.08$; the rest of the gap is the spread of amplitudes at equal degree
(Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  Closures that keep this spread, such as the dynamic cavity method solved by
population dynamics \cite{tarabolo2025,tonolo2025}, are the natural next step.

\subsection{Derivation of the volatility law}

Start from Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-log}.  Let $y_F$ solve the noise-free equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ along the same field and from
the same initial size, and write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$.  Subtracting,
\begin{equation}
\dd\delta=-e^{y_F}\big(e^{\delta}-1\big)\dd t-\frac{D}{2S}\,\dd t+\sqrt{\frac DS}\,\dd W .
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Linearise in $\delta$: $-e^{y_F}(e^\delta-1)\simeq-S_F\delta$ with $S_F=e^{y_F}\simeq S$.
\item Freeze the coefficients over the relaxation time $1/S$ (adiabatic): $\delta$ is an OU process with rate $k=S$ and noise
  intensity $D/S$, stationary variance $v_\delta=(D/S)/(2S)=D/(2S^2)$.
\item The It\^o drift $-D/2S$ shifts the mean of $\delta$, not its fluctuations, to this order.
\end{itemize}
The increment over $\Delta t$ has variance $2v_\delta(1-e^{-k\Delta t})$:
\begin{equation}
\Var(\Delta\delta)=\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big).
\end{equation}
The growth rate is $g=\Delta y_F+\Delta\delta$.  Taking the two parts as uncorrelated (the noise $W$ is independent of the field,
and $y_F$ is a functional of the field only) and averaging the local variance over the life:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac D{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$ is the growth variance of $y_F$ over the life.  Limits: $S\Delta t\ll1$ gives $B\to D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle$; $S\Delta t\gg1$ gives
$B\to D\langle S^{-2}\rangle$.

\paragraph{Conditions.}  (1) $\delta$ small: $v_\delta=D/(2S^2)\ll1$, i.e.\ $S\gg\sqrt{D/2}\simeq0.22$ at $D=0.1$ (on the plateau, a simulation of one node at constant field gives a variance error of about $2D/S^2$, i.e.\ $20\%$ at $S=1$; along a life the relevant $S$ is the instantaneous one, and
the error is partly averaged out over a life that also visits larger sizes).  (2) $S$ varies slowly on the time $1/S$.  (3) The cross term $2\Cov(\Delta y_F,\Delta\delta)$ is small: $\delta$
relaxes towards $y_F$, so the deviation and the path are not exactly independent once the linearisation fails.  All three are
worst for small firms, consistent with the 9\% error in the lowest decile ($\Sb=0.25$) against 5\% elsewhere.

\paragraph{Why the filter matters.}  If a firm followed its field exactly it would sit on $S=\Phi$, with $\Var g=\Var\Delta\ln\Phi$ plus unfiltered noise
$D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle$.  The noise part changes little (at $\Delta t=0.5$, $\sqrt{D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle}$ is within 6\% of $\sqrt B$),
but $\Var\Delta\ln\Phi$ is much larger than $V_F$ for small firms: $\ln\Phi$ moves violently when $\Phi$ is small, while $y_F$
low-pass filters the field at rate $S$.  (Numbers: $\sqrt{\Var\Delta\ln\Phi}$ from $0.92$ to $0.44$, $\sqrt{V_F}$ flat at $0.32$--$0.38$.)

\section{The criterion, the floor and the length of a life}
\label{app:multiscaling}

This appendix derives the results of Secs.~\ref{sec:criterion} and \ref{sec:floor}: the general relation between the
ratio curve and the dispersion at fixed size, the identity for a growth variance made of a floor and a falling
part, the conditions under which the curve bends, the make-up of the floor, and the law of life lengths.

\subsection{The general criterion}
\label{app:cumulant}

Write $v=\bar v(\Sb)\,X$ with $\bar v(\Sb)=\E[v\mid\Sb]$, so that $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln\bar v(\Sb)+K_q(\Sb)$ with $K_q=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$, and $K_1=0$.  Differentiating in $\ln\Sb$, the
case $q=1$ gives $\zeta_1=-d\ln\bar v/d\ln\Sb$, and the general case
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb},
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:general}.  No assumption has been made.  $K_q$ is the cumulant generating function of $\ln X$
at fixed size, $K_q=\ln\E[e^{q\ln X}]=\sum_{n\ge1}\gamma_nq^n/n!$ with $\gamma_n(\Sb)$ the cumulants of $\ln X$.  It is convex in
$q$ (H\"older's inequality) and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so it is negative between them and positive for
$q>1$.  Since $K_1=\sum_n\gamma_n/n!=0$, subtracting $q$ times this from $K_q$ removes the mean $\gamma_1$:
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q-q\zeta_1=-\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{q^n-q}{n!}\,\frac{d\gamma_n}{d\ln\Sb}
=-\frac{q(q-1)}2\,\frac{d\Var(\ln X\mid\Sb)}{d\ln\Sb}-\frac{q(q^2-1)}{6}\,\frac{d\gamma_3}{d\ln\Sb}-\dots
\end{equation}
To second order the curve lies below $q$ if and only if the variance of $\ln v$ at fixed size grows with size, and
above $q$ if it shrinks.  For a lognormal $X$ only $\gamma_2=\sigma^2(\Sb)$ is left and the mean of $\ln X$ is $-\sigma^2/2$, so
$K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}.  If the law of $X$ does not depend on size,
every $\gamma_n$ is constant and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.

\subsection{The identity for a floor and a falling part}

Assume $v_i^2=(u_i+B(\Sb_i))\,\epsilon_i^2$, with $\epsilon_i$ independent of $u_i$ and of $\Sb_i$ (estimation noise), and $B$ a function
of size alone.  Let $p(u\mid\Sb)$ be the law of the floor at fixed size and
\begin{equation}
E_q(\Sb)=\int p(u\mid\Sb)\,(u+B(\Sb))^{q/2}\,\dd u,\qquad \E[v^q\mid\Sb]=E_q(\Sb)\,\E[\epsilon^q].
\end{equation}
Since $\E[\epsilon^q]$ does not depend on size, $\zeta_q=-\dd\ln E_q/\dd\ln\Sb$.  Differentiate under the integral; there are two
contributions, from $B(\Sb)$ inside the power and from $p$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd E_q}{\dd\ln\Sb}=\int p\,\frac q2(u+B)^{q/2-1}\frac{\dd B}{\dd\ln\Sb}\,\dd u+\int\frac{\partial p}{\partial\ln\Sb}(u+B)^{q/2}\dd u .
\end{equation}
With $\dd B/\dd\ln\Sb=-bB$ and $(u+B)^{q/2-1}B=(u+B)^{q/2}w$, $w=B/(u+B)$, the first term is $-\tfrac q2bE_q\langle w\rangle_q$, where
\begin{equation}
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E[(u+B)^{q/2}w\mid\Sb]}{\E[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb]} .
\end{equation}
Dividing by $-E_q$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\qquad c_q=\frac1{E_q}\int\frac{\partial p(u\mid\Sb)}{\partial\ln\Sb}(u+B)^{q/2}\,\dd u .}
\label{eq:app-identity}
\end{equation}
$c_q=0$ when the law of the floor does not depend on size.
The composition term does not vanish on the network: larger lives have larger degrees on average, and the floor grows
with the degree, so $c_q>0$ and the decline is slower than the noise share alone implies.

\paragraph{Floor whose law shifts with size.}  If $\ln u=\vartheta(\Sb)+\xi$ with the law of $\xi$ fixed, then the size dependence of $p$
is entirely through $\vartheta$, and $\partial_{\ln\Sb}$ at fixed $B$ acts on $(e^{\vartheta+\xi}+B)^{q/2}$ as
$\tfrac q2(u+B)^{q/2-1}u\,\vartheta'$.  So
\begin{equation}
c_q=\frac q2\big(1-\langle w\rangle_q\big)\vartheta',\qquad
\zeta_q=\frac q2\Big[b\langle w\rangle_q-\big(1-\langle w\rangle_q\big)\vartheta'\Big],\qquad \vartheta'=\frac{\dd\vartheta}{\dd\ln\Sb}.
\end{equation}
A rising floor ($\vartheta'>0$) flattens the decline.  It also adds to the bend: write $\zeta_q=\tfrac q2[(b+\vartheta')x_q-\vartheta']$ with
$x_q=\langle w\rangle_q$.  For $\vartheta'>0$, $x_q<x_1$ and $\zeta_q,\zeta_1>0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{(b+\vartheta')x_q-\vartheta'}{(b+\vartheta')x_1-\vartheta'}<\frac{x_q}{x_1}\iff -x_1\vartheta'<-x_q\vartheta'\iff x_q<x_1 ,
\end{equation}
so the ratio is below the $c_q=0$ value $qx_q/x_1$.

\paragraph{Homogeneous floor.}  If $u$ is the same for all firms of a given size, however it depends on size, then
$E_q=Y(\Sb)^{q/2}$ with $Y=u+B$ and $\zeta_q=-\tfrac q2\dd\ln Y/\dd\ln\Sb=q\zeta_1$ exactly, whatever $c_q$.

\subsection{Monotonicity of $\langle w\rangle_q$ and the bound $\zeta_q/\zeta_1\le q$}

With $Y=u+B$ at fixed size, $\langle w\rangle_q=\E[Y^{q/2}\cdot B/Y]/\E[Y^{q/2}]=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$.  Let
$\psi(q)=\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]=\ln\E[e^{q\cdot\frac12\ln Y}]$, the cumulant generating function of $\tfrac12\ln Y$; it is convex (H\"older).  Then
$\langle w\rangle_q=B\exp[\psi(q-2)-\psi(q)]$.  For a convex function the secant slope $[\psi(q)-\psi(q-2)]/2$ is non-decreasing in $q$, so
$\langle w\rangle_q$ is non-increasing, and strictly decreasing unless $\psi$ is linear, i.e.\ unless $\ln Y$ is a.s.\ constant at fixed size.
With $c_q=0$ (and $b$ the same for all $q$, as it is the slope of $B$),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q\qquad(q\ge1),
\end{equation}
with equality iff the floor is the same for all firms of equal size.  $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratio.

\subsection{The lognormal case}

If $\ln Y\mid\Sb\sim\mathcal N(\ell,s^2)$, then $\E[Y^a]=e^{a\ell+a^2s^2/2}$ and
\begin{equation}
\langle w\rangle_q=B\exp\Big[\Big(\frac q2-1\Big)\ell+\Big(\frac q2-1\Big)^2\frac{s^2}2-\frac q2\ell-\Big(\frac q2\Big)^2\frac{s^2}2\Big]
=B\,e^{-\ell}\,e^{(1-q)s^2/2},
\end{equation}
since $(q/2-1)^2-(q/2)^2=1-q$.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}=e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\qquad \frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2}\quad(c_q=0),
\qquad\frac{\langle w\rangle_4}{\langle w\rangle_1}=e^{-3s^2/2}.
\end{equation}
Exact for a lognormal $Y$; but $Y=u+B\ge B$, so $Y$ cannot be exactly lognormal and the formula is an approximation, good
when $s$ is small or $B$ is small compared with the typical $Y$.

\paragraph{Narrow floor.}  With $u=\bar ue^{\xi}$ and $\xi$ small, $\ln(u+B)\simeq\ln(\bar u+B)+\frac{\bar u}{\bar u+B}\xi$, so
$s^2\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb)$ with $w$ at $\bar u$.  A heterogeneous floor bends the curve only where it is not small against
$B$: at large sizes.


\paragraph{Scope.}  The statement that the curve bends only if floors differ holds within the two-part law
$v^2=(u+B(\Sb))\epsilon^2$, with $B$ a function of size alone and $\epsilon$ independent of size.  Outside it the general
criterion of Sec.~\ref{app:cumulant} applies: any dispersion of $\ln v$ that grows with size bends the curve, and any
dispersion that shrinks with size pushes it above $q$.  The second case occurs in two places below: the
estimation noise of short lives, and the per-life estimate of $B$, which weighs most in the variance of the
smallest firms.

\subsection{Estimation noise}

Let a life have $n$ growth rates, independent and Gaussian with standard deviation $s$.  With the mean absolute
deviation, $v=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,n^{-1}\sum_t|g_t-\bar g|$.  For a Gaussian $\E|g-\bar g|\simeq s\sqrt{2/\pi}$, so $\E v\simeq s$
(exactly, $g_t-\bar g$ has variance $s^2(1-1/n)$ and $\E v=s\sqrt{1-1/n}$; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}),
and $\Var|g|=s^2(1-2/\pi)$, so
\begin{equation}
\Var\ln\epsilon\simeq\frac{\Var v}{(\E v)^2}=\frac1n\,\frac{1-2/\pi}{2/\pi}=\frac{\pi/2-1}{n}.
\end{equation}
With the standard deviation, $v^2$ is $s^2$ times a chi-squared variable over its number of degrees of freedom,
$\Var(v^2)/s^4\simeq2/n$, and $\Var\ln\epsilon=\tfrac14\Var\ln v^2\simeq1/(2n)$.  Both are leading orders in $1/n$, and fat
tails raise both.  For short lives the leading order is far too small.  The sum of squares about the
sample mean is $s^2$ times a chi-squared variable with $n-1$ degrees of freedom, whose logarithm has
variance $\psi'((n-1)/2)$, with $\psi'$ the trigamma function, so that exactly
\begin{equation}
\Var\ln\epsilon=\tfrac14\,\psi'\Big(\frac{n-1}{2}\Big):
\end{equation}
$1.23$, $0.41$ and $0.16$ for $n=2$, $3$ and $5$, against $0.25$, $0.17$ and $0.10$ from $1/(2n)$.  With all
lives, the mean of the exact value within size bins falls from $0.92$ in the smallest fifth to $0.05$ in
the largest ($0.21$ to $0.03$ at leading order), and the measured variance of $\ln v$ from $1.04$ to $0.14$
(\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).  If the mean number of growth rates grows with size, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal} adds $+\tfrac{q(q-1)}2\,|d\Var\ln\epsilon/d\ln\Sb|$ to $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$: the ratios move up.

\subsection{What the floor is made of}

\paragraph{Splitting a life.}  The floor of a life is the variance of the growth rates $g^F_t$ of its noise-free path
$y_F$ (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}),
$V_F=n^{-1}\sum_t(g^F_t)^2-(\bar g^F)^2$.  A complete life starts at the newborn size and ends at an exit, so its
mean growth rate $\bar g^F=n^{-1}\ln(S_{\rm end}/S_0)$ is small and we drop its square.  The core of a life runs
from the first to the last sample at which the noise-free size is at least half its median; before it is the
climb and after it the collapse.  Splitting the sum,
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq\frac{n_{\rm core}}{n}\,V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},\qquad
E_{\rm in}=\sum_{\rm climb}(g^F_t)^2,\quad E_{\rm out}=\sum_{\rm collapse}(g^F_t)^2 ,
\end{equation}
and for the long lives considered $n_{\rm core}\simeq n$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.  In the numbers of
Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} the entry and exit part is the remainder $V_F-V_{\rm core}$, so it also carries the
factor $n_{\rm core}/n$ and the dropped $(\bar g^F)^2$.  For a path that changes
slowly over one step, $g^F_t\simeq(d\ln S/dt)\,\Delta t$ and a sum of squares over a stretch is
$\sum_t(g^F_t)^2\simeq\Delta t\int(d\ln S/dt)^2\,dt$.  By the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality
$(g^F_t)^2\le\Delta t\int_t^{t+\Delta t}(d\ln S/dt)^2\,dt$, so the integral is an upper bound on the sum.

\paragraph{The climb.}  In a constant field $h$ the noise-free equation is $\dot S=S(h-S)$.  With $z=S/h$,
$\dot z=hz(1-z)$ and $d\ln S/dt=h(1-z)$.  Changing variable from $t$ to $z$, $dt=dz/[hz(1-z)]$, and from the
initial size $S_0$ to the plateau,
\begin{equation}
\int\Big(\frac{d\ln S}{dt}\Big)^2dt=\int_{S_0/h}^1\frac{h^2(1-z)^2}{hz(1-z)}\,dz=h\int_{S_0/h}^1\frac{1-z}{z}\,dz
=h\Big[\ln\frac h{S_0}-1+\frac{S_0}h\Big].
\end{equation}
Times $\Delta t$ this is $E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$ for $S_0\ll h$.

\paragraph{The collapse.}  In a field that has fallen to $-\varphi<0$, $\dot S=-S(\varphi+S)$ and $d\ln S/dt=-(\varphi+S)$.
With $dt=-dS/[S(\varphi+S)]$, from the plateau $h$ down to $S_{\rm end}$,
\begin{equation}
\int\Big(\frac{d\ln S}{dt}\Big)^2dt=\int_{S_{\rm end}}^h\frac{\varphi+S}{S}\,dS=\varphi\ln\frac h{S_{\rm end}}+h-S_{\rm end}.
\end{equation}
Times $\Delta t$ this is $E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$.  Both terms grow with the plateau $h$,
and both are divided by the length of the life in Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.

\paragraph{Test.}  On the $175\,171$ complete lives with at
least $20$ growth rates, the exit formula follows the measured sum life by life (log-correlation
$0.85$--$0.91$ by fifth) and lies above it by $6$--$40\%$, as an upper bound should; the entry formula has the right scale but
correlates less (log-correlation $0.5$), because a firm climbs in a field that moves.  By fifth of size,
the core $V_{\rm core}$ falls from $0.084$ to $0.052$, while the share of entry and exit in $V_F$ grows from a
quarter to $62\%$.

\subsection{How long a firm lives}

\paragraph{A firm in a dip is a branching process.}  An established firm sits near its plateau.  If the
plateau is well above $\sqrt D$ its own noise cannot kill it: the exit time grows as $e^{\Phi^2/D}$
(Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  It dies when its field dips below zero.  Inside the dip the firm is small and the logistic term $-S^2$ is negligible, so
\begin{equation}
dS=r(t)\,S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW,
\end{equation}
with $r(t)$ the field less the self-limitation along the noise-free path.  This is Feller's branching diffusion
with a time-dependent rate \cite{feller1951}.  Because independent pieces of the firm evolve independently, the
Laplace transform of its size is exponential in the initial size, $\E[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-S_0\omega_0}$, where $\omega_0$ is
the value at time $0$ of the solution of the backward equation $-\partial_s\omega=r(s)\,\omega-\tfrac12D\omega^2$ with $\omega(t)=\lambda$.
The equation is linear in $1/\omega$, $\partial_s(1/\omega)=r/\omega-D/2$, and with $R(t)=\int_0^tr(s)\,ds$ its solution is
\begin{equation}
\frac1{\omega_0}=\frac{e^{-R(t)}}{\lambda}+\frac D2\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds .
\end{equation}
The probability that the firm is extinct by time $t$ is the limit $\lambda\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S_t=0)=\exp\Big[-\frac{2S_0}{D\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds}\Big].
\end{equation}
The noise-free path through the dip is $S_{\rm det}(t)=S_0e^{R(t)}$, so $S_0^{-1}\int e^{-R}ds=\int dt/S_{\rm det}$, which
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill}: $P_{\rm kill}=\exp[-2/(DJ)]$ with $J=\int dt/S_{\rm det}$.  For a constant rate $r>0$ and
$t\to\infty$ it reduces to the classical survival probability $1-e^{-2rS_0/D}$.

\paragraph{The bottom of the dip.}  $J$ is dominated by the times at which the noise-free path is smallest.  At its
minimum $\dot y_F=\Phi-S_{\min}=0$, and nearby $\ln S_{\rm det}\simeq\ln S_{\min}+\tfrac12\Phi't^2$, with $\Phi'$ the slope of
the field as it recovers.  A Gaussian integral gives
\begin{equation}
J\simeq\frac1{S_{\min}}\int e^{-\Phi't^2/2}\,dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}}{S_{\min}} .
\end{equation}
A dip kills when it brings the firm below a size of order $D$ times its recovery time $\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}$.

\paragraph{Test of the kill law.}  For every downcrossing of zero by the field of a node whose firm is established
($S\ge0.3$), we restart the noise-free path at the firm's actual size, integrate until it recovers to that size,
and compare $P_{\rm kill}$ with whether the firm died.  Over the $446\,554$ dips the predicted kill fraction is
$0.725$ against $0.722$ observed.  Binned by the predicted probability, the observed kill fractions are $0.015$ at
$0.027$, $0.102$ at $0.147$, $0.418$ at $0.450$, $0.894$ at $0.853$ and $0.986$ at $0.974$, and the log-likelihood per
dip is $-0.175$ against $-0.591$ for a constant kill rate.  No parameter is fitted.

\paragraph{Exits happen in dips.}  The test above is conditional on a dip.  Of the $186\,492$ exits of lives
with at least $20$ growth rates, the field is below zero at the last sample before the exit in $83\%$
($81\%$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $85\%$ in the largest), and at one of the last four samples in
$95\%$ ($92\%$ to $97\%$).  Over all the samples of the same lives the field is below zero $21\%$ of the
time ($35\%$ in the smallest fifth to $10\%$ in the largest).  Exits therefore happen in dips at every
size, also among the small firms whose plateau is not far above $\sqrt D$
(\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Lives are memoryless.}  The hazard of an established firm is the rate of its dips times their mean
kill probability.  Dips are rarer the higher the node's mean field $\bar\Phi$ stands over its temporal
amplitude $f$, so the mean life grows steeply with $\bar\Phi/f$.  It grows from about $23$ to $220$ growth rates
across deciles of $\bar\Phi/f$.  Successive dips are nearly independent, so
the life of an established firm is close to exponential.  Among complete lives the coefficient of variation of
$n-20$ is $1.05$--$1.22$ in every decile of $\bar\Phi/f$ but the highest, where the window cuts the longest lives; an
exponential law gives $1$.  The logarithm of an exponential variable has variance $\pi^2/6\simeq1.6$, which is
the scale of the spread of $\ln n$ among the largest firms ($0.91$ at fixed size, Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).

\section{Tests on firm data}
\label{app:tests}

The criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} holds for any panel.  The mechanism of Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} is a
claim about where the dispersion at fixed size comes from.  It leads to four tests (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions};
two reference economies, the panel's estimator).

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_predictions.pdf}

at least $20$ growth rates.  (c) $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ after trimming $k$ years after each birth and before

\label{fig:predictions}

\paragraph{The dispersion at fixed size grows with size.}  For long lives the within-bin variance of $\ln v$
grows with size, most among the largest firms (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}a).  Compustat's exponents imply that
its dispersion grows, but not where (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  Measuring $K_q(\Sb)$ by size would show where.

\paragraph{Life length and exposure explain a growing share of it.}  Within size bins, the life length and the
degree explain a share of $\Var\ln v$ that grows with size (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}b).  In firm data the life is
the span from listing to delisting.  The degree is a measure of exposure to other firms, such as the number of
major customers or suppliers a firm discloses.  The prediction is not that either matters, but that their share
grows with size.

\paragraph{Trimming entry and exit years moves the curve towards $q$.}  Dropping the first and last $k$ years
of each life steepens the decline and weakens the bend (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}c).  Keeping only the plateau,
which data cannot locate, goes further in the same direction.  Compustat firms enter at listing, already large,
so the years before delisting should carry the effect.  With short lives the test is not clean, because trimming
also adds estimation noise.

\paragraph{Short lives push the curve towards or above $q$.}  With all lives the ratios lie above $q$, and the
bend appears from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}d).  With short lives the dispersion at fixed size
is U-shaped: estimation noise dominates among small firms and floors among large ones
(Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}a).  Panels that include very small firms \cite{fontanelli2025} should therefore show
ratios near or above $q$, which fall below $q$ as the minimum life length grows.

$\sigma\in\{1.70,1.72\}$ and $D\in\{0.1,0.15,0.2\}$ gives $\zeta_1=0.17$--$0.25$ ($n_{\min}=5$), with $\zeta_1$ growing
with $D$ and falling with $\sigma$; the reference point $\sigma=1.70$, $D=0.1$ gives $\zeta_q=0.198$, $0.382$, $0.537$,
$0.657$.

\paragraph{Noise at equal depth.}  The noise moves the instability line, by $0.33$ per unit $D$ at $\mu=-1.25$
and $0.49$ at $\mu=-2.5$, and in the retune $\zeta_1$ grows with $D$ as the depth $\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu,D)$ falls.
The retune contains two pairs of economies at equal depth and different noise, $(\sigma,D)=(1.70,0.10)$ and
$(1.72,0.15)$, and $(1.70,0.15)$ and $(1.72,0.20)$, whose exponents agree to $0.006$ in $\zeta_1$ and $0.04$ in
the ratios.  Away from the reference point we ran two economies with $N=16000$ at depth $0.21$, where the fit
of Sec.~\ref{sec:data} gives $\zeta_1=0.21$, for $D=0.05$, $0.1$ and $0.2$ at $\mu=-1.25$ and $-2.5$.  They
give $\zeta_1=0.180$, $0.192$, $0.187$ and $0.213$, $0.227$, $0.226$ ($n_{\min}=5$): over a factor four in $D$
the exponent varies by at most $0.014$, against $0.07$ and $0.10$ at fixed $\sigma$.  The two graphs at
$\mu=-2.5$ differ by $0.09$, so a dependence on $\mu$ at equal depth is not resolved.  The ratio curve
collapses less well: between $D=0.05$ and $0.2$, $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ rises from $3.25$ to $3.43$ and from $3.57$
to $3.77$ ($n_{\min}=5$), and from $2.52$ to $2.94$ and from $2.80$ to $3.15$ ($n_{\min}=20$), as expected if
more noise raises the share of the demographic part $B\simeq D\Delta t/\Sb$ and hides the floors.  For
$\mu\le-1$ the facts of Sec.~\ref{sec:data} therefore hold in a slab $0.18\lesssim\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu,D)\lesssim0.25$,
with $\sigma_c(\mu,D)$ from the renewal theory, and less noise bends the ratio curve further.  At equal depth the
mean firm grows faster with more noise, by $0.08$ and $0.12$ per unit time between $D=0.05$ and $0.2$, because
the zero-growth line rises with $D$ more slowly than $\sigma_c$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaselines}a;
\texttt{notebooks/02\_phase\_diagram.ipynb}).

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Integration and the minimum life length.}  We integrate by Euler--Maruyama with step $0.01$, discard
$t<100$, and record sizes every $0.5$ time units over a window of length $480$.  MSB's growth rates are annual
differences rolled over quarters, so their robustness cut of $20$ growth rates spans about six years, or five
non-overlapping annual growth rates; their full sample has $46$ per firm on average.  Our $n_{\min}=5$ is the
like-for-like cut.

\paragraph{Spread between economies.}  The facts of Table~\ref{tab:facts} pool the lives of the kept economies,
and the uncertainty we quote for the model is the standard deviation between economies.  Over the seven quiet
production economies, $\zeta_1$ has a mean of $0.213$ and a standard deviation of $0.024$, and the ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ have standard deviations of $0.04$, $0.14$ and $0.29$.  A bootstrap over the lives of one
economy gives $0.0004$ for $\zeta_1$ and $0.002$, $0.008$ and $0.021$ for the ratios.  The spread is therefore not
the sampling noise of the lives.  It is the variation from one economy to the next, through its couplings and its
one finite history, and it grows with $q$ because a few lives with large volatility carry the high moments.  The
bootstrap treats lives as independent, so it is a lower bound.  Compustat is also one finite history: MSB's errors
on $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_4$ give its ratio $\zeta_4/\zeta_1=2.90$ an uncertainty of about $0.25$, close to the
model's $0.29$.

\paragraph{Bursts.}  In a burst one firm runs away.  It grows to $100$--$300$ times the mean firm and collapses
within a few years; in a quiet economy the largest firm is $15$--$45$ times the mean firm.  While the runaway is
large, its growth sets the growth rate $\fb$ of the mean firm, and through the term $-\fb$ in every field it gives
every firm the same shock.  The shock weighs most on large firms, whose own volatility is smallest, so the decline
of volatility flattens and the correlation with the aggregate rises to $0.5$--$0.7$.  Bursts are a property of the
couplings, not of the noise or of the step.  We re-ran the burst economy with two other realisations of the noise
and with half the step.  In all four runs the aggregate volatility is $0.45$--$0.69$ per year, a firm exceeds $50$
times the mean firm in $14$--$17\%$ of the samples, and it is the same node every time, of degree $3526$.  A quiet
economy re-run with other noise stays quiet.  Runaways can also be rare: in one production economy a firm exceeds
$50$ times the mean firm in $2\%$ of the samples, and the aggregate volatility is $0.26$ per year.

We exclude economies whose aggregate volatility exceeds $0.3$ per year: one of the eight production economies, and
five of the $80$ economies of Table~\ref{tab:robust}.  The rule excludes economies in which a runaway dominates,
not every economy with a runaway, and the spread between economies supports it.  The excluded production economy
lies $5.6$ standard deviations below the quiet ones in $\zeta_1$ ($0.076$) and $6.3$ in $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ ($1.54$).
The economy with a rare runaway lies within $1.8$ standard deviations of them, and we keep it.  With the excluded
economy, the pooled panel gives $\zeta_1=0.170$ and ratios $1.79$, $2.30$, $2.52$.  The fraction of excluded
economies shows no trend with $N$: $3$ of $36$ at $N=8000$, $0$ of $28$ at $16000$, $2$ of $14$ at $32000$ and $0$
of $2$ at $64000$, over several parameter points.  We do not know which property of a node lets its firm run away
(\texttt{scripts/bursts.py}).

\paragraph{The year.}  We sample every $0.5$ time units.  The lag-one autocorrelation of growth, demeaned within
each life, for lives with at least five growth rates of the two reference economies, is $-0.05$, $+0.30$,
$+0.36$, $+0.40$ and $+0.46$ by fifth of size at step $0.5$; $+0.13$, $+0.31$, $+0.32$, $+0.32$ and $+0.31$ at
step $1$; and $+0.02$, $+0.06$, $+0.05$, $+0.02$ and $-0.01$ at step $2$.  Demeaning biases each value down by
about $1/n$, most among the short lives of small firms, so the rise with size is robust and the sign in the
smallest fifth is not.  At step $0.5$ the serial correlation rises with size, as in firm data
\cite{coad2007}; at step $2$ it is gone at every size, and a step of $2$ also leaves few growth rates per life.  In the two reference economies, lives with at least five
growth rates give $\zeta_q=0.208$, $0.403$, $0.572$, $0.711$ (ratios $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$) over $2.0$
orders of magnitude at step $0.5$, and $\zeta_q=0.154$, $0.259$, $0.316$, $0.333$ (ratios $1.68$, $2.06$, $2.16$) over
$1.4$ orders of magnitude at step $2$: volatility per step rises by a factor $1.1$ in the smallest size bin and $2.2$
in the largest, so the decline flattens, and the ratio curve bends further below $q$.  For lives with
at least $20$ growth rates the change is smaller ($\zeta_1=0.253$ and $0.247$, ratios $1.79$, $2.37$,
$2.80$ and $1.75$, $2.28$, $2.62$, although at step $2$ these lives span only $0.9$ orders of magnitude).  The bend
below $q$ survives the coarser year; the level of $\zeta_1$ for short lives does not
(\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The factor in the volatility.}  If
$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$, then $\E|X-\mu|=s\int|z|\phi(z)\,\dd z=2s\int_0^\infty z\phi(z)\,\dd z=2s\phi_0=s\sqrt{2/\pi}$,
so $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\E|X-\mu|=s$.  For Gaussian growth $v$ estimates the standard deviation; for fat-tailed
growth it is a robust scale that is smaller than the standard deviation.  About the sample mean of $n$
growth rates the deviation $g_t-\bar g$ has variance $s^2(1-1/n)$, so $\E v=s\sqrt{1-1/n}$: the estimator is
low by $11\%$ at $n=5$ and $29\%$ at $n=2$.  Small firms live shorter, so the bias flattens the decline.
Multiplying $v$ by $\sqrt{n/(n-1)}$ gives $\zeta_1=0.254$, $0.230$ and $0.258$ for $n_{\min}=2$, $5$ and $20$
in the two reference economies, against $0.179$, $0.208$ and $0.253$; the ratios at $n_{\min}=5$ become
$1.93$, $2.72$, $3.35$ against $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$ (\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).  Most of
the dependence of $\zeta_1$ on the minimum life length is this bias; from $n_{\min}=5$ the ratio curve hardly
feels it.
In the comparison with Compustat we keep the estimator of MSB as it is.

\paragraph{Minimum life length.}  MSB keep every firm with at least two growth rates; their firms have $46$ on
average, and their results do not change for firms with at least $20$.  Their growth rates are annual
differences rolled over quarters, $g_t=\ln S_{t+4}-\ln S_t$, so $46$ of them span about twelve years and $20$
about six, or five non-overlapping annual growth rates.  The model's lives are much shorter: $38\%$ of the
lives with at least two growth rates have exactly two ($N=16000$).  We therefore compare with data at
$n_{\min}=5$ and test the theory at $n_{\min}=20$, where each life is long enough to estimate its floor.  In
the production run $\zeta_q=0.180$, $0.434$, $0.708$, $0.965$ (ratios $2.41$, $3.94$, $5.36$) for $n_{\min}=2$, $0.209$,
$0.400$, $0.561$, $0.685$ ($1.92$, $2.69$, $3.28$) for $n_{\min}=5$, $0.218$, $0.388$, $0.508$, $0.582$ ($1.78$, $2.33$,
$2.67$) for $n_{\min}=10$ and $0.254$, $0.452$, $0.598$, $0.701$ ($1.78$, $2.35$, $2.75$) for $n_{\min}=20$, over $2.1$,
$2.0$, $1.8$ and $1.4$ orders of magnitude: the ratios lie above $q$ for $n_{\min}=2$ and below from $n_{\min}=5$, as the
estimation noise of short lives requires (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), and the range shrinks because long lives
do not occur at the newborn sizes.

\paragraph{The fits.}  Figure~\ref{fig:fits} shows the lines behind the exponents of Table~\ref{tab:facts}.  For
$q\le2$ the residuals stay within $0.1$ in log units; for $q=3$ and $4$ the end bins lie above the lines.

\paragraph{The tail index $b$.}  For lives with at least two growth rates the model gives
$b=4.39$--$4.44$ at every parameter point, close to the Compustat value $4.6$.  A null with iid Laplace
growth rates, no differences between firms and the model's distribution of life lengths gives
$4.34$, so the agreement is an effect of short lives.  For longer lives $b$ grows: $5.2$, $5.5$ and $6.6$ in the production
run for $n_{\min}=5$, $10$ and $20$ ($5.7$ and $6.9$ at $N=16000$ for $10$ and $20$), against $9.3$ and $12.6$ for the Laplace null and $4.6$ in
Compustat for both samples (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Volatility estimator.}  For lives with at least five growth rates, the standard deviation instead of
$\sqrt{\pi/2}$ times the mean absolute deviation gives a $\zeta_1$ lower by $0.03$ and ratios higher by $0.03$--$0.3$
(\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}, rows SD; production run: $\zeta_1=0.178$, ratios $1.95$, $2.81$, $3.54$).  The
gap grows for short lives: with all lives the two reference economies give $\zeta_1=0.094$ with the standard
deviation and $0.179$ with the mean absolute deviation (Tables~\ref{tab:cases} and \ref{tab:reduction}).

\paragraph{Rescaled growth.}  MSB rescale each growth rate by its firm's leave-one-out mean and volatility,
$\hat g_t=(g_t-\bar g_{-t})/\sigma_{-t}$, so that a large rate does not shrink its own scale, and find densities
that hardly depend on size \cite{moran2024}.  We rescale the lives with at least five growth rates of the two
reference economies the same way (Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}).  Compustat's densities, kernel estimates in $25$ size
bins, are read from the vector paths of MSB's figures (\texttt{scripts/msb\_curves.py}); we use the plotted
curves because we could not reconcile the fitted parameters of their Table~1 with the plotted fit.  The
curves stop at a density of $10^{-4}$ on $|z|\le8$, so we compare the excess kurtosis on that window.  By fifth
of size the model gives $3.0$, $3.7$, $4.3$, $4.8$, $5.7$ and Compustat $4.2$, $5.6$, $5.1$, $5.4$, $5.7$
($3.5$--$4.2$ in its three smallest bins).  A Gaussian null with the same life lengths gives $2.5$, $2.4$, $1.6$,
$0.4$, $0.1$: among small firms most of the model's kurtosis is the rescaling of short lives.  The full fourth
moments are dominated by rare events beyond $|z|=8$, outside the range of the Compustat curves; rescaling
instead by each life's own volatility, lives with at least $10$ growth rates give $1.3$ to $23$ by fifth in the
production run (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The tail of the growth rates.}  The tails of the pooled growth rates of Fig.~\ref{fig:growth}a
are exponential, not power laws.  Over the $1.8\times10^7$ growth rates of lives with at least five, divided
by their common volatility, the Hill index of $|z|$ rises with the depth of the tail, $2.3$, $3.0$, $3.5$,
$4.3$ and $5.2$ for the top $10\%$, $3\%$, $1\%$, $0.3\%$ and $0.1\%$, as for a Laplace law ($3.1$ to $7.8$)
and unlike a power law (Student $t_3$: $2.5$ to $3.0$).  The slope of $\ln P(|z|>x)$ against $x$ is
$-0.53$, $-0.47$ and $-0.48$ over the three orders of magnitude of probability from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$: an exponential
tail, fatter than the Laplace law of the same volatility (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

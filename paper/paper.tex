\title{Interaction heterogeneity and the multiscaling of firm growth volatility}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The growth volatility of firms falls with their size as $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q}$, and the ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ lie below $q$: the dispersion of volatilities among firms of equal size grows with size.
Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of independent parts, give ratios equal to $q$ when
well-diversified firms dominate the moments and close to $1$ when firms made of a few parts do; the data
lie between.  We show that
whenever a firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size,
$\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$, with $w$ the share of the falling part, and the ratios bend below $q$
only if floors differ between firms of equal size.  We then build a dynamical economy in which they do: firms
interact on a scale-free network through generalised Lotka--Volterra dynamics, with birth--death noise
and turnover.  Each firm reduces to a logistic unit driven by its own field.  Its floor contains the
climb after entry and the collapse before exit, and lives end in dips of the field at a rate we derive,
so floors differ with the length of a life and with the degree of its node.  With equal degrees the
floors barely differ and the ratios return to $q$, as the criterion requires.  Sizes, entries, exits and the co-movement of firms, which grows with size as
in the data, are outputs of the dynamics, not inputs.  The model gives
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$, as in firm data, and a ratio curve below $q$, though less bent than in the data; it
caps firm size and has no Zipf law.  We derive tests of the mechanism on firm data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but much more erratically than a sum of independent
parts would.  If $v$ is the volatility of a firm's growth rate and $\Sb$ its mean size over its life,
the conditional moments in Compustat scale as
\begin{equation}
\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q},
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
with $\zeta_q=0.20(1)$, $0.39(2)$, $0.51(3)$, $0.58(4)$ for $q=1,\dots,4$ over four decades of size
\cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002,moran2024}.  The ratios $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.95$, $2.55$, $2.90$ lie
below $q$ \cite{moran2024}.  If a firm's volatility were a function of its size times a random factor
whose law does not depend on size, the ratios would be exactly $q$.  The bend therefore says that firms of
equal size differ more in volatility the larger they are.  Firm sizes themselves follow Zipf's law
\cite{axtell2001}.

Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of subunits with independent shocks
\cite{wyartbouchaud2003,gabaix2011}, give $\zeta_1<1/2$ when the subunit sizes are broad.  Moran, Secchi
and Bouchaud (MSB) derive their higher moments \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms give
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, but large firms made of a few subunits, whose volatility does not fall with size,
dominate every moment of order two and above, which then all fall with the same exponent, about $0.2$:
the ratios would be close to $1$.  The data lie between the two limits.  MSB attribute their departure
from $q$ to subdominant corrections from the tail of the volatility distribution, and show that granular
models also miss the shape of that distribution and the fat tails of growth rates at every size.  They
conclude that granular mechanisms alone cannot account for the data, and point to the co-movement of
firms, which grows with size, as the missing ingredient \cite{moran2024,herskovic2020}: firms made of
independent parts do not co-move.  What
makes the dispersion of volatilities grow with size, and by as much as it does, has not been explained.

We answer in two steps.  The first does not depend on a model (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  The ratio
curve lies below $q$ exactly where the dispersion of the rescaled volatility grows with size.  If a
firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size, as when a firm's own noise is
averaged out by its size, the exponents obey $\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$, with $b$ the slope of the
falling part and $w$ its share, and the dispersion grows only if the floors differ between firms of
equal size.  The noise of estimating a volatility from a short life acts the other way, because short
lives are concentrated among small firms; this is why the measured bend depends on the shortest lives
kept.

The second step is a dynamical economy of interacting firms in which floors differ
(Secs.~\ref{sec:economy}--\ref{sec:floor}).  A granular model takes the structure of each firm as given and
its shocks as independent.  Here nothing about a firm is put in: its size, its entry and exit, the length
of its life and its co-movement with other firms all follow from one dynamics with a few parameters.  We start
from the disordered generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV) model, whose strongly interacting phase keeps
fluctuating, each species behaving as a logistic unit driven by a coloured Gaussian field
\cite{roy2020,mallmin2024,arnouxbunin2024}.  We measure interactions and self-limitation in units of the
mean firm, so that the economy has no preferred size, place firms on a scale-free network
\cite{acemoglu2012,carvalho2019}, and replace immigration by birth--death noise with turnover: a firm
that reaches zero exits and a newborn takes its node.  Each firm then reduces to a logistic unit in its
own field, and its growth variance to a floor and a demographic part.  The floor contains the climb
after entry and the collapse before exit, spread over the life, and lives end in dips of the field at a
rate given by Rice's formula and the extinction probability of a branching process.  Floors therefore
differ with the length of a life and with the degree of its node, which sets the amplitude of its field.
On a graph with equal degrees they barely differ, and the ratios return close to $q$.  Anomalous moment
scaling has been derived for GLV communities before: with multiplicative environmental noise and migration,
abundances obey Taylor's law with a single exponent for every order \cite{alhiyasat2026}, through the
Bouchaud--M\'ezard wealth model \cite{bouchaudmezard2000}.  That law concerns the moments of an abundance;
ours concerns the moments of the growth volatility conditioned on size, whose exponents bend below
$q\zeta_1$, and depends on the network and on turnover.

At the minimum life length that matches the data, the model gives a monotone decline with
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$ and a ratio curve below $q$, though less bent than Compustat's, and
fails elsewhere (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}): the self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also caps
firm size, so the decline spans two decades rather than four and there is no Zipf law.  Its theory is a
one-firm problem given three statistics of the field.  Heterogeneous mean-field theory gives their form,
but not the amplitude of the field's motion or the tail of its spread at equal degree, which at finite
connectivity are set close to marginal amplification; we take them from simulation
(Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).

The mechanism leads to tests on firm data (Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions}).  The dispersion of volatilities
among firms of equal size should grow with size; the length of a firm's observed life should explain a
growing share of it; trimming the years after entry and before exit should move the ratio curve towards
$q$ and steepen the decline; and adding short lives should push the curve towards or above $q$.
Derivations, checks and robustness tests are in the appendices, and every number is printed by a
notebook of the companion code \cite{code}.

\section{When the ratio curve bends}
\label{sec:criterion}

\paragraph{Dispersion at fixed size.}  Write a firm's volatility as its conditional mean times a rescaled
volatility, $v=m(\Sb)\,X$ with $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$, and let $K_q(\Sb)=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln m+K_q$, and since $K_1=0$,
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
$K_q$ is convex in $q$ and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so for $q>1$ it measures the dispersion of $X$,
weighted more towards its upper tail as $q$ grows.  The ratio curve lies below $q$ where this dispersion
grows with size, on $q$ where it does not change, and above $q$ where it shrinks.  For a lognormal $X$,
with $\sigma^2(\Sb)$ the variance of $\ln X$, $K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$ and
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\,\frac{d\sigma^2}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general-lognormal}
\end{equation}
Granular models show both limits \cite{moran2024}.  When well-diversified firms set the moments, the law
of $X$ does not depend on size and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.  When firms made of a few subunits, whose volatility
does not fall with size, set them, the upper tail of $X$ stretches with size so fast that $\zeta_q$ no longer
depends on $q$ for $q\ge2$.  A model of the bend has to say why the dispersion grows, and by how much.

In Compustat, $\zeta_2-2\zeta_1=-0.01$, within the uncertainties of the exponents, while
$\zeta_3-3\zeta_1=-0.09$ and $\zeta_4-4\zeta_1=-0.22$ \cite{moran2024}.  The second moment of $X$ hardly
changes with size; the third and fourth grow.  The dispersion that grows with size is therefore in the
upper tail of the rescaled volatility, and the collapse of its distribution that MSB report can only be
approximate there.

\paragraph{A floor and a falling part.}  Suppose that a firm's squared volatility is a floor $u_i$ plus a
part $B(\Sb)$ common to all firms of the same size, up to an estimation factor $\epsilon_i$:
\begin{equation}
v_i^2=\big(u_i+B(\Sb_i)\big)\,\epsilon_i^2 .
\label{eq:twopart}
\end{equation}
Let $b=-d\ln B/d\ln\Sb$ be the local slope of the falling part and $w=B/(u+B)$ its share of the variance.
If $\epsilon$ is independent of $u$ and of size, differentiating $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ gives
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\qquad
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\,w\mid\Sb\big]}{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb\big]},
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
where the composition term $c_q$ vanishes when the law of the floor at fixed size does not change with
size (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  The exponent is the slope of the falling part times its share of
the variance, weighted by $v^q$: largest where the falling part dominates and zero where the floor does.

\paragraph{The criterion.}  With $Y=u+B$, $\langle w\rangle_q=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$.  Since
$\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is convex in $q$, $\langle w\rangle_q$ does not increase with $q$, and it is constant only if
$Y$ is the same for all firms of equal size.  When $c_q=0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q,
\label{eq:bend}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if the floor does not vary between firms of equal size.  When $c_q\neq0$ one
direction survives: a floor that is the same for every firm of a given size, however it depends on size,
gives $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, like a granular model of well-diversified firms.  If $\ln Y$ is Gaussian at fixed size with variance $s^2$,
\begin{gather}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}\simeq q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\nonumber\\
s^2=\Var\big(\ln(u+B)\mid\Sb\big)\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb),
\label{eq:lognormal}
\end{gather}
the last form for a narrow floor.  This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:general} at work: as the falling part
shrinks with size the floors show through, $w$ falls and $s^2$ grows.  A heterogeneous floor bends the
curve only where it is not small against the falling part, which is among the large firms.

\paragraph{Estimation noise.}  The factor $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratios only if its law does not depend
on size.  A volatility estimated from $n$ growth rates by their mean absolute deviation has
$\Var\ln\epsilon\simeq(\pi/2-1)/n$ for Gaussian growth, and by their standard deviation about $1/(2n)$;
fat tails raise both.  Wherever small firms live shorter, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and, by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}, pushes the ratio curve above $q$.  Short lives can therefore hide a bend
or reverse it.  In the model, all lives with at least two growth rates give ratios $2.83$, $5.00$, $7.30$
with the standard deviation as the volatility, and the bend appears as the shortest lives are dropped (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).  In firm data the same
effect makes the ratio curve depend on the minimum life length, which should be reported with it.

\paragraph{Three cases.}  Equations~\eqref{eq:general}--\eqref{eq:lognormal} account for the three panels of
the model: on a scale-free network the dispersion at fixed size grows from $s^2=0.02$ in the smallest
fifth of sizes to $0.26$ in the largest and the ratios bend; on a graph with equal degrees it stays below
$0.05$ and the ratios are close to $q$; with all lives the estimation noise dominates and the ratios lie
above $q$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).

\section{An economy of interacting firms}
\label{sec:economy}

\paragraph{Model.}  We consider $N$ firms with sizes $x_i$ and call $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ the mean firm.  The
firms sit on the nodes of a configuration-model graph with degree law $p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and mean
degree $C$, and their sizes evolve as
\begin{equation}
dx_i=x_i\Big[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\sum_jJ_{ij}\frac{x_j}{m}\Big]dt+\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}\;dW_i ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with independent Wiener processes $W_i$.  The couplings are $J_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$, with
$A$ the adjacency matrix and $z_{ij}$ independent standard Gaussians, $z_{ij}$ and $z_{ji}$ uncorrelated;
$\mu<0$ means that competition dominates on average.  A firm that reaches zero is replaced by a newborn of
size $Dm$ on the same node, the size at which the noise $\sqrt{Dmx_i}$ equals $x_i$, so entries occur where
and when the dynamics makes firms exit.  Every term is homogeneous of degree one in the sizes: the economy
has no preferred scale, and the mean firm grows at a rate the dynamics selects.  The noise unit grows
with the economy, and can be read as an indivisible piece of business, such as a contract or a customer.

\paragraph{A logistic unit in a moving field.}  In relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$,
\begin{equation}
dS_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i+O(N^{-1/2}),
\label{eq:onefirm}
\end{equation}
with the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\bar f(t),
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
where $\bar f$ is the growth rate of the mean firm, including the dilution by entries
(Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a logistic equation whose carrying capacity
moves in time.  If the field on a firm stays at $\Phi>0$, the firm relaxes at rate $\Phi$ to the plateau
$x_i=m\Phi$; if $\Phi<0$, it shrinks until it exits.  For $C\to\infty$ the relative sizes map onto the
standard GLV with demographic noise \cite{altieri2021}, and on the complete graph, with immigration in place
of the noise, onto the chaotic-turnover model of Refs.~\cite{bunin2017,mallmin2024}.

\paragraph{Phase diagram.}  For $C\to\infty$ heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory (HDMFT) replaces the
network by one process per degree class, closed by averages over neighbours drawn in proportion to their
degree \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026}.  A node of relative degree $\kappa=k/C$ feels
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)=1-\bar f+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
\label{eq:hdmft}
\end{equation}
with $\bar B$ the degree-weighted mean size and $\xi$ a Gaussian process whose correlation is the
degree-weighted correlation of sizes.  The static solution loses stability at a line $\sigma_c(\mu)$, which
on an uncorrelated graph is, to first order in the degree variance,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_c\simeq\sqrt2\Big[1-\frac{\Var\kappa}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\mu^2}{2\pi}\Big)\Big]
\label{eq:sigmac}
\end{equation}
(Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}): hubs feel a field of amplitude $\propto\sqrt\kappa$ and lower the threshold, while a mean
interaction of either sign spreads the classes' distances from extinction and raises it.  With noise the line
follows from the renewal law of a node at constant field, which also predicts the turnover to $1$--$2\%$
(Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  Above it
the composition of the economy keeps changing.  Our reference point, $\alpha=2.5$, $\sigma=1.70$, $D=0.1$,
$C=200$, $\mu=-1.76$, lies in this fluctuating phase ($\sigma_c=1.497$ at $D=0.1$), close to the line on which
the mean firm stops growing.

\paragraph{Protocol.}  We integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} in relative form by Euler--Maruyama with step
$0.01$, discard $t<100$, and record sizes every $\Delta t=0.5$, which we take as one year, over a window of
length $480$.  A \emph{life} runs from a birth to the next exit at the same node; lives cut by the window
are kept, as in MSB's unbalanced panel.  A life recorded at $n+1$ times has growth rates
$g_t=\ln S_{t+1}-\ln S_t$, and its volatility and size are
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|g_t-\bar g\big|,\qquad
\Sb=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}S_t,
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
the factor making $v$ the standard deviation for Gaussian growth.  Exponents are least-squares slopes of
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ against $\ln\Sb$ over $25$ equal-count bins.  We compare with data on lives with at
least five growth rates, the cut that matches MSB's (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}), and test the theory on lives with
at least $20$, long enough to estimate each life's floor.  The comparison with data uses seven
economies with $N=32000$; the theory tests use two with $N=16000$.

\paragraph{Where the facts hold.}  The reference point is one point of a band (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).  Near
the zero-growth line, $\zeta_1$ and the ratio curve depend on a single variable, the depth $\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu)$
of the economy in the fluctuating phase: for $\mu\le-1$ and lives with at least five growth rates,
$\zeta_1=0.49-1.34\,(\sigma-\sigma_c)$, with a scatter of $0.014$ over $27$ points.  The deeper the economy, the
more its field moves, the larger the floor of large firms and the flatter the decline.  Depths from $0.18$
to $0.25$ give $\zeta_1=0.15$--$0.25$.  For $\mu$ from $-1.25$ to $-2.25$ this band contains economies whose
mean firm grows by between $-0.08$ and $+0.16$ per unit time, so an economy that competes moderately and
grows slowly sits in it; slow growth alone does not, since along the zero-growth line $\zeta_1$ falls from
$0.40$ under weak competition to $0.13$ under strong competition.  The band is about $0.07$ wide in $\sigma$.

\section{One firm in its field}
\label{sec:onefirm}

\paragraph{The field can be taken as given.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is exact; its content is that a
firm's own noise does not feed back on its field through the network.  We store the fields of the two
reference economies, integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} for every node with its measured field and fresh
noise, and compare the life panels: the exponents agree to $0.01$ and the ratios to $0.06$ for every
minimum life length, and the exit rates agree exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}).  The size--volatility
statistics are a one-firm problem, given the statistics of the field.

\paragraph{Three statistics of the field.}  A node's mean field falls with its degree, from $0.5$ at
$\kappa\simeq0.4$ to $-2.3$ at $\kappa\simeq2$: hubs compete with more firms.  Among nodes of equal degree the
mean field has a spread of $2.66\sqrt\kappa$ in standard deviation, with a fat tail (excess kurtosis $2.1$),
and around its mean the field moves with amplitude $2.16\sqrt\kappa$ (as $\kappa^{0.54}$ in a fit over nodes)
and decorrelates over about $4$ time units.  These three statistics are enough.  Driving each firm by a
Gaussian field with its node's time mean, its temporal amplitude and one correlation function gives
$\zeta_1=0.23$ and ratios $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model.
Reshuffling the means among nodes of similar degree changes little ($0.28$; $1.81$, $2.42$, $2.86$), but
setting them to their mean in the degree class sends the ratios back towards $q$ ($1.93$, $2.79$, $3.56$).
The essential input is the spread of the mean field among firms of equal degree.  HDMFT,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, gives the form of these statistics, and its closure holds on the measured sizes to
$4$--$6\%$.  Solved self-consistently with a Gaussian field, it gets the mean but not the temporal amplitude,
which comes out $20$--$30\%$ low, or the tail of the spread; at $C=200$ both depend on the few neighbours that
dominate a firm's field, close to a marginal amplification (Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).  We therefore take
the field's statistics from simulation.

\paragraph{The volatility law.}  In logs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} reads
$d\ln S=[\Phi-S-D/(2S)]\,dt+\sqrt{D/S}\,dW$.  Write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$, where $y_F$ solves the noise-free
equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ from the same initial size.  To linear order $\delta$ is an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process that relaxes at the local rate $S$ and is driven by noise of variance $D/S$.
Treating the two parts as independent, the variance of a firm's yearly growth over its life is
\begin{equation}
\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$, the floor, is the growth variance of $y_F$.  For every life with at least $20$ growth rates,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} reproduces the measured volatility to within $5\%$ above the lowest decile of size and
$9\%$ in it (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}a), where the linearisation is worst: it needs $S\gg\sqrt{D/2}\simeq0.22$.
The floor is nearly flat, $\sqrt{V_F}=0.32$--$0.38$, although $\sqrt{\Var\Delta\ln\Phi}$ falls from $0.92$ to
$0.44$ with size: a firm does not follow the fast part of its field.  The demographic part falls as
$\Sb^{-b}$ with $b=0.65$--$0.79$, below the value $1$ of $B\simeq D\Delta t/\Sb$ because $B$ is dominated by the
low excursions of a life, and the two parts are equal at $\Sb\simeq1.6$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:law} is
therefore the two-part law of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}, with $u=V_F$.  The decomposition has the two-regime
shape reported for data that include very small firms \cite{fontanelli2025}, but the measured panel does not
resolve the two regimes, and $\zeta_1$ is the effective slope of the whole range.

\section{Where the floors differ}
\label{sec:floor}

Section~\ref{sec:onefirm} gives every life a growth variance $V_F+B$: a floor $V_F$, the growth variance of
the noise-free firm along the life's own field, and a demographic part $B$ that falls with size.  This
is the two-part law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:twopart} with $u=V_F$.  Here we ask what the floor is made of, why
it differs between lives of equal size, and whether Eqs.~\eqref{eq:identity}--\eqref{eq:lognormal}
account for the panel.  Throughout, the volatility is the growth standard deviation, and the lives are
those with at least $20$ growth rates in the two reference economies, unless stated otherwise.

\paragraph{The floor is mostly entry and exit.}  A life of this length usually begins as a newborn at
$S\simeq D$ and ends at an exit, so its noise-free path climbs to a plateau, follows the field there,
and collapses in the final dip.  Splitting the path where the noise-free size first and last reaches
half its median,
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
with $V_{\rm core}$ the growth variance on the plateau and $E_{\rm in}$, $E_{\rm out}$ the sums of squared
growth rates over the climb and the collapse.  For a logistic climb from $S_0$ to a plateau $h$,
$E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$, and for a collapse in a field that has fallen to $-\varphi$,
$E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$: both grow with the plateau
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  The two parts move in opposite directions.  The core falls from
$0.084$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $0.052$ in the largest, while the share of entry and exit grows
from a quarter of $V_F$ to $62\%$: large firms climb further from the newborn size and collapse from
higher.  Their sum is the nearly flat floor of Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}.

\paragraph{Lives end in dips of the field.}  An established firm dies when its field dips.  Inside the
dip the firm is small, the logistic term is negligible, and the firm is a Feller branching process,
whose extinction probability is exact:
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm kill}=\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{D\,J}\Big),\qquad J=\int\frac{dt}{S_{\rm det}(t)},
\label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}
with $S_{\rm det}$ the noise-free path through the dip.  Over the $446\,554$ dips of established firms,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill} predicts a kill fraction of $0.725$ against $0.722$ observed, with no fitted
parameter.  Dips begin at the downcrossings of zero, which occur at Rice's rate
$\sqrt{-\rho''(0)}\,e^{-u^2/2}/2\pi$, with $u$ the node's mean field over its temporal amplitude.  Lives
are therefore memoryless, with a mean that grows steeply with $u$, from about $23$ to $220$ growth rates
across deciles of $u$.

\paragraph{Floors differ with life length and degree.}  By Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} the same climb and
collapse are spread over lives of very different lengths, and the lengths are broad because lives end
at memoryless dips.  Within size bins, $\Var(\ln V_F\mid\Sb)$ grows from $0.5$ in the smallest fifth to
$0.9$ in the largest, and the spread of life lengths, $\Var(\ln n\mid\Sb)$, from $0.08$ to $0.91$.  In
the four upper fifths the life length explains $26$--$62\%$ of $\Var\ln V_F$ within bins, more than the
degree ($19$--$41\%$; $8\%$ against $14\%$ in the smallest fifth).  The degree acts through both parts
of the floor.  A better connected firm feels a field of larger amplitude, which keeps its core up, and
more frequent killing dips, which shorten its life (the correlation of $\ln n$ with $\ln\kappa$ within
size bins falls from $-0.16$ in the smallest fifth to $-0.49$ in the largest) and raise its entry and
exit part.  This keeps the floor of the larger firms at $V_F\simeq0.13$ while their demographic part
falls.

\paragraph{The criterion on the panel.}  The noise share falls from $\langle w\rangle_1=0.80$ in the
smallest fifth to $0.44$ in the largest, and the dispersion at fixed size, with $B$ at its bin mean,
grows from $s^2=0.021$ to $0.264$.  Within size bins $\langle w\rangle_4/\langle w\rangle_1=0.96$, $0.92$,
$0.87$, $0.79$, $0.61$ by fifth, against $0.97$, $0.93$, $0.89$, $0.83$, $0.67$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lognormal}.  In the four lower fifths the local exponents agree with
$(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$ to within $13\%$; in the largest the composition term dominates, because the
largest lives also have the largest degrees (mean $\kappa$ from $1.00$ to $1.53$), and the local
exponents are $0.13$, $0.16$, $0.10$, $-0.03$ against $0.17$, $0.29$, $0.37$, $0.41$.  Keeping each life's
own floor and replacing $B$ by its bin mean, so that the floor is the only source of dispersion, gives
ratios $1.85$, $2.55$, $3.07$ against $1.75$, $2.30$, $2.70$ measured.  Replacing instead the floor by its
bin mean and keeping each life's own estimate of $B$ gives $2.08$, $3.68$, $6.61$, above $q$: without the
spread of the floor only estimation noise is left, as in Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}.  Keeping one part of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} at its own value and the other at its bin mean gives $1.98$, $2.94$, $3.85$ with
only the core heterogeneous and $1.90$, $2.69$, $3.33$ with only the entry and exit part.  The bend is
carried mostly by the climb and the collapse, through the spread of life lengths.

\paragraph{What the model gets.}  At the like-for-like cut, $\zeta_1=0.209\pm0.024$ against $0.20(1)$.  The
decline is monotone, with no hump at small sizes, and the ratio curve lies below $q$ at both cuts.  Rescaled
growth rates are fat-tailed at every size, and among the largest firms as fat as in Compustat
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}).  The
co-movement of a firm with the aggregate and with firms of similar size rises with size, as in
Compustat.  This is the feature MSB single out as the missing ingredient of granular models, whose firms
do not co-move \cite{moran2024}; here it comes from the interactions.  A natural reading is that small
firms are moved mostly by their own noise and large ones by their field, which they share with their
neighbours, but we have no quantitative theory of it.

\paragraph{The shape of the bend.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:general} reads the row $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$ of
Table~\ref{tab:facts} as the growth with size of the moments of the rescaled volatility.  In Compustat the
second moment hardly grows and the fourth does: the upper tail spreads.  At the like-for-like cut the
model gets the second moment but its upper tail spreads too little ($-0.15$ against $-0.22$).  At
$n_{\min}=20$ its dispersion grows already in the bulk ($-0.06$ against $-0.01$) and its tail at least as
much as in Compustat.  The difference between the two cuts is the estimation noise of short lives, which
shrinks with size and offsets part of the floors' spread (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  Neither cut gives
Compustat's shape: the floors of the model spread the whole distribution of volatilities at fixed size,
while in Compustat the spread is concentrated in the upper tail.

\paragraph{What it misses.}  The decline spans two decades rather than four, and volatility falls by a
factor $2.8$ against $5.5$.  The tail of the volatility distribution is too thin, $b=5.2$ against $4.6$, and thinner for longer
lives; with all lives the model gives $b=4.4$, but a null with iid Laplace growth and no differences
between firms gives $4.3$, so that value is an artefact of short lives.  The rescaled growth rates of small
firms have tails that are too thin: their excess kurtosis is $3.0$ against $4.2$, and a Gaussian null with
the same lives gives $2.5$ from the rescaling of short lives alone.  The model's tails are also asymmetric, the
negative one fatter, and multi-year persistence is confined to the largest firms: small firms are moved by
near-Gaussian, mean-reverting birth--death noise, large firms by rare and persistent dips of their field,
which make their fat tails and the collapses before exit.  Aggregate volatility is several times too large
and does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$ over the sizes we simulate, and $5$ of $80$ economies in our robustness runs
enter a collective burst in which volatility no longer depends on size.  The size distribution has no
Pareto tail, newborns have extreme infant mortality, and small established firms shrink on average.  The
missing tail and the short range come from the self-limitation: a firm whose field stays at $\Phi>0$
relaxes to the plateau $x=m\Phi$, a few times the mean firm, so sizes are capped near the highest plateaus,
and the decline runs from a few times the newborn size $D$, below which lives are too short to measure, to
the plateau.

\section{Predictions for firm data}
\label{sec:predictions}

The criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} holds for any panel; the mechanism of Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} is a
claim about where the dispersion at fixed size comes from.  It leads to four tests.  The model's numbers
below are for the two reference economies, with the panel's estimator.

\paragraph{1. The dispersion at fixed size grows with size.}  For lives with at least $20$ growth rates the
within-bin variance of $\ln v$ grows from $0.048$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $0.162$ in the largest.
Compustat's exponents imply that its dispersion grows mainly in the upper tail (Sec.~\ref{sec:data});
measuring $K_q(\Sb)$ by size directly would show where, and whether the growth is concentrated among the
largest firms, as the criterion requires when it comes from floors.

\paragraph{2. Life length and exposure explain a growing share of it.}  Within size bins, the length of a
life explains $5\%$ of $\Var\ln v$ in the smallest fifth and $61\%$ in the largest, and the degree $4\%$ and
$40\%$.  In firm data the life is the observed span from listing to delisting, and the degree a measure
of exposure to other firms, such as the number of major customers or suppliers a firm discloses.  The
prediction is not that either matters, but that their share of the dispersion grows with size.

\paragraph{3. Trimming entry and exit years moves the curve towards $q$.}  Dropping the first $k$ years
after a birth and the last $k$ before an exit, and keeping lives that still have at least $20$ growth
rates, steepens the decline and weakens the bend: for $k=0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, $5$, $\zeta_1=0.253$, $0.255$,
$0.272$, $0.291$, $0.320$ and $\zeta_4/\zeta_1=2.80$, $2.96$, $3.08$, $3.17$, $3.23$.  Keeping only the
plateau of each life, which data cannot locate, goes further in the same direction, to $\zeta_1=0.38$ and
$\zeta_4/\zeta_1=3.44$.  Compustat firms enter at listing, already large, so the climb from the newborn size has no
counterpart there and the years before delisting should carry the effect.  With shorter lives the test is
not clean: at $n_{\min}=5$ trimming also shortens lives and adds estimation noise, and the sequence is not
monotone.

\paragraph{4. Short lives push the curve towards or above $q$.}  With all lives the model's ratios are
$2.42$, $4.01$, $5.64$, and the bend appears from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ ($1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$).  When short lives are kept, the
dispersion at fixed size is not monotone in size: for $n_{\min}=5$ the within-bin variance of $\ln v$ falls
from $0.18$ in the smallest fifth to $0.10$ and rises again to $0.17$ in the largest, estimation noise
dominating among small firms and floors among large ones.  Panels that include very small firms, as in
Ref.~\cite{fontanelli2025}, should therefore show ratios close to or above $q$ unless short lives
are dropped, and ratios that fall below $q$ as the minimum life length grows.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The ratio curve of firm volatility bends below $q$ exactly when the dispersion of volatilities among firms
of equal size grows with size.  When a firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size,
this happens only if floors differ between firms of equal size; the noise of estimating a volatility from
a short life works the other way.  The criterion does not depend on a model.  Granular models sit at its
two ends: well-diversified firms give a rescaled volatility whose law does not depend on size, on the
boundary, and firms made of a few subunits stretch its upper tail so much that the ratios fall towards
$1$.  Firm data lie between, and so does the economy we build.  The corrections that MSB invoke to bring
a granular model between the limits would act through the same growth of $K_q$ with size, but they give
the length of a life, and the years after entry and before exit, no role beyond estimation noise, which
separates them from our mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions}).

In an economy of interacting firms the floors differ for two reasons.  A firm's floor contains the climb
after its entry and the collapse before its exit, spread over its life, and lives end at memoryless dips
of the field, so their lengths are broad.  And the degree of a firm's node sets the amplitude of its field,
which keeps the floors of the larger, better connected firms large against their noise and makes their
differences visible.  Removing the degree heterogeneity removes the bend, and keeping short lives reverses
it; one criterion accounts for the three.  None of this is put in by hand.  A granular model takes the
structure of firms and the independence of their shocks as given; here the floors, the life lengths that
spread them, entries and exits, and the co-movement of large firms all come from one dynamics of
interacting firms.

Two limits are clear.  The theory is a one-firm problem given three statistics of the field, but at finite
connectivity a Gaussian closure predicts neither the amplitude of the field's motion nor the fat tail of
its spread at equal degree, both set close to a marginal amplification; a closure that keeps the
differences between firms of equal degree, such as the cavity method solved by population dynamics, is
open.  And the model spreads the whole distribution of volatilities at fixed size, while in Compustat only
its upper tail spreads; the self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also caps firm size, so
the range is two decades rather than four and there is no Zipf law.  A dynamics that decouples the decline
of volatility from the size cap, and a theory of the co-movement of large firms, are the next steps.  The
tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions} do not depend on these limits: they ask whether firm data show the
dispersion, the role of life length and the response to trimming that the mechanism implies.

\section{Relative sizes and the standard model}
\label{app:relative}

Write $x_i=mS_i$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}.  By It\^o's rule, with $\bar f=\dot m/m$ the drift of the
mean firm,
\begin{align}
dS_i={}&S_i\big[F_i-\bar f\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i\nonumber\\
&-\frac{S_i}{N}\sum_j\sqrt{DS_j}\,dW_j+O(N^{-1})\,dt,\nonumber\\
F_i={}&1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j ,
\label{eq:app-rel}
\end{align}
and $\bar f=N^{-1}\sum_iS_iF_i$ is the value that keeps $\sum_iS_i=N$ between entries.  The third term, of order
$N^{-1/2}$, is the share of the aggregate noise that each firm carries.  Each entry adds $D$ to $\sum_iS_i$, i.e.\
$Dm$ to the economy, and multiplies every relative size by $1-D/N$; at a rate $N\nu$ of entries this is a drift
$-D\nu S_i$, so the growth rate of the mean firm is $\bar f=N^{-1}\sum_iS_iF_i+D\nu$, the value used in the field
throughout.  This is a replicator equation
with birth--death noise; with $\Phi_i=F_i+S_i-\bar f$ it is Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}.  If $\bar f$ self-averages to a constant
$f^*$ at large $N$, the substitution $S=ry$, $t'=rt$ with $r=1-f^*$ gives
\begin{equation}
dy_i=y_i\Big[1-y_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}y_j\Big]dt'+\sqrt{(D/r^2)\,y_i}\,dW_i' ,
\end{equation}
the GLV with demographic noise of Ref.~\cite{altieri2021} on the same graph, with noise $D/r^2$ and newborns
of size $D/r$.  The
stationary statistics of relative sizes are therefore those of the standard model with renormalised
noise; the normalisation adds the growth rate $f^*$ as an output, a common mode $\bar f(t)-f^*$ at
finite $N$, and observables measured relative to the mean firm.

\section{Heterogeneous mean-field theory and the deterministic stability line}
\label{app:hdmft}

\paragraph{Degree classes.}  Group the nodes into classes $p$ with fractions $\rho_p$ and degrees
$k_p$, and let $P_{pr}$ be the probability that a node of class $p$ links to one of class $r$, with
$k_p/N=\sum_r\rho_rP_{pr}$.  The uncorrelated choice $P_{pr}=k_pk_r/(NC)$ exceeds one for pairs of hubs
at $\alpha=2.5$; we use the soft-configuration kernel $P_{pr}=h_ph_r/(h_ph_r+C/N)$ with hidden degrees fixed
by the degree constraint \cite{patil2026}, and write $K_{pr}=N\rho_rP_{pr}/C$.

\paragraph{Effective process.}  For $C\to\infty$ the mean part of the interaction field on a node of
class $p$ tends to $\mu B_p$ and, by the dynamical cavity argument
\cite{bunin2017,galla2018,aguirrelopez}, the random part to a Gaussian process of covariance
$\sigma^2q_p(t,t')$, with
\begin{gather}
B_p(t)=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)],\nonumber\\
q_p(t,t')=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\label{eq:app-closure}
\end{gather}
At zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.  Each class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with field
$\Phi_p(t)=1-\bar f(t)+\mu B_p(t)+\sigma\eta_p(t)$, $\E[\eta_p(t)\eta_p(t')]=q_p(t,t')$, closed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} and by $\sum_p\rho_p\E[S_p]=1$, which fixes $\bar f$.  For the bilinear kernel the
closures factorise, $B_\kappa=\kappa\bar B$ and $q_\kappa=\kappa Q$, with $\kappa=k/C$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}: the field's mean falls linearly in $\kappa$ under competition and its amplitude grows as
$\sqrt\kappa$.

\paragraph{Stability line.}  Without noise, a relaxed composition has $S_p^*=\sigma\sqrt{q_p^*}
[\Delta_p+z]_+$ with $\Delta_p=(a+\mu B_p^*)/(\sigma\sqrt{q_p^*})$, $a=1-f^*$ and $z$ standard Gaussian.  With the
truncated moments $w_n(\Delta)=\int_{-\Delta}^\infty(\Delta+z)^n\phi(z)\,dz$, the survival fraction is
$\varphi_p=w_0(\Delta_p)$.  A persistent perturbation of variance $Y_r$ in class $r$ produces in class $p$ a
field of variance $\sigma^2K_{pr}Y_r$, which the survivors convert into $Y_p=\sigma^2\varphi_p\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r$; the
fixed point loses stability when the Perron root of $\sigma^2\varphi_pK_{pr}$ reaches one.  On a regular graph
this gives $\sigma_c=\sqrt2$ at zero reciprocity, independent of $\mu$, the onset of the fluctuating
phase of the GLV \cite{roy2020,mallmin2024}.  For the bilinear kernel the operator has rank one, and
the threshold is
\begin{gather}
\sigma_c^{-2}=\big\langle\kappa^2\PhiN(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle,\qquad \Delta_\kappa=A\kappa^{-1/2}+M\kappa^{1/2},\nonumber\\
\big\langle\kappa^2\Delta_\kappa w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle=0,\qquad M=\mu\big\langle\kappa^{3/2}w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle .
\label{eq:app-sigmac}
\end{gather}
Writing $\kappa=1+\epsilon$ and expanding to second order in $\epsilon$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmac}.  At this
order $\mu$ enters only through $\mu^2$: the terms odd in $\mu$, which lower or raise the survival of the hubs,
cancel against the shift of the mean of $\Delta_\kappa$, and what remains is the variance $\mu^2\Var\kappa/(2\pi)$ of
$\Delta_\kappa$ across classes.  Since $\Delta w_1(\Delta)$ is convex at $\Delta=0$ while $w_0$ has no curvature there,
this spread lowers the mean of $\Delta_\kappa$ and with it the Perron root.  Beyond first order the dependence is
not even: at $\alpha=3.5$ the exact relation gives $\sigma_c=1.32$ at $\mu=1$ and $1.29$ at $\mu=-1$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:app-sigmac} agrees with the 60-class HDMFT to $0.002$ at $\alpha=6$ and $0.03$ at $\alpha=3.5$.  The
zero-growth line, obtained by imposing $a=1$, shifts in $\mu$ with the degree variance by $c(\sigma)\Var\kappa$, with
$c$ running from $0$ as $\sigma\to0$ to $5/4-\pi/2\simeq-0.32$ as $\sigma\to\sqrt2$.  There the regular-graph lines meet, at
$\mu=-1$, and the zero-growth line $\mu=\Delta/w_1(\Delta)-1$, $\sigma^2w_2(\Delta)=1$ has slope $d\mu/d\sigma=-\pi/\sqrt2$; converted to a
shift in $\sigma$ its shift is $0.14\Var\kappa$, about a quarter of the shift $(1-1/(2\pi))\Var\kappa/\sqrt2=0.59\Var\kappa$

\section{Demographic noise at constant field}
\label{app:constant}

\paragraph{Renewal law.}  With noise and turnover, a node whose field is constant is a renewal
process: its successive lives are independent and identically distributed.  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm}
at constant $\Phi=h$ is a one-dimensional diffusion with scale function $s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,du$, where
$s'(u)=\exp[-(2/D)(hu-u^2/2)]$, and by the renewal--reward theorem the fraction of time the node spends
at size $x$ is
\begin{gather}
p(x\mid h)=\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)\,T(D)},\nonumber\\
T(D)=\int_0^\infty\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)}\,dx,
\label{eq:renewal}
\end{gather}
with turnover rate $1/T(D)$.  The law has two parts: a plateau $\propto x^{-1}e^{-(x-h)^2/D}$, Gaussian of
variance $D/2$ around $h$ for $h\gg\sqrt D$, and below $D$ a nearly flat density of newborns drifting to exit.  Establishment probabilities and
exit times follow from the same quadratures and agree with direct simulation to $3\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).

\paragraph{Instability line with noise.}  Replacing the clipped Gaussian by the renewal law in the
static self-consistency gives the mean sizes $\langle S\rangle_h$ and the susceptibility
$\chi=\partial\langle S\rangle_h/\partial h$; the static state is stable while the Perron root of
$\sigma^2{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K$ is below one.  Noise smooths $\chi$ and raises the line almost linearly with
$D$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaselines}): from $1.460$ to $1.577$ at $\alpha=2.5$ and from $1.399$ to $1.551$ at
$\alpha=6$ between $D=0.003$ and $0.3$; at $D=0.003$ it agrees with the deterministic line to $0.002$
($1.399$ against $1.400$ at $\alpha=6$, $1.413$ against $\sqrt2$ on the complete graph).  The closure
treats each node's field as frozen, so it is controlled only below the line; in simulations at $\alpha=6$ and
$D=0.1$, slow collective fluctuations of the composition set in between $\sigma=1.40$ and $1.50$, around the
predicted $1.451$, as a smooth crossover rather than a sharp transition (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  The same theory
predicts the turnover rate to $1\%$ below $\sigma_c$ and to about $2\%$ above it, where it is no longer
controlled ($\alpha=6$; Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  Turnover is set by
the nodes with $h<0$, where a newborn's life hardly depends on $D$, and is nearly independent of the
noise.  Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows the phase diagram in the plane of the mean interaction and the
disorder.  On the reference graph competition raises $\sigma_c$ strongly, from $0.83$ at $\mu=0$ to $1.68$ at
$\mu=-2.5$, because its degrees are the most spread, and with them the mean field across classes; on the
complete graph the line does not depend on $\mu$, and at $\alpha=6$ it barely does.  Under strong
competition the zero-growth line is almost the same on the three graphs, within $0.05$ for $\mu\le-1.25$;
under weak competition it is lower on the reference graph ($0.50$ against $0.63$ and $0.64$ at $\mu=0$).  The reference point, $\sigma=1.70$ against $\sigma_c(0.1)=1.497$, lies in the fluctuating phase, close to
the static estimate of the zero-growth line ($1.72$ at $D=0.1$), which is not controlled there.  In
simulation the zero-growth line nevertheless lies within $0.04$ of the static estimate for $\mu$ from $-0.5$ to $-2.5$, and
the region where the facts of Sec.~\ref{sec:data} hold is a band along it (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).

\paragraph{Quadratures.}  At constant $\Phi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a diffusion with drift $S(\Phi-S)$ and diffusion
coefficient $DS$, absorbed at $0$.  Its scale density is $s'(y)=\exp[-(2/D)(\Phi y-y^2/2)]$, its scale function
$s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,du$ and its speed density $m(y)=2/(Dy\,s'(y))$ \cite{karlintaylor1981}.  A newborn reaches its plateau before exiting with
probability
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm est}=\frac{\int_0^Ds'(y)\,dy}{\int_0^\Phi s'(y)\,dy}\qquad(\Phi>D),
\end{equation}
and the mean time to exit from $S_0$ is
\begin{equation}
T(S_0)=2\int_0^{S_0}s'(y)\int_y^\infty\frac{dz}{Dz\,s'(z)}\,dy .
\end{equation}
For $-\Phi\gg\sqrt D$, $T$ is of order $1/|\Phi|$ and, for a newborn, nearly independent of $D$: near zero the
logistic term is negligible and the Feller survival probability from $S_0=D$ does not contain $D$ ($T=2.32$,
$2.16$, $1.92$ at $\Phi=-0.5$ and $D=0.01$, $0.1$, $0.3$).  At $\Phi=0$ the logistic term cuts the Feller tail
$2/t$ off at $t\sim D^{-1/2}$, and $T$ grows as $\ln(1/D)$ ($6.05$, $3.84$, $2.88$).  For
$\Phi>0$ it grows as $e^{\Phi^2/D}$, so an established firm exits through dips of its field rather than
through noise.  Table~\ref{tab:constant} compares the quadratures with direct simulation.  On the
plateau, linearising at $S=\Phi$ gives an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process in $\ln S$ with rate $\Phi$, $\Var
S=D/2$ and $\Var g=(D/\Phi^2)(1-e^{-\Phi\Delta t})$, accurate for $\Phi\gtrsim1$.

\paragraph{Derivation of the renewal law.}  With turnover the lives at a node of constant field are independent and
identically distributed.  By the renewal--reward theorem the fraction of time spent near $x$ is the
expected time one life spends there, the Green's function $G(D,x)=s(\min(x,D))\,m(x)$, divided by the
mean life $T(D)=\int_0^\infty G(D,x)\,dx$; this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:renewal}.  Against direct
simulation of one node at $D=0.1$ and $\Phi=-0.5,\dots,1$ it gives $\langle S\rangle$, $\langle S^2\rangle$ and the
turnover to within $0.5\%$ (\texttt{notebooks/02\_phase\_diagram.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Static closure and phase lines.}  When the field is static each node follows the renewal
law at its $h=a+\mu B_p+\sigma\sqrt{q_p}z$, and the self-consistency reads
\begin{gather}
B_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r},\qquad q_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r}^2,\nonumber\\
\sum_p\rho_p\E\langle S\rangle_{h_p}=1,
\end{gather}
with $\E$ the Gaussian average over $z$.  Counting the temporal fluctuations of $S$ in $q$ as if
quenched moves the lines by less than $0.015$.  Stability is the Perron condition with $\E\chi^2$, where
$\chi=\partial\langle S\rangle_h/\partial h$, in place of the survival fraction (without noise $\chi$ is the
survival step and $\E\chi^2=\varphi$).  Table~\ref{tab:phase} tests the
turnover against simulation.  The share of the field variance that moves in time grows from $0.025$
at $\sigma=1.2$ to $0.138$ at $\sigma=1.7$; the static closure underestimates it below $\sigma_c$, by a factor
of about $2$ at $\sigma=1.2$ and $5$ at $\sigma_c$, because it omits the collective amplification of slow field
fluctuations.  The two-time closure includes it, but at $C=200$ only in part.

\paragraph{Onset in simulation.}  Since the closure is least accurate near $\sigma_c$, we locate the onset
directly.  For each $\sigma$ we run two replicas on each of two graphs ($\alpha=6$, $\mu=-1.76$, $D=0.1$,
$N=4000$, $C=100$, $T=4200$, the first $200$ discarded) with the same couplings and different initial sizes and
noise, and compare their time-averaged sizes over the last $W$ units of time,
$d(W)=\overline{(\bar S_i^A-\bar S_i^B)^2}/\Var_i\bar S_i$.  Fast noise alone gives $d\propto1/W$ with $Wd$ set by its
correlation time; slow collective motion raises $Wd$, and distinct attractors would keep $d$ finite.  For
$W=125$ to $2000$, $Wd\simeq0.6$ at $\sigma=1.30$ and $1.35$, $0.7$--$1.1$ at $1.40$, $1.3$--$2.9$ at $1.45$, $1.2$--$4.6$ at
$1.50$ and $5$--$8$ at $1.60$ and $1.70$.  The slow component sets in between $1.40$ and $1.50$, steepest
between $1.45$ and $1.50$, in agreement with the predicted $\sigma_c=1.451$.  Up to $\sigma=1.7$, $d$ keeps falling
roughly as $1/W$: the replicas agree on average, and what grows is the correlation time of the
composition, not the number of attractors (\texttt{scripts/phase\_onset.py}).

\section{Two-time closure}
\label{app:closure}

\paragraph{Effective process.}  Each degree class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with a Gaussian field
closed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} (Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}); at zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.

\paragraph{Solution.}  At the reference point we solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} in the stationary state by
sampling the effective process \cite{eissfeller1992,roy2019} (\texttt{scripts/two\_time\_dmft.py}), with $31$
classes, of equal population in the bulk and of equal degree mass in the tail; each sampled node carries
its own degree, its field is scaled by $k/k_p$, and neighbour averages are weighted by degree.  Evaluated
with the measured sizes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} gives the temporal and frozen parts of the measured
field to within $4\%$ and $6\%$.  The self-consistent solution reproduces the mean field to within the
difference between the two economies, but its temporal amplitude is $0.71$--$0.81$ of the measured one in
every class, and its correlation is gone by $\tau=16$, where the measured one is still $0.05$--$0.13$.  Its
life panel has $\zeta_1=0.34$ and ratios $1.85$, $2.53$, $3.07$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$.

\paragraph{Why it falls short.}  At $C=200$ a few strongly fluctuating neighbours dominate the temporal
field of a node, so its amplitude varies between nodes of equal degree, with a coefficient of variation of
$0.24$--$0.36$; the closure replaces this spread by its mean.  Started from the measured state of one
economy, one step of the iteration returns $0.955\pm0.01$ of the temporal field variance (three replicas);
driving each node instead with the mean field and temporal amplitude of a measured node of its class
returns $1.02\pm0.03$.  A loss of $4.5\%$ per step that halves the self-consistent temporal variance means a
loop gain of about $0.9$: the temporal field is set close to marginal amplification.

\paragraph{With measured inputs.}  Taking the temporal field from simulation, an amplitude $2.16\sqrt\kappa$
and an exponential correlation with the $1/e$ time of the class-weighted measured correlation, $4.5$, and
solving for the rest, the panel has $\zeta_1=0.26$ and ratios $1.85$, $2.54$, $3.08$; the volatility of the
smallest and largest bins is $0.71$ and $0.37$, against $0.70$ and $0.32$.  The frozen spread is then
$12$--$15\%$ low.  Fixing it at its measured $2.66\sqrt\kappa$ leaves $\zeta_1$ at $0.26$ but moves the ratios
only to $1.83$, $2.49$, $2.98$, against $1.81$, $2.42$, $2.86$ with the measured mean fields reshuffled within
degree and $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$ with each node's own (Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  The bend depends on the shape
of the spread of mean fields at equal degree, whose fat tail a Gaussian closure cannot produce.

\section{The identity and the floor}
\label{app:multiscaling}

\paragraph{The identity with a composition term.}  Let $p(u\mid\Sb)$ be the law of the floor at fixed size
and $E_q(\Sb)=\int p(u\mid\Sb)\,(u+B(\Sb))^{q/2}\,du$, so that $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=E_q\,\E[\epsilon^q]$.  Then
\begin{gather}
\zeta_q=-\frac{d\ln E_q}{d\ln\Sb}=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\nonumber\\
c_q=\frac{1}{E_q}\int\frac{\partial p(u\mid\Sb)}{\partial\ln\Sb}\,(u+B)^{q/2}\,du .
\label{eq:app-identity}
\end{gather}
The composition term vanishes if the law of the floor does not depend on size.  On the network it does not
vanish: larger lives have larger degrees on average, and the floor grows with the degree, so $c_q>0$ and
the decline is slower than the noise share alone implies.  If the law of $\ln u$ only shifts with size, by
$\delta(\Sb)$, then $c_q=(q/2)(1-\langle w\rangle_q)\,d\delta/d\ln\Sb$: a rising floor flattens the decline and,
since $\langle w\rangle_q$ falls with $q$, adds to the bend.  If the floor is the same for all firms of a given
size, $E_q=(u+B)^{q/2}$ and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$ exactly, whatever $c_q$.

\paragraph{Monotonicity of $\langle w\rangle_q$.}  With $Y=u+B$, $\langle w\rangle_q=B\exp[h(q-2)-h(q)]$, where
$h(q)=\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is the cumulant generating function of $\tfrac12\ln Y$ evaluated at $q$ and therefore
convex.  Convexity makes $h(q)-h(q-2)$ non-decreasing, so $\langle w\rangle_q$ is non-increasing in $q$, strictly
unless $Y$ is degenerate at fixed size.

\paragraph{Floor make-up.}  The core of a life runs from the first to the last sample at which the
noise-free size is at least half its median.  For a climb in a constant field $h$, $u=S/h$ obeys
$\dot u=hu(1-u)$ and $d\ln S/dt=h(1-u)$, so $\int(d\ln S/dt)^2dt=h[\ln(h/S_0)-1+S_0/h]$.  For a collapse in a
field $-\varphi$, $d\ln S/dt=-(\varphi+S)$ and $\int(\varphi+S)^2dt=\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h-S_{\rm end}$.  Times
$\Delta t$ these are $E_{\rm in}$ and $E_{\rm out}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.  On the $175\,171$ complete lives with at
least $20$ growth rates, the exit formula follows the measured sum life by life (log-correlation
$0.85$--$0.91$ by fifth) and lies above it by $6$--$40\%$; the entry formula has the right scale but
correlates less (log-correlation $0.5$), because a firm climbs in a field that moves.

\paragraph{Life lengths.}  For $dS=r(t)S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW$ the probability of extinction by time $t$ is
$\exp[-2S_0/(D\int_0^te^{-R(s)}ds)]$, $R=\int_0^tr$, from the Laplace transform of this Feller diffusion;
with $S_{\rm det}=S_0e^R$ this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill}.  At the bottom of a dip, where $\Phi=S_{\min}$, a saddle
point gives $J\simeq\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}/S_{\min}$: a dip kills when it brings the firm below a size of order $D$
times its recovery time.  Binned by the predicted probability, the observed kill fractions are $0.015$ at
$0.027$, $0.102$ at $0.147$, $0.418$ at $0.450$, $0.894$ at $0.853$ and $0.986$ at $0.974$, and the log-likelihood per
dip is $-0.175$ against $-0.591$ for a constant kill rate.  Rice's rate, from each node's mean, standard
deviation and lag-$\Delta t$ autocorrelation of the field, lies $1$--$13\%$ above the observed rate in every
decile of $u$ but the lowest.  Among complete lives the coefficient of variation of $n-20$ is $1.05$--$1.22$
in every decile of $u$ but the highest, where the window cuts the longest lives.

\paragraph{A fitted floor.}  An alternative test, without the noise-free integration, fits

the mean degree gives exactly $2$, $3$, $4$ (\texttt{notebooks/05\_multiscaling.ipynb}).  The
fit assigns all of the top bins' variance to the floor ($B_j\to0$), so its partition differs from the
physical one of Table~\ref{tab:fifths}.

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{The year.}  We sample every $0.5$ time units.  The lag-one autocorrelation of growth within
lives is $+0.13$, $+0.18$ and $0.00$ at steps $0.5$, $1$ and $2$; a step of $2$ would remove it but leaves
too few growth rates per life for the panel.  In the two reference economies, lives with at least five
growth rates give $\zeta_q=0.208$, $0.403$, $0.572$, $0.711$ (ratios $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$) over $2.0$
decades at step $0.5$, and $\zeta_q=0.154$, $0.259$, $0.316$, $0.333$ (ratios $1.68$, $2.06$, $2.16$) over
$1.4$ decades at step $2$: volatility per step rises by a factor $1.1$ in the smallest size bin and $2.2$
in the largest, so the decline flattens, and the ratio curve bends further below $q$.  For lives with
at least $20$ growth rates the change is smaller ($\zeta_1=0.253$ and $0.247$, ratios $1.79$, $2.37$,
$2.80$ and $1.75$, $2.28$, $2.62$, although at step $2$ these lives span only $0.9$ decades).  The bend
below $q$ survives the coarser year; the level of $\zeta_1$ for short lives does not
(\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Minimum life length.}  MSB keep every firm with at least two growth rates; their firms have $46$ on
average, and their results do not change for firms with at least $20$.  Their growth rates are annual
differences rolled over quarters, $g_t=\ln S_{t+4}-\ln S_t$, so $46$ of them span about twelve years and $20$
about six, or five non-overlapping annual growth rates.  The model's lives are much shorter: $38\%$ of the
lives with at least two growth rates have exactly two ($N=16000$).  We therefore compare with data at
$n_{\min}=5$ and test the theory at $n_{\min}=20$, where each life is long enough to estimate its floor.  In
the production run $\zeta_q=0.180$, $0.434$, $0.708$, $0.965$ (ratios $2.41$, $3.94$, $5.36$) for $n_{\min}=2$, $0.209$,
$0.400$, $0.561$, $0.685$ ($1.92$, $2.69$, $3.28$) for $n_{\min}=5$, $0.218$, $0.388$, $0.508$, $0.582$ ($1.78$, $2.33$,
$2.67$) for $n_{\min}=10$ and $0.254$, $0.452$, $0.598$, $0.701$ ($1.78$, $2.35$, $2.75$) for $n_{\min}=20$, over $2.1$,
$2.0$, $1.8$ and $1.4$ decades: the ratios lie above $q$ for $n_{\min}=2$ and below from $n_{\min}=5$, as the
estimation noise of short lives requires (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), and the range shrinks because long lives
do not occur at the newborn sizes.

\paragraph{The tail index $b$.}  For lives with at least two growth rates the model gives
$b=4.39$--$4.44$ at every parameter point, close to the Compustat value $4.6$.  A null with iid Laplace
growth rates, no differences between firms and the model's distribution of life lengths gives
$4.34$, so the agreement is an effect of short lives.  For longer lives $b$ grows: $5.2$, $5.5$ and $6.6$ in the production
run for $n_{\min}=5$, $10$ and $20$ ($5.7$ and $6.9$ at $N=16000$ for $10$ and $20$), against $9.3$ and $12.6$ for the Laplace null and $4.6$ in
Compustat for both samples (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Volatility estimator.}  With the standard deviation instead of $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ times the mean
absolute deviation, $\zeta_1$ is $0.03$ lower and the ratios are $0.03$--$0.3$ higher (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb},
rows SD; production run: $\zeta_1=0.178$, ratios $1.95$, $2.81$, $3.54$ at $n_{\min}=5$).

\paragraph{Rescaled growth.}  MSB rescale each growth rate by its firm's leave-one-out mean and volatility,
$\hat g_t=(g_t-\bar g_{-t})/\sigma_{-t}$, so that a large rate does not shrink its own scale, and find densities
that hardly depend on size \cite{moran2024}.  We rescale the lives with at least five growth rates of the two
reference economies the same way (Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}).  Compustat's densities, kernel estimates in $25$ size
bins, are read from the vector paths of MSB's figures (\texttt{scripts/msb\_curves.py}); we use the plotted
curves because we could not reconcile the fitted parameters of their Table~1 with the plotted fit.  The
curves stop at a density of $10^{-4}$ on $|z|\le8$, so we compare the excess kurtosis on that window.  By fifth
of size the model gives $3.0$, $3.7$, $4.3$, $4.8$, $5.7$ and Compustat $4.2$, $5.6$, $5.1$, $5.4$, $5.7$
($3.5$--$4.2$ in its three smallest bins).  A Gaussian null with the same life lengths gives $2.5$, $2.4$, $1.6$,
$0.4$, $0.1$: among small firms most of the model's kurtosis is the rescaling of short lives.  The full fourth
moments are dominated by rare events beyond $|z|=8$, outside the range of the Compustat curves; rescaling
instead by each life's own volatility, lives with at least $10$ growth rates give $1.3$ to $23$ by fifth in the
production run (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

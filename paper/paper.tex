\title{Interaction heterogeneity and the multiscaling of firm growth volatility}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, and the size dependence of their volatility is multiscaling,
as Moran, Secchi and Bouchaud showed for Compustat firms.  Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of
independent parts, cannot produce this multiscaling together with the other regularities of firm growth.  Here
we ask whether interactions between firms can.  We study an economy of interacting firms with demographic noise
and scale-invariant dynamics.  Tuned to the observed decline of mean volatility, the model reproduces the multiscaling, the
tent-shaped distribution of growth rates and the fat tails of rescaled growth among large firms.  It does not
reproduce the range of sizes over which volatility falls, nor Zipf's law of firm sizes.  A theory of one firm in its
moving field explains the decline: self-limitation makes a firm a low-pass filter of its market, with a cutoff equal
to its size, and the panel of firm lives averages this law.  The bend comes from the spread of mean fields among
firms of equal degree.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but much more erratically than a sum of independent
parts would.  Let $v$ be the volatility of a firm's growth rate and $\Sb$ its mean size over its life.  In
Compustat the conditional moments scale as
\begin{equation}
\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q},
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
with $\zeta_q=0.20(1)$, $0.39(2)$, $0.51(3)$, $0.58(4)$ for $q=1,\dots,4$, over four orders of magnitude of size
\cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002,moran2024}.  The ratios $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.95$, $2.55$, $2.90$ lie
below $q$ \cite{moran2024}.

The bend has a simple meaning.  If a firm's volatility were a function of its size times a random factor
whose law does not depend on size, the ratios would be exactly $q$.  Ratios below $q$ therefore say that firms
of equal size differ more in volatility the larger they are.

Three further facts come with the bend.  Growth rates have tent-shaped tails, and they stay fat-tailed when
each firm's growth is rescaled by its own volatility, even among the largest firms \cite{moran2024}.  The
growth of a firm correlates more with the aggregate, and with firms of similar size, the larger the firm
\cite{moran2024}.  And firm sizes follow Zipf's law \cite{axtell2001}.

Granular models make a firm a sum of subunits with independent shocks \cite{wyartbouchaud2003,gabaix2011}.
They give $\zeta_1$ close to $0.2$ for suitable subunit sizes \cite{sutton2002}.  Moran, Secchi and Bouchaud
(MSB) tested them against the other facts \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms give $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$,
and firms made of a few subunits give ratios close to $1$; the data lie between.  The rescaled growth of a
granular firm becomes Gaussian, and independent parts do not co-move.  MSB conclude that granular mechanisms
alone cannot account for the data, and point to correlations that grow with size as the missing ingredient
\cite{moran2024,herskovic2020}.  Other models get parts of the picture: customer--supplier networks make
volatilities co-move \cite{herskovic2020}, proportional growth of products gives tent-shaped growth
\cite{fu2005}, and agent-based models of firm formation give Zipf's law \cite{axtell2016}.

Firms interact: they compete for the same customers, and they buy from and sell to each other
\cite{acemoglu2012,carvalho2019}.  Interactions make firms co-move.  They keep the environment of a firm
changing, so even a large firm cannot average its volatility away.  And they make firms enter and exit.  We
therefore ask whether an economy of interacting firms, with nothing about a single firm put in by hand, can
produce the facts, and where it fails.  We take the simplest such dynamics, the generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV)
model, whose strongly interacting phase keeps fluctuating \cite{roy2020,mallmin2024,arnouxbunin2024}.  Firms grow,
limit themselves, and gain or lose from the firms they are linked to on a scale-free network.  We add demographic
noise and turnover: a firm that reaches zero exits, and a newborn takes its place.

We find three things.  First, with one fitted exponent the model reproduces in kind the facts that rule out
granular models: a ratio curve below $q$, tent-shaped growth, and rescaled growth that stays fat-tailed among the
largest firms (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}).  Second, the decline has a simple cause: self-limitation makes a firm relax at
a rate equal to its size, so a firm is a low-pass filter of its market, and a larger firm is a stiffer one
(Sec.~\ref{sec:filter}).  Third, the panel on which the facts are measured shapes them.  Against current size the
model's volatility falls with a slope of $0.58$, against the mean size of a life with $\zeta_1=0.19$; and the bend
comes from the spread of mean fields among firms of equal degree, which the life protocol turns into a dispersion
of volatilities that grows with size (Sec.~\ref{sec:panel}).  Section~\ref{sec:fails} explains where the model
fails, and Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the open problems.

\section{An economy of interacting firms}
\label{sec:economy}

\subsection{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Equation.}  We consider $N$ firms with sizes $x_i$, and $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ is the mean firm.  The firms
sit on the nodes of a configuration-model graph with degree law $p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and mean degree $C$.
Their sizes evolve as
\begin{equation}
dx_i=x_i\Big[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\sum_jJ_{ij}\frac{x_j}{m}\Big]dt+\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}\;dW_i ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with independent Wiener processes $W_i$.  The couplings are $J_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$, with
$A$ the adjacency matrix and $z_{ij}$ independent standard Gaussians; $z_{ij}$ and $z_{ji}$ are uncorrelated.  A
firm that reaches zero exits, and a newborn of size $Dm$ takes its place on the same node.  The model has five
parameters: $\mu$, $\sigma$, $\alpha$, $C$ and $D$.

\paragraph{Reading the equation.}  The bracket is the growth rate of firm $i$.  The $1$ is the growth rate of a
firm alone.  The term $-x_i/m$ is self-limitation: a firm slows down as it saturates its own market.  The sum is
the effect of the linked firms.  A negative $J_{ij}$ means that firm $j$ takes growth from firm $i$, as a
competitor does; a positive one means that it feeds firm $i$, as a customer or a supplier does.  With $\mu<0$,
competition dominates on average.

The last term is demographic noise, as for gains and losses of indivisible pieces of business of size $Dm$.  It
adds $Dm/x_i$ per unit time to the variance of the growth rate, so it matters only for small firms.  It is the
only granular, $1/\text{size}$ effect in the model.  Every term is homogeneous of degree one in the sizes, so the
economy has no preferred scale.

\paragraph{A logistic unit in a moving field.}  In relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$,
\begin{equation}
dS_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i+O(N^{-1/2}),
\label{eq:onefirm}
\end{equation}
with the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\bar f(t),
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
where $\bar f$ is the growth rate of the mean firm, entry dilution included (Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).
Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a logistic equation whose carrying capacity moves in time.  If the field stays at
$\Phi>0$, the firm relaxes to the plateau $x_i=m\Phi$.  If $\Phi<0$, the firm shrinks until it exits.  The field is
the rate at which a firm would grow, relative to the mean firm, if it were very small; two firms differ only
through their fields, and the neighbours of a firm set its field.

A rescaling by $1-\bar f$ maps the relative sizes onto a standard GLV model on the same graph
(Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  On the complete graph it is the GLV model with demographic noise
\cite{altieri2021}, with a newborn in place of each firm that exits.  With immigration in place of the noise, the
strongly interacting phase of that model shows the chaotic turnover of Refs.~\cite{roy2020,mallmin2024}.

\subsection{Phase diagram}
\label{sec:phase}

\paragraph{Two regimes.}  For weak interactions the economy relaxes: every field settles to a constant, and only
the demographic noise moves sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}).  For strong interactions the fields keep moving, because
the firms that make them keep rising and falling.  The facts need the second regime, because the volatility of the
larger firms comes from the motion of their field.  The instability line $\sigma_c(\mu)$ separates the two
regimes, and the zero-growth line separates economies whose mean firm grows from those where it shrinks.

\paragraph{Theory and test.}  For $C\to\infty$, heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory (HDMFT) replaces the
network by one process per degree class \cite{aguirrelopez,park2024,poley2025,patil2026}.  A node of relative
degree $\kappa=k/C$ feels
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)=1-\bar f+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
\label{eq:hdmft}
\end{equation}
with $\bar B$ the degree-weighted mean size and $\xi$ a Gaussian process (Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}).  Under
competition a better connected firm has a lower mean field, because it competes with more firms, and a field of
larger amplitude, because it sums more random terms.  In the relaxing regime $\xi$ is nearly static
(Table~\ref{tab:phase}), and each node follows the exact stationary law of one firm at constant field, with noise
and turnover (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  This static solution loses stability at $\sigma_c(\mu)$.  Without
noise the line is known \cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026}; noise raises it almost linearly in $D$, as on the
complete graph \cite{altieri2021}.  In simulation the onset of the fluctuating regime follows the predicted line,
with nothing fitted (Fig.~\ref{fig:instability}), and the predicted zero-growth line is close to the simulated one
(Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}a).  Our reference point is $\alpha=2.5$, $\sigma=1.70$, $D=0.1$, $C=200$, $\mu=-1.76$.  It
lies in the fluctuating phase ($\sigma_c=1.497$), close to the line on which the mean firm stops growing
($\sigma=1.72$ in the static estimate).

\subsection{One firm in its field}
\label{sec:onefirm}

\paragraph{The field can be taken as given.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} treats the field as an external input,
which assumes that a firm's own noise does not feed back on it.  We test this by driving independent firms, with
fresh noise, by the stored fields of the reference economies.  Their life panels agree with the full model to
$0.01$ in $\zeta_1$ and $0.06$ in the ratios at every minimum life length (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}), so the
size--volatility statistics are a one-firm problem: they follow from one firm and the statistics of its field.

\paragraph{Two statistics of the field.}  Around its mean $h$, the field of a node moves with a temporal amplitude
$f$ and a correlation $\rho(t)$ that is nearly the same for every node; one year after a given time it is still
$0.941$ on the power-law graph and $0.957$ on the regular graph.  Hubs have a lower mean field and a larger
amplitude, and among nodes of equal degree the mean field has a spread close to Gaussian (Appendix~\ref{app:field}).
The amplitude hardly depends on a firm's current size, as $S^{0.10}$ on the power-law graph and $S^{0.02}$ on the
regular one.  HDMFT, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, gives the form of these statistics but at $C=200$ gets the amplitude
$20$--$30\%$ low, because a few neighbours dominate the field of a firm, so we take them from simulation.

\section{The model against the MSB observables}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  We integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} in relative form and record sizes every $\Delta t=0.5$,
which we take as one year (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  A \emph{life} runs from a birth to the next exit at the
same node.  Lives cut by the observation window are kept, as in MSB's unbalanced panel.  A life with $n$ growth
rates $g_t=\ln S_{t+1}-\ln S_t$ has volatility and size
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|g_t-\bar g\big|,\qquad
\Sb=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}S_t,
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
the factor making $v$ the standard deviation for Gaussian growth.  Exponents are least-squares slopes of
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ against $\ln\Sb$ over $25$ equal-count bins (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}b).

\paragraph{Samples.}  We compare with data on lives with at least $n_{\min}=5$ growth rates, the cut that matches MSB's
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  The comparison uses seven economies with $N=32000$.  An eighth is excluded,
because one firm runs away to hundreds of times the mean firm and drives the aggregate
(Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  The theory tests use lives with at least $20$ growth rates, long enough to
measure each life's own field, in two economies with $N=16000$.

\paragraph{What is fitted.}  One fact is fitted: we place the reference point at the depth that gives
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$.  The other entries of Table~\ref{tab:facts} are outputs.

\paragraph{Where the facts hold.}  The reference point is one point of a band (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).  Near the
zero-growth line, $\zeta_1$ and the ratio curve depend on a single variable, the depth $\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu)$ of the
economy in the fluctuating phase (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}b): the deeper the economy, the more its field moves and the
flatter the decline.  Exponents $\zeta_1=0.15$--$0.25$ come from a band about $0.07$ wide in $\sigma$, which
contains economies that compete moderately and grow slowly (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).  Across this band the
ratio curve stays below $q$, but not far enough: $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ lies between about $3.0$ and $3.5$, above
Compustat's $2.90$, and reaches $2.90$ only deeper in the fluctuating phase, where $\zeta_1$ has fallen to about
$0.13$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}c and Table~\ref{tab:robust}).

\paragraph{What works.}  Beyond the fitted $\zeta_1$, the model reproduces three qualitative facts.  The decline
of volatility is monotone, and the ratio curve lies below $q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}a--c).  Growth rates are
tent-shaped, with exponential tails (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}e and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  And rescaled by
each firm's own volatility, growth rates stay fat-tailed even among the largest firms, where granular models
predict Gaussian tails \cite{moran2024}; in the largest fifth their excess kurtosis matches Compustat's
(Table~\ref{tab:facts}).

\paragraph{What works in part.}  Three facts hold only with a qualification.  The curve bends less than in
Compustat, because the higher exponents are too large ($\zeta_4=0.685$ against $0.58(4)$), and the bend needs a
minimum life length: with all lives the ratios lie above $q$, and they fall below $q$ only from $n_{\min}\simeq5$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), whereas MSB's results do not change with the cut.  The fat tails of rescaled growth
are right among large firms but too thin among small ones, where most of the kurtosis comes from the rescaling of
short lives (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  Multi-year persistence shows the same split: Compustat has it at all
sizes, the model only among the largest firms (Table~\ref{tab:facts}), because small firms are moved by
near-Gaussian, mean-reverting demographic noise and large firms by rare and persistent dips of their field.
Co-movement rises with size as in Compustat \cite{moran2024}, from a correlation of $0.11$ with the aggregate in the
smallest fifth to $0.29$ in the largest, but we do not count it as a result: the aggregate of the model is several
times too volatile, and a common shock alone would make this correlation rise with size.

\paragraph{What fails.}  The decline spans two orders of magnitude of size rather than four, and volatility falls by
a factor $2.8$ rather than $5.5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}a).  The size distribution has no Pareto tail
(Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}f): it falls off steeply above about ten times the mean firm.  The distribution of volatilities
at fixed size has the wrong shape: its tail is too thin, and thinner for longer lives (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}d), and
it spreads among the largest firms, where MSB find an approximate collapse.  With all lives the model does give
$b=4.4$, close to Compustat's $4.6$, but a null with iid Laplace growth gives $4.3$, so the match is an artefact of
short lives.  Lives are too short, with $38\%$ of the lives with at least two growth rates having exactly two,
whereas MSB's firms have $46$ growth rates on average, and the aggregate volatility, $0.088$ per year, is several
times too large and does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$ over the sizes we simulate (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).  Section~\ref{sec:fails}
explains these failures.

\section{Why volatility falls with size}
\label{sec:filter}

This section derives the decline of volatility with size from Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}, at a given size and over a
given horizon.  In this section and the next the volatility is the growth standard deviation and the lives have at
least $20$ growth rates, unless stated.

\paragraph{Linear response.}  In logs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} reads $d\ln S=[\Phi-S-D/(2S)]\,dt+\sqrt{D/S}\,dW$.
Write $\Phi=h+f\xi(t)$, with $\xi$ of zero mean, unit variance and correlation $\rho$, and follow the firm from its
current size $S$: $y(s)=\ln S(t+s)-\ln S(t)$.  To linear order, and once its mean drift is removed,
\begin{equation*}
dy=-S\,y\,ds+f\,\xi\,ds+\sqrt{D/S}\;dW .
\end{equation*}
Self-limitation pulls the log size back at a rate equal to the size, while the motion of the market and the firm's
own noise push it.  The growth $g_\tau$ over a horizon $\tau$ then has the variance (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction})
\begin{gather}
\Var\big[g_\tau\mid S\big]=f^2\,G(S,\tau)+\frac{D\,(1-e^{-S\tau})}{S^2},\label{eq:filter}\\
G(S,\tau)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\hat\rho(\omega)\,\frac{2\,(1-\cos\omega\tau)}{S^2+\omega^2},\nonumber
\end{gather}
with $\hat\rho$ the spectrum of the field's correlation.  We call Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} the filter law.

\paragraph{Two regimes.}  A small firm relaxes slowly and integrates its market: $G\to2\int_0^\tau(\tau-s)\rho(s)\,ds$,
which is about $\tau^2$ for a horizon shorter than the field's correlation time, so its volatility does not depend on
its size.  A large firm relaxes fast and follows its market: $G\to2[1-\rho(\tau)]/S^2$, and its volatility falls as
$1/S$.  The noise term goes the same way, from $D\tau/S$ to $D/S^2$.  The decline of volatility with size is the
crossover between the two regimes, near the size at which the firm relaxes as fast as its field changes.  It has a
single cause: self-limitation makes a larger firm stiffer.

\paragraph{Test.}  We condition on the current size, taking every firm alive at $t$ and still alive, in the same
life, at $t+\tau$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:filter}a,b).  With the mean squared amplitude of the firms of each size and the
measured correlation, and nothing fitted, Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} gives the volatility over one year to within $6\%$
from $S=0.3$ to $9$ on both graphs, and over two years to within $15\%$.  Between $S=0.3$ and $8$ the slope is $0.58$
measured against $0.59$ predicted on the power-law graph, and $0.67$ against $0.66$ on the regular graph.  The law
fails in two places: below $S\simeq0.15$, where the firms are newborns and the few that survive the horizon are a
selected group, and over four years on the power-law graph, where the firms move too far for linear response.

\paragraph{The horizon.}  The model has its own clock, set by the rate at which a firm relaxes and the time over
which its field changes, and the year is our choice of how to read it.  In Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} the horizon is a
variable of the law, through the motion of the field over $\tau$.  On the panel, a step of $2$ time units instead of
$0.5$ lowers $\zeta_1$ to $0.15$ for lives with at least five growth rates, and changes the serial correlation of
growth (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).

\section{What the panel of MSB measures}
\label{sec:panel}

\subsection{From current size to lives}
\label{sec:lives}

\paragraph{Averaging over a life.}  The panel of MSB conditions on the mean size of a life, not on the current size.
If each year of a life contributes the filter law at the size the firm had that year, a life with sizes
$S_1,\dots,S_n$ has
\begin{equation}
v^2\simeq\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big[f^2\,G(S_k,\Delta t)+\frac{D\,(1-e^{-S_k\Delta t})}{S_k^2}\Big],
\label{eq:lifeavg}
\end{equation}
set against $\Sb=\langle S\rangle$.  The law falls steeply with size, so the average is dominated by the years that a
life spends small, after its birth and in its dips, whereas its mean size is dominated by the years it spends large.
The curve against mean size is therefore much flatter than the curve against current size: the same economies give
$0.58$ at current size and $\zeta_1=0.19$ on the panel.

\paragraph{The conditioning matters.}  The match with MSB's exponent therefore depends on their protocol as much as on
the dynamics.  Whether firm data show the same gap between current size and mean size is a test of the model
(Appendix~\ref{app:tests}).

\paragraph{Sorting.}  The average needs the right amplitude.  Averaging the measured volatility at current size over
each life gives $\zeta_1=0.37$, too large for the smallest lives and too small for the largest.  Firms of equal
current size are not alike, and ranking the lives by their mean size sorts the firms by their own field.  A firm can
stay small for many years only if its field rarely dips, since lives end in dips (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}),
whereas lives that are large on average have rough fields: the amplitude of the field over a life rises from $0.70$
in the smallest decile of mean sizes to $2.24$ in the largest, while the node's amplitude barely changes with current
size.  With each life's own amplitude in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lifeavg}, the law gives $\zeta_1=0.217$ against $0.190$, and
the level of every fifth of sizes to within $8\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:filter}c); with the node's amplitude it gives
$0.241$.  On the regular graph, where amplitudes vary less, the two give $0.418$ and $0.461$ against $0.397$.

\paragraph{What the average misses.}  The average fails for short lives: with at least five growth rates it gives
$\zeta_1=0.25$ against $0.155$ on the power-law graph, and it overstates the smallest lives by $30\%$.  We use it for
the decline and the levels only; the higher moments have a different origin.

%======================================================================
\subsection{When the ratio curve bends}
\label{sec:criterion}

\paragraph{Dispersion at fixed size.}  The higher exponents say how the distribution of volatilities at fixed
size changes with size, since high moments weigh the most volatile firms of each size.  Write a firm's volatility as
its conditional mean times a rescaled volatility, $v=\bar v(\Sb)\,X$ with $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$, and let
$K_q(\Sb)=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$.  Then $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln\bar v+K_q$, and since $K_1=0$
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}),
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
$K_q$ is convex in $q$ and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so for $q>1$ it measures the dispersion of $X$, with more
weight on the upper tail as $q$ grows.  Equation~\eqref{eq:general} then says that the ratio curve lies below $q$
where this dispersion grows with size, on $q$ where it stays constant, and above $q$ where it shrinks.

For a lognormal $X$, with $\sigma^2(\Sb)$ the variance of $\ln X$, $K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$ and
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\,\frac{d\sigma^2}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general-lognormal}
\end{equation}
Granular models sit at the two limits \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms keep the law of $X$ fixed and give
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, while firms made of a few subunits stretch the upper tail of $X$ so fast that $\zeta_q$ stops
depending on $q$ for $q\ge2$ and the ratios fall towards $1$.  A model of the bend has to say why the dispersion
grows, and by how much.

In Compustat, $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$ is $-0.01$, $-0.09$ and $-0.22$ for $q=2$, $3$ and $4$: negative and falling with
$q$, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal} fits with a lognormal spread whose variance grows by $0.035$ per unit
of $\ln\Sb$.  The dispersion at fixed size therefore grows with size, so the collapse of the rescaled volatilities
that MSB report can only be approximate.  The exponents alone do not resolve whether the whole distribution
spreads or only its upper tail.

\paragraph{Where the bend comes from in the model.}  The filter law describes the typical firm of a size, and we do
not use it for the higher moments: the bend is a matter of how firms of equal size differ.  At current size they
differ little, and the ratio curve of the growth over one year stays close to $q$ ($1.97$, $2.87$, $3.67$ on the
power-law graph).  On the panel the curve bends ($1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for lives with at least $20$ growth rates,
with the panel's estimator).  One firm per node, driven by a Gaussian field with the node's mean, amplitude and
correlation, reproduces this bend ($1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$).  Setting every node's amplitude to the value of its
degree class leaves the bend ($1.81$, $2.42$, $2.86$), whereas setting the mean fields to their class value as well
removes it ($1.93$, $2.79$, $3.56$; Appendix~\ref{app:field}).  The bend therefore comes from the frozen spread of
mean fields among nodes of equal degree.  The life protocol turns this spread into a spread of volatilities at fixed
size that grows with size: the mean field decides how long a firm lives and how much of its field's motion its life
sees, and among the largest lives these differences are widest (Sec.~\ref{sec:lives}).  On the regular graph the
curve stays closer to $q$ ($1.93$, $2.78$, $3.53$ with the growth standard deviation).

\paragraph{Estimation noise.}  The ratio curve also depends on how precisely each life's volatility is measured, and
that precision depends on the length of the life.  The estimation variance of $\ln v$ falls as $1/n$ with the number
of growth rates, and for the shortest lives it is several times larger than this law gives
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  Where small firms live shorter, this variance falls with size and, by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}, pushes the ratio curve above $q$: with all lives the power-law graph gives ratios
$2.83$, $5.00$ and $7.30$.  Short lives can hide a bend or reverse it, so in firm data the ratio curve should be
reported with its minimum life length.

\paragraph{The size of the bend.}  In the model the dispersion at fixed size first falls with size, as short
lives drop out, and then rises steeply among the largest firms (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}a).  The net growth
gives $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1=-0.02$, $-0.07$ and $-0.15$, close to Compustat's, though the fourth moment grows somewhat
less; because the growth is weakest for $q=2$, the bend sits in the higher moments.

\section{Where the model fails, and why}
\label{sec:fails}

\subsection{The range of the decline}
\label{sec:range}

\paragraph{The ends of the range.}  The bins hold equal numbers of lives, so the range is the spread of life-mean
sizes between the smallest and the largest $4\%$ of lives.  The smallest lives are newborns that survive a few
years: in the production run the smallest bin sits at $\Sb=0.032$, that is $0.32D$.  The largest lives sit on high
plateaus, since a firm in a field $\Phi$ relaxes to $S=\Phi$, and the largest bin sits at $\Sb=3.2$.  The range is
therefore
\begin{equation}
R\simeq\log_{10}\frac{\Sb_{\rm top}}{0.3\,D},
\label{eq:range}
\end{equation}
with $\Sb_{\rm top}$ the mean size of the largest lives: two orders of magnitude at $D=0.1$.  Against Compustat's
bins, which run from about $10^{-3}$ to $10$ times the mean firm \cite{moran2024}, the model loses one and a half
orders of magnitude at the bottom, set by the newborn size, and half of one at the top, set by the thin tail of
sizes.  At equal depth, from $D=0.05$ to $0.2$, the smallest bin sits at $0.32$--$0.33\,D$ and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:range} gives the number of decades to within $0.05$.

\paragraph{The bottom of the decline.}  A small firm is far below its plateau, so its self-limitation is
negligible and $dS=\Phi S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW$.  In units of the newborn size, $s=S/D$, this reads
$ds=\Phi s\,dt+\sqrt s\,dW$, and $D$ drops out.  Growth rates are differences of logarithms, which the change of
units leaves alone, so the lower part of the decline depends on $\Sb/D$ alone.  At equal depth ($\mu=-1.25$,
$\sigma-\sigma_c=0.21$) the curves $\E[v\mid\Sb]$ for $D=0.05$, $0.1$ and $0.2$ coincide against $\Sb/D$ up to
$\Sb\simeq4D$, and the smallest lives have volatility $1.10$--$1.11$ at every $D$; at the reference point it is
$1.11$.

\paragraph{The top of the decline.}  The largest lives sit on high plateaus, where the noise term of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} is small and the firm follows its market, so their volatility is the motion of the field
divided by the size.  The top is not set by the field alone, however.  At equal depth the volatility of
the largest bin rises from $0.40$ to $0.50$ between $D=0.05$ and $0.2$, for reasons we have not isolated.  The
fall from the bottom to the top, $2.8$ at the reference point, therefore shrinks as $D$ grows, from $2.75$ to
$2.19$ at equal depth, together with the range, and the exponent, close to the logarithm of the fall over the
range, changes only from $0.169$ to $0.193$.

\paragraph{What it would take.}  Matching Compustat would take more than a smaller newborn.  A smaller $D$
extends the range downwards, but the fall grows less than the range, so $\zeta_1$ falls, to $0.169$ at
$D=0.05$.  Four orders of magnitude at $\zeta_1\simeq0.2$ need a decline that stays steep below the plateaus.
The top needs a fatter tail of sizes, which is the failure on Zipf's law.

\subsection{Sizes, lives and the aggregate}

\paragraph{The tail of sizes.}  A firm in a field $\Phi$ relaxes to $S=\Phi$, and the field of a node is a sum over its
neighbours whose mean has a nearly Gaussian spread at equal degree (Appendix~\ref{app:field}).  The largest firms
therefore sit at the highest plateaus, a few times the mean firm, and the size distribution falls off steeply above
about ten times the mean firm instead of following Zipf's law.  The same cap sets the top of the range of the
decline.

\paragraph{Short lives.}  A newborn takes the node, and the couplings, of a firm that exited, and exits happen in dips
of the field: the field is below zero at the last sample before $83\%$ of the exits of established firms
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  A newborn therefore starts in a field that has just failed, and many newborns die at
once: $38\%$ of the lives with at least two growth rates have exactly two.  The bottom of the size range is made of these newborns (Sec.~\ref{sec:range}), whereas
Compustat's smallest firms are listed firms.

\paragraph{The aggregate.}  The growth rate of the mean firm is several times more volatile than in the data, and it
does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$ over the sizes we simulate (Table~\ref{tab:robust}).  The economy therefore keeps a common
mode, which we have not separated from the effect of the interactions; this is also why we do not count the rise of
co-movement with size as a result.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We have proposed an economy of interacting firms and scored it on the size--volatility observables of MSB.  With
one fitted exponent, it gives a ratio curve below $q$, tent-shaped growth rates, and rescaled growth that stays
fat-tailed among the largest firms.  Its higher exponents are too large, and its bend needs lives of at least five
growth rates.  It fails on the range of the decline, the tail of the volatility distribution, the size distribution,
the length of lives and the aggregate volatility.

One firm in a moving field explains both sides.  Self-limitation makes a firm relax at a rate equal to its size, so
a firm is a low-pass filter of its market: a small firm integrates the motion of its market, a large one follows it
and resists it, and the decline of volatility with size is the crossover between the two.  The same self-limitation
ties a firm's size to the level of its field, which caps the tail of sizes and the top of the decline.  On the panel
of MSB the law is averaged over each life and flattened, because a life's volatility carries the years it spends
small.  The bend comes from elsewhere: firms of equal degree differ in their mean field, and the life protocol turns
this into a dispersion of volatilities that grows with size.

In a granular model a large firm is calm because it has many independent parts; here it is calm because
self-limitation makes it stiff, and the protocol of the panel matters as much as the dynamics.  Firm data can tell
the two apart (Appendix~\ref{app:tests}): the decline should be steeper against current size than against mean
size, the dispersion at fixed size should grow with size, and trimming the years after entry and before exit should
move the ratio curve towards $q$.

Anomalous moment scaling has been derived for GLV communities before.  With multiplicative environmental noise
and migration, abundances obey Taylor's law with one exponent for every order \cite{alhiyasat2026}, through the
Bouchaud--M\'ezard wealth model \cite{bouchaudmezard2000}.  That law concerns the moments of an abundance.  Ours
concerns the moments of the growth volatility at a given size; its exponents bend below $q\zeta_1$, and it
depends on the network and on turnover.

Three problems are open.  First, the theory reproduces the panel only with each life's own field: we know the
direction in which the life protocol sorts the firms by their field, but not its law.  Its ingredients are the
mean, the frozen spread and the moving amplitude of the field at each degree; a Gaussian closure at finite
connectivity gets the amplitude $20$--$30\%$ low and misses its spread between firms of equal degree.  A closure that keeps this spread, such as the cavity method solved by population dynamics \cite{tarabolo2025,tonolo2025}, is the
natural next step.  Second, the model needs a dynamics
that decouples the decline of volatility from the size cap, both for the range and for Zipf's law.  Third, a theory
of co-movement in a finite economy would separate the common mode from the effect of the interactions.

\section{Relative sizes and the one-firm equation}
\label{app:relative}

This appendix goes from the model, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, to the equation of one firm in its field,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}, and to the standard model it maps onto.  Throughout, $S_i=x_i/m$ is the relative size, so that
$\sum_iS_i=N$; $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is an average over degree classes or nodes, $\E$ an average over the disorder or the
noise, and an overbar a time or life average.

\subsection{The mean firm}

Write $F_i=1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j$, so that the drift of $x_i$ is $x_iF_i$ (because $x_j/m=S_j$).  Summing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} over $i$ and dividing by $N$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\dd m&=\frac1N\sum_jmS_jF_j\,\dd t+\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{D\,m\cdot mS_j}\,\dd W_j\\
&=m\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],
\end{split}
\label{eq:app-dm}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\fb_0=\frac1N\sum_jS_jF_j,\qquad \dd Z=\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{DS_j}\,\dd W_j,\\
(\dd Z)^2=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_jDS_j\,\dd t=\frac DN\,\dd t ,
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:app-Z}
\end{equation}
where the last equality uses $\sum_jS_j=N$.  The common noise $Z$ has quadratic variation of order
$1/N$.

\subsection{It\^o's rule for the quotient}

For $S_i=x_i/m$ It\^o's rule for $f(x,m)=x/m$ gives
\begin{equation}
\dd S_i=\frac{\dd x_i}{m}-\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}+\frac{x_i\,(\dd m)^2}{m^3}-\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}.
\end{equation}
Term by term:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x_i}{m}&=S_iF_i\,\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i,\\
\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=S_i\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],\\
\frac{x_i(\dd m)^2}{m^3}&=S_i\,(\dd Z)^2=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t,\\
\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i\cdot\frac1N\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t ,
\end{align}
where the last line uses $\dd W_i\dd W_j=\delta_{ij}\dd t$.  The two It\^o corrections cancel exactly, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[F_i-\fb_0\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-S_i\,\dd Z .}
\label{eq:app-rel}
\end{equation}
This is exact between entries.

\paragraph{Conservation.}  Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel},
$\sum_i\dd S_i=\big(\sum_iS_iF_i-N\fb_0\big)\dd t+\sum_i\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-N\,\dd Z=0$
by the definitions of $\fb_0$ and $Z$.  So $\sum_iS_i=N$ is preserved between entries.

\paragraph{Size of the common-noise term.}  $S_i\,\dd Z$ has variance $S_i^2D\,\dd t/N$, against $DS_i\,\dd t$
for the own noise: relative size $S_i/N$.  It is of order $N^{-1/2}$ in amplitude and is dropped in the
one-firm equation.  It is the only place where the finite size of the economy enters the single-firm
dynamics directly.

\subsection{Entry dilution}

An exit at $S_i=0$ removes nothing from $m$.  The newborn adds $Dm$ to $\sum_jx_j$, so
$m\to m'=m(1+D/N)$, every incumbent's relative size is multiplied by $1/(1+D/N)$, and the newborn's
relative size is $D/(1+D/N)=D+O(1/N)$.  If entries happen at a rate $N\nu$ per unit time ($\nu$ per
node), then over $\dd t$
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\frac{m(t+\dd t)}{m(t)}=(1+D/N)^{N\nu\,\dd t}\big[1+\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big]\\
\Rightarrow\quad \fb=\fb_0+D\nu ,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and each incumbent picks up the drift $-D\nu S_i$.  So the growth rate of the mean firm is
\begin{equation}
\fb(t)=\frac1N\sum_iS_iF_i+D\nu(t),
\label{eq:app-fbar}
\end{equation}
and in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel} $\fb_0$ is replaced by $\fb$.  Here $\nu(t)$ is the empirical entry rate, which fluctuates at finite $N$.

\subsection{The field and the one-firm equation}

Collect everything except the firm's own self-limitation into the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=F_i+S_i-\fb=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\fb(t).
\label{eq:app-field}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i+O(N^{-1/2}),}
\label{eq:app-onefirm}
\end{equation}
with exit at $S_i=0$ and entry at $S_i=D$.  Up to the common-noise term this is exact as a rewriting; the content of the
``one firm is enough'' result is that $\Phi_i$ may be treated as an external input, i.e.\ that firm $i$'s own
noise does not feed back on $\Phi_i$ through its neighbours.  The feedback is of order
$J_{ij}J_{ji}\sim A_{ij}(\mu^2/C^2+\sigma^2\E[z_{ij}z_{ji}]/C)$, so at zero reciprocity its mean is $\mu^2/C^2$ per
neighbour and $O(1/C)$ in total after summing over $k\sim C$ neighbours.  The products $z_{ij}z_{ji}$ have zero mean but do
not vanish, so the total differs from node to node by $O(C^{-1/2})$.  The approximation is controlled in $C^{-1/2}$ and is tested in Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}.

\paragraph{Plateau.}  At constant $\Phi>0$ the noise-free equation $\dot S=S(\Phi-S)$ has the stable fixed
point $S=\Phi$ ($\partial_S[S(\Phi-S)]=\Phi-2S=-\Phi<0$ there) and relaxes to it at rate $\Phi$.  For
$\Phi<0$, $S=0$ is the only non-negative fixed point and is attracting.  In absolute units the plateau is
$x_i=m\Phi$.

\subsection{The standard model}

In the stationary state of a large economy $\fb$ is constant.  Write $a=1-\fb$ and assume $a>0$.  The drift
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} is $S_i[a-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j]$.  With $S_i=a\,y_i$ and $\tau=a\,t$, so that
$\dd W_i(t)=a^{-1/2}\dd W_i(\tau)$,
\begin{equation}
\dd y_i=y_i\Big[1-y_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}y_j\Big]\dd\tau+\sqrt{\frac{D}{a^2}\,y_i}\;\dd W_i(\tau) .
\end{equation}
This is the standard GLV model with unit growth rate and unit self-limitation, on the same graph and with
the same couplings, with demographic noise of strength $D/a^2$; a firm that reaches zero restarts at
$y=D/a$.  The constraint $\sum_iS_i=N$ fixes $a=1/\langle y\rangle$: the relative sizes are the abundances
of the standard model divided by their mean, $S_i=y_i/\langle y\rangle$.  On the zero-growth line $a=1$
and the two models coincide.

\subsection{Logarithmic form}

By It\^o, $\dd\ln S=\dd S/S-\tfrac12(\dd S)^2/S^2$ with $(\dd S)^2=DS\,\dd t$:
\begin{equation}
\dd\ln S_i=\Big[\Phi_i-S_i-\frac{D}{2S_i}\Big]\dd t+\sqrt{\frac{D}{S_i}}\,\dd W_i .
\label{eq:app-log}
\end{equation}

\section{Mean-field theory and the phase diagram}
\label{app:hdmft}

\subsection{Heterogeneous mean-field theory}

This appendix states the mean-field description of the network, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, in our notation.  The
derivation is the standard dynamical cavity method with degree classes \cite{bunin2017,galla2018,aguirrelopez}.
Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026} derive the static solution without noise and its stability;
Appendix~\ref{app:constant} adds the demographic noise and the turnover.

\paragraph{Degree classes and kernels.}  Group the nodes into classes $p$ with population fractions $\rho_p$ and
degrees $k_p$.  Let $P_{pr}$ be the probability that a node of class $p$ is linked to a node of class $r$, and
define
\begin{equation}
K_{pr}=\frac{N\rho_rP_{pr}}{C},\qquad\sum_rK_{pr}=\kappa_p .
\end{equation}
The uncorrelated kernel $P_{pr}=k_pk_r/(NC)$ gives $K_{pr}=\rho_r\kappa_p\kappa_r$.  It is a probability only if
$k_pk_r\le NC$, and at $\alpha=2.5$ the largest degrees violate this.  The soft-configuration kernel
\cite{patil2026}, $P_{pr}=h_ph_r/(h_ph_r+C/N)$, is always in $[0,1]$, with hidden degrees $h_p$ chosen so that
$\sum_rK_{pr}=\kappa_p$.

\paragraph{The field.}  For $C\to\infty$ the field on a node of class $p$ is a Gaussian process,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
\Phi_p(t)=1-\fb(t)+\mu B_p(t)+\sigma\eta_p(t),\\
\E[\eta_p(t)\eta_p(t')]=q_p(t,t'),
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
closed by
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
B_p(t)=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)],\\
q_p(t,t')=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:app-closure}
\end{equation}
Each class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} with this field, and $\sum_p\rho_p\E[S_p(t)]=1$ fixes $\fb(t)$.  At
zero reciprocity the reaction of the neighbours vanishes for $C\to\infty$ (Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).

\paragraph{The field law.}  With the uncorrelated kernel, $B_p=\kappa_p\bar B$ and $q_p=\kappa_pQ$, where
$\bar B=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r]$ and $Q(t,t')=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')]$ are averages weighted by
degree.  With $\eta_p=\sqrt{\kappa_p}\,\xi$ and $\E[\xi(t)\xi(t')]=Q(t,t')$, this gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}.
Under competition the mean field falls linearly in $\kappa$, and its amplitude grows as $\sqrt\kappa$.  On the
reference graph the uncorrelated kernel fails for the hubs, so the law is approximate there: the measured
amplitude grows as $\kappa^{0.54}$.

\subsection{One firm at constant field, and the instability line with noise}
\label{app:constant}

With noise a firm in a constant field no longer sits at a fixed size: it fluctuates around its plateau, or fails
and is replaced.  This subsection solves that problem exactly, uses the solution in place of the clipped Gaussian
of the noise-free theory \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} to obtain the phase lines with noise, and tests the instability line in simulation.
Local stationary laws with demographic noise have been derived on sparse graphs with immigration, which keeps
every species present \cite{machado2025}; here zero is absorbing, and a newborn replaces the firm that exits.

\subsubsection{Scale function, speed measure and boundaries}

At constant $\Phi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} is the one-dimensional diffusion
$\dd S=b(S)\,\dd t+\sqrt{a(S)}\,\dd W$ with $b(y)=y(\Phi-y)$ and $a(y)=Dy$, generator
$\mathcal L=\tfrac12Dy\,\partial_y^2+y(\Phi-y)\partial_y$.  The scale density and speed density
\cite{karlintaylor1981} (in this appendix $m(y)$ is the speed density, not the mean firm) are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
s'(y)&=\exp\Big[-\int_0^y\frac{2b(u)}{a(u)}\dd u\Big]\\
&=\exp\Big[-\frac2D\Big(\Phi y-\frac{y^2}2\Big)\Big],\\
m(y)&=\frac{2}{a(y)s'(y)}=\frac{2}{Dy\,s'(y)},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
since $2b/a=2(\Phi-u)/D$.  Let $s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,\dd u$.  Properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item Near $0$, $s(y)\simeq y$ and $m(y)\simeq2/(Dy)$; $\int_0s(y)m(y)\,\dd y<\infty$, so $0$ is reached in finite time
  (an exit boundary in Feller's classification).
\item $s'(y)\sim e^{y^2/D}$ for large $y$, so $s(\infty)=\infty$: $\infty$ is never reached, and the process started anywhere
  hits $0$ with probability one.  Every life ends, so at a node with constant field the lives form a proper
  renewal sequence.
\item $\int^\infty m(z)\,\dd z<\infty$ (Gaussian decay).
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Mean exit time}

$T(x)$ solves $\mathcal LT=-1$ with $T(0)=0$ and no flux at infinity.  Since $s''/s'=-2b/a$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd y}\Big(\frac{T'}{s'}\Big)=\frac{T''+(2b/a)T'}{s'}=\frac{2\,\mathcal LT}{a\,s'}=-\frac{2}{a\,s'}=-m(y).
\end{equation}
Integrating from $y$ to $\infty$ with $T'/s'\to0$ at infinity, $T'(y)=s'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z$, and
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
T(x)&=\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z\,\dd y\\
&=2\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty\frac{\dd z}{Dz\,s'(z)}\,\dd y .
\end{split}
\label{eq:app-T}
\end{equation}


\subsubsection{Green's function and the renewal law}

Exchanging the order of integration in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} (Fubini; all integrands positive),
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
T(x_0)&=\int_0^\infty m(z)\int_0^{\min(x_0,z)}s'(y)\,\dd y\,\dd z\\
&=\int_0^\infty\underbrace{s(\min(x_0,z))\,m(z)}_{G(x_0,z)}\dd z .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
$G(x_0,z)$ is the Green's function: the expected time spent near $z$, per unit $z$, before absorption, starting at $x_0$.

\paragraph{Renewal--reward.}  With replacement at $D$, the successive lives at a node are i.i.d.\ copies of the
process started at $D$.  For any set $\mathcal A$, the long-run fraction of time spent in $\mathcal A$ equals
$\E[\text{time in }\mathcal A\text{ during one life}]/\E[\text{life length}]$.  So the stationary density of sizes at a node with constant field $\Phi=h$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
p(x\mid h)=\frac{G(D,x)}{T(D)}=\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)\,T(D)},\\
T(D)=\int_0^\infty G(D,x)\,\dd x,
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:renewal}
\end{equation}
and by the elementary renewal theorem the turnover rate is $1/T(D)$ per node per unit time.

\paragraph{Shape.}  For $x>D$, $s(\min(x,D))=s(D)$ is constant and
$1/(xs'(x))=x^{-1}\exp[(2/D)(hx-x^2/2)]=x^{-1}e^{h^2/D}e^{-(x-h)^2/D}$: a Gaussian factor of variance $D/2$ around
$h$ times $x^{-1}$.  For $x<D$, $x\ll D/|h|$ and $x\ll\sqrt D$, $s(x)\simeq x$ and $s'(x)\simeq1$, so $p\simeq2/(DT(D))$: flat.


\paragraph{Exit from a plateau.}  For a firm on its plateau, $\Phi>0$, Laplace's method on the peak of $m(z)$ at
$z\simeq\Phi$ gives $T\simeq\sqrt{\pi D}\,\Phi^{-2}e^{\Phi^2/D}$ to exponential order, for $\Phi^2\gg D$.  At $D=0.1$
the quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} from the plateau gives $13\,900$ time units at $\Phi=1$ ($12\,300$ from the
Laplace form), $200$ at $\Phi=0.7$, $38$ at $\Phi=0.5$ and $13$ at $\Phi=0.3$.  A firm on a plateau well above
$\sqrt D$ therefore exits through dips of its field rather than through its own noise; even on low plateaus most
exits happen in dips (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).

\subsubsection{The static closure with noise}

When the field is static, each node follows the renewal law \eqref{eq:renewal} at its own $h=a+\mu B_p+\sigma\sqrt{q_p}\,z$, with $a=1-f^*$, $f^*$ the stationary growth rate of the mean firm, and $z$ a frozen standard Gaussian.
Let $\langle S\rangle_h=\int xp(x\mid h)\,\dd x$.  The static closure is Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} with time averages:
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
B_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r},\qquad q_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r}^2,\\
\sum_p\rho_p\E\langle S\rangle_{h_p}=1,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $\E$ the Gaussian average over $z$.  Using $\langle S\rangle^2_h$ in $q_p$ counts only the static part of the neighbours'
sizes in the field covariance, i.e.\ the temporal fluctuations of $S$ produced by the noise are taken as not
feeding the field.  (Using $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ instead, ``as if quenched'', moves the lines by less than $0.015$.)

\subsubsection{The susceptibility and the instability line}
\label{app:susceptibility}

The response argument of Refs.~\cite{opperdiederich1992,bunin2017,aguirrelopez,patil2026} carries over with one change.  Shift the growth rate of every
node by a small static amount $\delta h_i$, independent between nodes, of zero mean and variance $\varsigma^2$.  The
time-averaged size of a node responds to the shift of its own field and to the shifts of its neighbours' mean
sizes,
\begin{equation}
\delta\langle S_i\rangle=\chi_i\Big(\delta h_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}\,\delta\langle S_j\rangle\Big),\qquad
\chi=\frac{\partial\langle S\rangle_h}{\partial h},
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i$ is evaluated at the node's own field.  By the cavity argument the sum is a Gaussian of variance
$\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r$ for a node of class $p$, independent of $\chi_i$, with $Y_r=\E[\delta\langle S\rangle_r^2]$.
Squaring and averaging,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
Y_p=\E[\chi_p^2]\Big(\varsigma^2+\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r\Big)\\
\Longrightarrow\quad Y=\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2\,\varsigma^2 .
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The mean squared response per unit variance of the shift is the spin-glass susceptibility,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\chi_{\rm SG}=\frac1{\varsigma^2}\sum_p\rho_pY_p=\rho^{\sf T}\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2 .}
\label{eq:chisg}
\end{equation}
It is finite while the Perron root $\Lambda$ of $\sigma^2{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K$ is below one and diverges as
$\Lambda\to1$, which defines $\sigma_c(\mu,D)$.  Without noise $\langle S\rangle_h=[h]_+$, $\chi=\theta(h)$, $\chi^2=\chi$ and
$\E\chi^2=\Pr(h>0)$, the fraction of surviving firms: the deterministic criterion of Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} is recovered.  With noise $\chi$ is a smooth function of $h$ in place of a step, and the line moves up, almost linearly in $D$ (below).

\paragraph{Numerical solution.}  We tabulate $\langle S\rangle_h$, $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ and $\ln T(D;h)$ once per $D$ on a grid of fields,
by quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:renewal} in logarithms, with a logarithmic grid below $D$, and obtain $\chi$ by
differentiation.  The closure is iterated with damping on $(B_p,q_p)$, the normalisation being solved for $a$ at
every step, with $60$ degree classes of equal population built from the realised degree sequence and the
soft-configuration kernel.  The instability line is the root in $\sigma$ of $\Lambda=1$, and the zero-growth line the
root of $a=1$.

\paragraph{Turnover.}  $\sum_p\rho_p\E[1/T(D;h_p)]$ per node per unit time.  Nodes with $h<0$ dominate it, and by
the first limit above their newborns' lives are nearly $D$-independent: turnover is nearly independent of the noise.

\subsubsection{The instability line in simulation}
\label{app:linetest}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:chisg} can be measured directly.  For each point we run three economies with
the same couplings and the same realisation of the noise ($D=0.1$, total time $700$, the first $200$ discarded,
$N=4000$, $C=100$): a base run; a run in which the growth rate of every firm is shifted by $\varsigma\eta_i$, with
$\eta_i$ independent standard Gaussians and $\varsigma=0.01$; and a run from other initial sizes.  The mean squared
difference between the time-averaged sizes of the first two, divided by $\varsigma^2$, is $\chi_{\rm SG}$; using the
same noise removes most of the sampling error.  The third run tells whether the composition is slaved to the
noise.  Below the line two economies with the same noise forget their initial sizes and follow each other;
above it the composition has a motion of its own and they separate.  We measure their distance
$d=\overline{(S_i^A-S_i^B)^2}/(2\Var S)$ over the last $100$ samples, which is $1$ for two economies with nothing in common; the frozen part that the same couplings give to both keeps it below $1$.  For
each graph we take nine values of $\sigma$ around the predicted line: $\alpha=2.5$ at $\mu=0$, $-0.5$, $-1$, $-1.76$ and
$-2.5$, and $\alpha=6$ at $\mu=0$, $-1.76$ and $-2.5$, two graphs per point.  The theory uses the degree classes of the
same graphs, so its lines differ from those of Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}, which are for the reference graph ($N=32000$,
$C=200$): at $\mu=0$ and $\alpha=2.5$, $0.95$ here against $0.83$ there.

\subsection{The field a firm feels}
\label{app:field}

\paragraph{Field statistics at the reference point.}  A node's mean field falls with its degree, from $0.5$ at
$\kappa\simeq0.4$ to $-2.3$ at $\kappa\simeq2$.  Among nodes of equal degree the mean field has a spread of
$2.66\sqrt\kappa$ in standard deviation, close to Gaussian (excess kurtosis $0.3$).  Around its mean the field
moves with amplitude $2.15\sqrt\kappa$ (as $\kappa^{0.54}$ across degree classes); this amplitude differs between
nodes of equal degree by about $30\%$, and the field decorrelates over about $4$ time units.  Driving each firm
by a Gaussian field with its node's time mean, its temporal amplitude and one correlation function gives
$\zeta_1=0.23$ and ratios $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model.
Setting the amplitudes to their class value raises $\zeta_1$ to $0.28$ but barely changes the ratios ($1.81$,
$2.42$, $2.86$); setting the means to their class value as well gives ratios $1.93$, $2.79$, $3.56$.  At the level of
the nodes, the bend therefore comes from the frozen spread of mean fields at equal degree, which decides how long a
firm lives and how much of its field's motion its life sees.  A Gaussian spread of the means with the same variance
gives the same panel.

\paragraph{The closure.}  Each degree class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with a Gaussian field closed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure}; at zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.  We solve the closure in the stationary
state at the reference point by sampling the effective process \cite{eissfeller1992,roy2019}, with $31$ degree
classes.  Evaluated on the measured sizes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} gives the temporal and frozen parts of the
measured field to within $4\%$ and $6\%$.  Solved self-consistently, it gets the mean field, but its temporal
amplitude is $0.71$--$0.81$ of the measured one in every class, and its life panel has $\zeta_1=0.34$ and ratios
$1.85$, $2.53$, $3.07$.  The cause is the finite connectivity: at $C=200$ a few strongly fluctuating neighbours
dominate the temporal field of a node, so its amplitude varies between nodes of equal degree, with a coefficient
of variation of $0.24$--$0.36$, and the closure replaces this spread by its mean.

\subsection{Derivation of the filter law}

This subsection derives Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter} by linearising the one-firm equation around the firm's current size.

\paragraph{From the model to the log equation.}  In relative sizes the model reduces to
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm}, $\dd S=S(\Phi-S)\,\dd t+\sqrt{DS}\,\dd W$, with the field of Eq.~\eqref{eq:field}
(Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  By It\^o's rule $y=\ln S$ obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-log},
\begin{equation*}
\dd y=\Big[\Phi(t)-S-\frac{D}{2S}\Big]\dd t+\sqrt{\frac DS}\;\dd W .
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Field.}  Write $\Phi(t)=h+\delta\Phi(t)$, with $h$ the mean field of the node and $\delta\Phi$ a stationary
motion of variance $f^2$ and correlation $\rho$, $\E[\delta\Phi(t)\delta\Phi(t')]=f^2\rho(t-t')$.

\paragraph{Linearisation at the current size.}  Follow the firm from its size $S$ at time $t$: write
$y=\ln S+\delta y$, so that the size is $Se^{\delta y}\simeq S(1+\delta y)$.  Term by term,
$\Phi-Se^{\delta y}\simeq(h-S)+\delta\Phi-S\,\delta y$, $D/(2Se^{\delta y})\simeq D/(2S)$ and
$\sqrt{D/(Se^{\delta y})}\simeq\sqrt{D/S}$.  The constants $h-S$ and $D/(2S)$ only shift the mean growth, which the
volatility subtracts, so
\begin{equation}
\dd\,\delta y=\big[-S\,\delta y+\delta\Phi(t)\big]\dd t+\sqrt{\frac DS}\;\dd W .
\label{eq:app-linear}
\end{equation}
The firm relaxes at a rate equal to its size, because self-limitation is proportional to size, and two inputs drive
it: the motion of its field and its own noise.

\paragraph{Solution.}  With the rate held at $S$, the stationary solution is
\begin{equation*}
\delta y(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-S(t-u)}\Big[\delta\Phi(u)\,\dd u+\sqrt{D/S}\;\dd W(u)\Big].
\end{equation*}
It is the sum of a field part and a noise part.  The noise $W$ is independent of the field, since the field does
not respond to the firm's own noise (Table~\ref{tab:reduction}), so the autocovariance of $\delta y$ is the sum
$C=C_\Phi+C_W$ of the autocovariances of the two parts.

\paragraph{Growth variance.}  The growth over a horizon $\tau$ is $g_\tau=\delta y(t+\tau)-\delta y(t)$ plus a
constant, so $\Var g_\tau=2[C(0)-C(\tau)]$.

\paragraph{Noise part.}  The noise part is an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process of rate $S$ and intensity $D/S$, with
$C_W(t)=D\,e^{-S|t|}/(2S^2)$.  Its contribution is $D(1-e^{-S\tau})/S^2$.

\paragraph{Field part.}  In frequency the field part is $\delta y_\Phi(\omega)=\delta\Phi(\omega)/(S+i\omega)$,
with spectrum $f^2\hat\rho(\omega)/(S^2+\omega^2)$, where $\hat\rho$ is the spectrum of $\rho$.  Since
$2[C_\Phi(0)-C_\Phi(\tau)]$ is the spectrum weighted by $|e^{i\omega\tau}-1|^2=2(1-\cos\omega\tau)$, its contribution
is $f^2G(S,\tau)$ with $G$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter}.  For an exponential correlation of time $\tau_c$,
\begin{equation*}
G(S,\tau)=\frac{2\big[S\big(1-e^{-\tau/\tau_c}\big)-\tau_c^{-1}\big(1-e^{-S\tau}\big)\big]}{S\big(S^2-\tau_c^{-2}\big)},
\end{equation*}
which tends to $2\tau_c^2(\tau/\tau_c-1+e^{-\tau/\tau_c})\simeq\tau^2$ for $S\to0$, the variance of the field summed
over the horizon, and to $2(1-e^{-\tau/\tau_c})/S^2$ for $S\to\infty$, where $\delta y\simeq\delta\Phi/S$ follows the
level of the field.  The measured correlation is smoother than an exponential at short lags, so we evaluate $G$ from
the measured $\rho$, as the network mean of the normalised fields.

\paragraph{The law.}  Adding the two parts gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:filter}.  At a given size, $f^2$ is the mean squared
amplitude of the firms of that size; it grows only as $S^{0.10}$ on the power-law graph and $S^{0.02}$ on the regular
graph, and a single network value gives nearly the same law on the regular graph.

\paragraph{Conditions.}  (1) The noise is small, $D/(2S^2)\ll1$, i.e.\ $S\gg\sqrt{D/2}\simeq0.22$ at $D=0.1$.  This is
why the law fails among newborns.  (2) The firm stays near its current size over the horizon, so that its rate stays
$S$; this fails for long horizons, over four years on the power-law graph.  (3) The field is Gaussian and stationary.
(4) The economy is large, so the noise of the mean firm drops out, and the field ignores the firm's own noise.

\section{The criterion and the length of a life}
\label{app:multiscaling}

This appendix derives the general relation between the ratio curve and the dispersion at fixed size
(Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), the estimation noise of a life's volatility, and the law of life lengths that sorts
the firms on the panel (Sec.~\ref{sec:panel}).

\subsection{The general criterion}
\label{app:cumulant}

Write $v=\bar v(\Sb)\,X$ with $\bar v(\Sb)=\E[v\mid\Sb]$, so that $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln\bar v(\Sb)+K_q(\Sb)$ with $K_q=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$, and $K_1=0$.  Differentiating in $\ln\Sb$, the
case $q=1$ gives $\zeta_1=-d\ln\bar v/d\ln\Sb$, and the general case
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb},
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:general}.  No assumption has been made.  $K_q$ is the cumulant generating function of $\ln X$
at fixed size, $K_q=\ln\E[e^{q\ln X}]=\sum_{n\ge1}\gamma_nq^n/n!$ with $\gamma_n(\Sb)$ the cumulants of $\ln X$.  It is convex in
$q$ (H\"older's inequality) and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so it is negative between them and positive for
$q>1$.  Since $K_1=\sum_n\gamma_n/n!=0$, subtracting $q$ times this from $K_q$ removes the mean $\gamma_1$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\zeta_q-q\zeta_1&=-\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{q^n-q}{n!}\,\frac{d\gamma_n}{d\ln\Sb}\\
&=-\frac{q(q-1)}2\,\frac{d\Var(\ln X\mid\Sb)}{d\ln\Sb}\\
&\quad-\frac{q(q^2-1)}{6}\,\frac{d\gamma_3}{d\ln\Sb}-\dots
\end{split}
\end{equation}
To second order the curve lies below $q$ if and only if the variance of $\ln v$ at fixed size grows with size, and
above $q$ if it shrinks.  For a lognormal $X$ only $\gamma_2=\sigma^2(\Sb)$ is left and the mean of $\ln X$ is $-\sigma^2/2$, so
$K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}.  If the law of $X$ does not depend on size,
every $\gamma_n$ is constant and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.

\subsection{Estimation noise}

Let a life have $n$ growth rates, independent and Gaussian with standard deviation $s$.  With the mean absolute
deviation, $v=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,n^{-1}\sum_t|g_t-\bar g|$.  For a Gaussian $\E|g-\bar g|\simeq s\sqrt{2/\pi}$, so $\E v\simeq s$
(exactly, $g_t-\bar g$ has variance $s^2(1-1/n)$ and $\E v=s\sqrt{1-1/n}$; Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}),
and $\Var|g|=s^2(1-2/\pi)$, so
\begin{equation}
\Var\ln\epsilon\simeq\frac{\Var v}{(\E v)^2}=\frac1n\,\frac{1-2/\pi}{2/\pi}=\frac{\pi/2-1}{n}.
\end{equation}
With the standard deviation, $v^2$ is $s^2$ times a chi-squared variable over its number of degrees of freedom,
$\Var(v^2)/s^4\simeq2/n$, and $\Var\ln\epsilon=\tfrac14\Var\ln v^2\simeq1/(2n)$.  Both are leading orders in $1/n$, and fat
tails raise both.  For short lives the leading order is far too small.  The sum of squares about the
sample mean is $s^2$ times a chi-squared variable with $n-1$ degrees of freedom, whose logarithm has
variance $\psi'((n-1)/2)$, with $\psi'$ the trigamma function, so that exactly
\begin{equation}
\Var\ln\epsilon=\tfrac14\,\psi'\Big(\frac{n-1}{2}\Big):
\end{equation}
$1.23$, $0.41$ and $0.16$ for $n=2$, $3$ and $5$, against $0.25$, $0.17$ and $0.10$ from $1/(2n)$.  With all
lives, the mean of the exact value within size bins falls from $0.92$ in the smallest fifth to $0.05$ in
the largest, and the measured variance of $\ln v$ from $1.04$ to $0.14$.  If the mean number of growth rates grows with size, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal} adds $+\tfrac{q(q-1)}2\,|d\Var\ln\epsilon/d\ln\Sb|$ to $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$: the ratios move up.

\subsection{How long a firm lives}

\paragraph{A firm in a dip is a branching process.}  An established firm sits near its plateau.  If the
plateau is well above $\sqrt D$ its own noise cannot kill it: the exit time grows as $e^{\Phi^2/D}$
(Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  It dies when its field dips below zero.  Inside the dip the firm is small and the logistic term $-S^2$ is negligible, so
\begin{equation}
dS=r(t)\,S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW,
\end{equation}
with $r(t)$ the field less the self-limitation along the noise-free path.  This is Feller's branching diffusion
with a time-dependent rate \cite{feller1951}.  Because independent pieces of the firm evolve independently, the
Laplace transform of its size is exponential in the initial size, $\E[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-S_0\omega_0}$, where $\omega_0$ is
the value at time $0$ of the solution of the backward equation $-\partial_s\omega=r(s)\,\omega-\tfrac12D\omega^2$ with $\omega(t)=\lambda$.
The equation is linear in $1/\omega$, $\partial_s(1/\omega)=r/\omega-D/2$, and with $R(t)=\int_0^tr(s)\,ds$ its solution is
\begin{equation}
\frac1{\omega_0}=\frac{e^{-R(t)}}{\lambda}+\frac D2\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds .
\end{equation}
The probability that the firm is extinct by time $t$ is the limit $\lambda\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S_t=0)=\exp\Big[-\frac{2S_0}{D\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds}\Big].
\end{equation}
The noise-free path through the dip is $S_{\rm det}(t)=S_0e^{R(t)}$, so $S_0^{-1}\int e^{-R}ds=\int dt/S_{\rm det}$, which
gives the kill probability of a dip,
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm kill}=\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{D\,J}\Big),\qquad J=\int\frac{dt}{S_{\rm det}(t)} .
\label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}  For a constant rate $r>0$ and
$t\to\infty$ it reduces to the classical survival probability $1-e^{-2rS_0/D}$.

\paragraph{Test of the kill law.}  For every downcrossing of zero by the field of a node whose firm is established
($S\ge0.3$), we restart the noise-free path at the firm's actual size, integrate until it recovers to that size,
and compare $P_{\rm kill}$ with whether the firm died.  Over the $446\,554$ dips the predicted kill fraction is
$0.725$ against $0.722$ observed.  Binned by the predicted probability, the observed kill fractions are $0.015$ at
$0.027$, $0.102$ at $0.147$, $0.418$ at $0.450$, $0.894$ at $0.853$ and $0.986$ at $0.974$, and the log-likelihood per
dip is $-0.175$ against $-0.591$ for a constant kill rate.  No parameter is fitted.

\paragraph{Exits and life lengths.}  Of the $186\,492$ exits of lives with at least $20$ growth rates, the field is below
zero at the last sample before the exit in $83\%$ ($81\%$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $85\%$ in the largest),
and at one of the last four samples in $95\%$, although the field of the same lives is below zero only $21\%$ of the
time.  Exits therefore happen in dips at every size.  Dips are rarer the higher the node's mean field $\bar\Phi$
stands over its temporal amplitude $f$, so the mean life grows from about $23$ to $220$ growth rates across deciles of
$\bar\Phi/f$.  Successive dips are nearly independent, so the life of an established firm is close to exponential:
among complete lives the coefficient of variation of $n-20$ is $1.05$--$1.22$ in every decile of $\bar\Phi/f$ but the
highest, where the window cuts the longest lives.

\section{Tests on firm data}
\label{app:tests}

The criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} holds for any panel.  The theory of Secs.~\ref{sec:filter} and \ref{sec:panel} makes two
claims that firm data can test: the decline of volatility is a property of the current size, which the panel averages
over each life, and the bend comes from the spread of mean fields among firms of equal degree, which the life protocol turns
into a dispersion that grows with size.  They lead to four tests
(Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}; two reference economies, the panel's estimator).

\paragraph{The decline is steeper against current size than against mean size.}  The same firms of the model give
a slope of $0.58$ against current size over one year and $\zeta_1=0.19$ against life-mean size
(Sec.~\ref{sec:lives}).  Both conditionings can be measured on the same firm data, against the size at the start of
each year \cite{stanley1996} and against the mean size of each firm's listed life \cite{moran2024}.  A small
difference between them would mean that real firms pass through a much narrower range of sizes within a life than
the firms of the model.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_predictions.pdf}
\caption{Three tests for firm data (two reference economies, $N=16000$, volatility from the mean absolute
deviation).  (a) Within-bin variance of $\ln v$ by fifth of life-mean size, for lives with at least $20$ and at
least $5$ growth rates.  (b) $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ after trimming $k$ years after each birth and before
each exit, lives that keep at least $20$ growth rates; diamond: only the plateau of each life.  (c) Ratio curves
by minimum life length.}
\label{fig:predictions}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{The dispersion at fixed size grows with size.}  For long lives the within-bin variance of $\ln v$
grows with size, most among the largest firms (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}a).  Compustat's exponents imply that
its dispersion grows, but not where (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  Measuring $K_q(\Sb)$ by size would show where.

\paragraph{Trimming entry and exit years moves the curve towards $q$.}  Dropping the first and last $k$ years
of each life steepens the decline and weakens the bend (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}b), because these are the years
that a life spends small (Sec.~\ref{sec:lives}).  Keeping only the plateau,
which data cannot locate, goes further in the same direction.  Compustat firms enter at listing, already large,
so the years before delisting should carry the effect.  With short lives the test is not clean, because trimming
also adds estimation noise.

\paragraph{Short lives push the curve towards or above $q$.}  With all lives the ratios lie above $q$, and the
bend appears from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}c).  With short lives the dispersion at fixed size
is U-shaped: estimation noise dominates among small firms and the spread of mean fields among large ones
(Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}a).  Panels that include very small firms \cite{fontanelli2025} should therefore show
ratios near or above $q$, which fall below $q$ as the minimum life length grows.

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{Integration.}  We integrate by Euler--Maruyama with step $0.01$, discard $t<100$, and record sizes
every $0.5$ time units over a window of length $480$.

\paragraph{Minimum life length.}  MSB keep every firm with at least two growth rates; their firms have $46$ on
average, and their results do not change for firms with at least $20$.  Their growth rates are annual
differences rolled over quarters, $g_t=\ln S_{t+4}-\ln S_t$, so $46$ of them span about twelve years and $20$
about six, or five non-overlapping annual growth rates.  Our $n_{\min}=5$ is therefore the like-for-like cut.
The model's lives are much shorter (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}).  We test the theory at $n_{\min}=20$, where each
life is long enough to measure its own field.  In
the production run $\zeta_q=0.180$, $0.434$, $0.708$, $0.965$ (ratios $2.41$, $3.94$, $5.36$) for $n_{\min}=2$, $0.209$,
$0.400$, $0.561$, $0.685$ ($1.92$, $2.69$, $3.28$) for $n_{\min}=5$, $0.218$, $0.388$, $0.508$, $0.582$ ($1.78$, $2.33$,
$2.67$) for $n_{\min}=10$ and $0.254$, $0.452$, $0.598$, $0.701$ ($1.78$, $2.35$, $2.75$) for $n_{\min}=20$, over $2.1$,
$2.0$, $1.8$ and $1.4$ orders of magnitude: the ratios lie above $q$ for $n_{\min}=2$ and below from $n_{\min}=5$, as the
estimation noise of short lives requires (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), and the range shrinks because long lives
do not occur at the newborn sizes.

\paragraph{Spread between economies.}  The facts of Table~\ref{tab:facts} pool the lives of the kept economies,
and the uncertainty we quote for the model is the standard deviation between economies.  Over the seven quiet
production economies, $\zeta_1$ has a mean of $0.213$ and a standard deviation of $0.024$, and the ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ have standard deviations of $0.04$, $0.14$ and $0.29$.  A bootstrap over the lives of one
economy gives $0.0004$ for $\zeta_1$ and $0.002$, $0.008$ and $0.021$ for the ratios.  The spread is therefore not
the sampling noise of the lives.  It is the variation from one economy to the next, through its couplings and its
one finite history, and it grows with $q$ because a few lives with large volatility carry the high moments.  The
bootstrap treats lives as independent, so it is a lower bound.  Compustat is also one finite history: MSB's errors
on $\zeta_1$ and $\zeta_4$ give its ratio $\zeta_4/\zeta_1=2.90$ an uncertainty of about $0.25$, close to the
model's $0.29$.

\paragraph{Bursts.}  In a burst one firm runs away.  It grows to $100$--$300$ times the mean firm and collapses
within a few years; in a quiet economy the largest firm is $15$--$45$ times the mean firm.  While the runaway is
large, its growth sets the growth rate $\fb$ of the mean firm, and through the term $-\fb$ in every field it gives
every firm the same shock.  The shock weighs most on large firms, whose own volatility is smallest, so the decline
of volatility flattens and the correlation with the aggregate rises to $0.5$--$0.7$.  Bursts are a property of the
couplings, not of the noise or of the step.  We re-ran the burst economy with two other realisations of the noise
and with half the step.  In all four runs the aggregate volatility is $0.45$--$0.69$ per year, a firm exceeds $50$
times the mean firm in $14$--$17\%$ of the samples, and it is the same node every time, of degree $3526$.  A quiet
economy re-run with other noise stays quiet.  Runaways can also be rare: in one production economy a firm exceeds
$50$ times the mean firm in $2\%$ of the samples, and the aggregate volatility is $0.26$ per year.

We exclude economies whose aggregate volatility exceeds $0.3$ per year: one of the eight production economies, and
five of the $80$ economies of Table~\ref{tab:robust}.  The rule excludes economies in which a runaway dominates,
not every economy with a runaway, and the spread between economies supports it.  The excluded production economy
lies $5.6$ standard deviations below the quiet ones in $\zeta_1$ ($0.076$) and $6.3$ in $\zeta_4/\zeta_1$ ($1.54$).
The economy with a rare runaway lies within $1.8$ standard deviations of them, and we keep it.  With the excluded
economy, the pooled panel gives $\zeta_1=0.170$ and ratios $1.79$, $2.30$, $2.52$.  The fraction of excluded
economies shows no trend with $N$: $3$ of $36$ at $N=8000$, $0$ of $28$ at $16000$, $2$ of $14$ at $32000$ and $0$
of $2$ at $64000$, over several parameter points.  We do not know which property of a node lets its firm run away.

\paragraph{The year.}  We sample every $0.5$ time units.  Figure~\ref{fig:limits} shows the lag-one
autocorrelation of growth, demeaned within each life, at three steps.  Demeaning biases each value down by
about $1/n$, most among the short lives of small firms, so the rise with size is robust and the sign in the
smallest fifth is not.  At step $0.5$ the serial correlation rises with size, as in firm data
\cite{coad2007}; at step $2$ it is gone at every size, and a step of $2$ also leaves few growth rates per life.  In the two reference economies, lives with at least five
growth rates give $\zeta_q=0.208$, $0.403$, $0.572$, $0.711$ (ratios $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$) over $2.0$
orders of magnitude at step $0.5$, and $\zeta_q=0.154$, $0.259$, $0.316$, $0.333$ (ratios $1.68$, $2.06$, $2.16$) over
$1.4$ orders of magnitude at step $2$: volatility per step rises by a factor $1.1$ in the smallest size bin and $2.2$
in the largest, so the decline flattens, and the ratio curve bends further below $q$.  For lives with
at least $20$ growth rates the change is smaller ($\zeta_1=0.253$ and $0.247$, ratios $1.79$, $2.37$,
$2.80$ and $1.75$, $2.28$, $2.62$, although at step $2$ these lives span only $0.9$ orders of magnitude).  The bend
below $q$ survives the coarser year; the level of $\zeta_1$ for short lives does not.

\paragraph{The factor in the volatility.}  For a Gaussian, $\E|X-\E X|$ is $\sqrt{2/\pi}$ times the standard
deviation.  For Gaussian growth $v$ therefore estimates the standard deviation; for fat-tailed
growth it is a robust scale that is smaller than the standard deviation.  About the sample mean of $n$
growth rates the deviation $g_t-\bar g$ has variance $s^2(1-1/n)$, so $\E v=s\sqrt{1-1/n}$: the estimator is
low by $11\%$ at $n=5$ and $29\%$ at $n=2$.  Small firms live shorter, so the bias flattens the decline.
Multiplying $v$ by $\sqrt{n/(n-1)}$ gives $\zeta_1=0.254$, $0.230$ and $0.258$ for $n_{\min}=2$, $5$ and $20$
in the two reference economies, against $0.179$, $0.208$ and $0.253$; the ratios at $n_{\min}=5$ become
$1.93$, $2.72$, $3.35$ against $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$.  Most of
the dependence of $\zeta_1$ on the minimum life length is this bias; from $n_{\min}=5$ the ratio curve hardly
feels it.
In the comparison with Compustat we keep the estimator of MSB as it is.

\paragraph{The fits.}  Figure~\ref{fig:facts}b shows the lines behind the exponents of Table~\ref{tab:facts}.  For
$q\le2$ the residuals stay within $0.1$ in log units; for $q=3$ and $4$ the end bins lie above the lines, by up
to $0.19$ and $0.52$ in the largest bin.

\paragraph{The tail index $b$.}  For lives with at least two growth rates the model gives
$b=4.39$--$4.44$ at every parameter point, close to the Compustat value $4.6$.  A null with iid Laplace
growth rates, no differences between firms and the model's distribution of life lengths gives
$4.34$, so the agreement is an effect of short lives.  For longer lives $b$ grows: $5.2$, $5.5$ and $6.6$ in the production
run for $n_{\min}=5$, $10$ and $20$ ($5.7$ and $6.9$ at $N=16000$ for $10$ and $20$), against $9.3$ and $12.6$ for the Laplace null and $4.6$ in
Compustat for both samples.

\paragraph{Volatility estimator.}  For lives with at least five growth rates, the standard deviation instead of
$\sqrt{\pi/2}$ times the mean absolute deviation gives a $\zeta_1$ lower by $0.03$ and ratios higher by $0.03$--$0.3$
(production run: $\zeta_1=0.178$, ratios $1.95$, $2.81$, $3.54$).  The
gap grows for short lives: with all lives the two reference economies give $\zeta_1=0.094$ with the standard
deviation and $0.179$ with the mean absolute deviation (notebook 05 and Table~\ref{tab:reduction}).

\paragraph{Rescaled growth.}  MSB rescale each growth rate by its firm's leave-one-out mean and volatility,
$\hat g_t=(g_t-\bar g_{-t})/\sigma_{-t}$, so that a large rate does not shrink its own scale, and find densities
that hardly depend on size \cite{moran2024}.  We rescale the lives with at least five growth rates of the two
reference economies the same way.  Compustat's densities, kernel estimates in $25$ size
bins, are read from the vector paths of MSB's figures; we use the plotted curves because we could not
reconcile the fitted parameters of their Table~1 with the plotted fit.  The curves stop at a density of
$10^{-4}$ on $|z|\le8$, so we compare the excess kurtosis on that window.  By fifth of size the model gives
$3.0$, $3.7$, $4.3$, $4.8$, $5.7$ and Compustat $4.2$, $5.6$, $5.1$, $5.4$, $5.7$ ($3.5$--$4.2$ in its three
smallest bins).  A Gaussian null with the same life lengths gives $2.5$, $2.4$, $1.6$, $0.4$, $0.1$: among
small firms most of the model's kurtosis is the rescaling of short lives.  The full fourth moments are dominated by rare events
beyond $|z|=8$, outside the range of the Compustat curves; rescaling instead by each life's own volatility,
lives with at least $10$ growth rates give $1.3$ to $23$ by fifth in the production run.

\paragraph{The tail of the growth rates.}  The tails of the pooled growth rates of Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}e
are exponential, not power laws.  Over the $1.8\times10^7$ growth rates of lives with at least five, divided
by their common volatility, the tail exponent of $|z|$ estimated from the top $10\%$, $3\%$, $1\%$, $0.3\%$ and
$0.1\%$ rises with the depth of the tail, $2.3$, $3.0$, $3.5$, $4.3$ and $5.2$, as for a Laplace law ($3.1$ to $7.8$)
and unlike a power law (Student $t_3$: $2.5$ to $3.0$).  The slope of $\ln P(|z|>x)$ against $x$ is
$-0.53$, $-0.47$ and $-0.48$ over the three orders of magnitude of probability from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$: an exponential
tail, fatter than the Laplace law of the same volatility.

\title{Interaction heterogeneity and the multiscaling of firm growth volatility}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The growth volatility of firms falls with their size as $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q}$ with
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$, and the ratios $\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ fall below $q$: the shape of the volatility
distribution changes with size.  Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of independent parts,
give a volatility law of fixed shape and therefore $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.  We study instead an economy of
interacting firms: a generalised Lotka--Volterra model on a scale-free network, with interactions
and self-limitation measured in units of the mean firm, birth--death noise, and replacement of
exiting firms by newborns.  Heterogeneous mean-field theory gives the line above which the economy
keeps fluctuating, and a renewal law, exact for a node at constant field, predicts its turnover.  In the
fluctuating phase each firm behaves as a logistic unit driven by its own field, whose amplitude
grows as the square root of its degree.  A firm's growth variance is then the sum of a fitness floor,
nearly independent of size, and a demographic part that falls with size, so the model's exponent,
$\zeta_1=0.20$--$0.25$ depending on the shortest lives kept, is the effective slope of a crossover from a
slope near $-0.4$ to slope $0$.  It is set by a single variable, the depth of the economy in the fluctuating phase,
and economies that compete moderately and whose mean firm grows slowly lie in the band of depths where it matches
the data.  We show that for any such two-part law
$\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$, with $w$ the demographic share of the variance, and that the ratio
curve bends below $q$ only if the floor differs between firms of equal size, and always does when the law
of the floor does not change with size.  The floor contains the climb
from the newborn size and the final collapse, spread over the life, and lives end in dips of the field at
a memoryless rate given by Rice's formula and the exact extinction probability of a branching process, so
at fixed size the floor differs mainly because lives differ in length.  The floor's heterogeneity alone
predicts ratios $1.86$, $2.56$, $3.09$ against $1.77$, $2.35$, $2.78$ measured on the same lives ($1.95$, $2.55$,
$2.90$ in Compustat).  On a graph with equal degrees the floor of large firms stays small against their noise,
and the ratios return close to $q$: the degree, which sets the amplitude of a firm's field, makes the
differences of floor visible.  The same self-limitation that produces the decline caps firm size, so the model
has no Zipf law and the decline spans about two decades rather than four.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but much more erratically than a sum of
independent parts would.  If $v$ is the volatility of a firm's growth rate and $\Sb$ its mean size
over its life, the conditional moments in Compustat scale as
\begin{equation}
\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q},
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
with $\zeta_q=0.20(1)$, $0.39(2)$, $0.51(3)$, $0.58(4)$ for $q=1,\dots,4$,
over four decades of size \cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002,moran2024}.  The ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.95$, $2.55$, $2.90$ lie below $q$ \cite{moran2024}.  If a firm's volatility were a
function of its size times a random factor with a size-independent law, the ratios would be exactly
$q$; the bend means that the dispersion of volatilities among firms of equal size grows with size.
Moran, Secchi and Bouchaud \cite{moran2024} (MSB) add further facts: the volatility divided by its
size-conditional mean has a universal distribution with a power-law tail of index $b\simeq4.6$,
rescaled growth rates stay fat-tailed at every size, multi-year growth is persistent, and a firm's
co-movement with the aggregate rises with its size.  Firm sizes follow Zipf's law \cite{axtell2001}.

Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of subunits with independent shocks
\cite{wyartbouchaud2003,gabaix2011}, give $\zeta_1<1/2$ when the subunit sizes are broad, but in
their scaling regime the law of the rescaled volatility does not depend on size, so
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$; MSB show that they also fail the universality of the volatility distribution and
the absence of Gaussianisation \cite{moran2024}.  Data that include very small firms show a steep
decline, close to slope $-1/2$, for small firms and a nearly flat relation for large ones
\cite{fontanelli2025}, which suggests a crossover rather than a single power law.

Here we ask whether interactions between firms can produce these facts.  Our starting point is the
disordered generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV) model, whose strongly interacting phase keeps
fluctuating: species exchange the roles of rare and abundant, and each behaves as a logistic unit
driven by a coloured Gaussian field \cite{roy2020,mallmin2024,arnouxbunin2024}.  We make three
changes to adapt it to firms.  Interactions and self-limitation are measured in units of the mean
firm, so that the economy has no preferred size and grows at a rate the dynamics selects.  Firms
sit on a scale-free network, as production and competition networks do
\cite{acemoglu2012,carvalho2019}.  And the immigration that keeps species from extinction is
replaced by birth--death noise with turnover: a firm that reaches zero exits and a newborn takes its
place.  The model has five parameters: the mean $\mu$ and spread $\sigma$ of the interactions, the
degree tail $\alpha$ and mean degree $C$, and the noise $D$.  Anomalous moment scaling has been derived
for GLV communities before: with multiplicative environmental noise and migration, abundances are
power-law distributed and obey Taylor's law, $\langle N^{1+p}\rangle\propto\langle N\rangle^{1+D/T}$ with
a single exponent for every $p$ \cite{alhiyasat2026}; the mean-field equation there is the
Bouchaud--M\'ezard wealth model \cite{bouchaudmezard2000} with GLV interactions.  That law concerns
the moments of an abundance, with an exponent independent of the order; ours concerns the moments of
the growth volatility conditioned on size, whose exponents grow with $q$ and bend below $q\zeta_1$.
Neither network structure nor turnover enters there.

Our results are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The phase diagram (Sec.~\ref{sec:phase}): heterogeneous mean-field theory gives the line
  $\sigma_c$ above which the composition of the economy keeps changing, in closed form to first
  order in the degree variance, and its shift with the noise, $\sigma_c(D)$, from a static closure
  built on the renewal law of a single node at constant field, which also predicts the turnover rate;
  simulations place the onset within $0.05$ of the line.
\item One firm is enough (Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}): a firm driven by its own measured field and its own
  noise reproduces the size--volatility statistics of the full economy, and the field follows
  the degree-resolved mean-field law: its mean falls and its temporal amplitude grows as $\sqrt\kappa$ with
  the relative degree $\kappa$.
\item A volatility law (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}): a firm's growth variance is a fitness floor, nearly
  independent of size, plus demographic noise filtered by the firm's relaxation.  The exponent
  $\zeta_1$ is the effective slope of the crossover between them.
\item The multiscaling mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:multiscaling}), our main result: for any law of the
  form floor plus size-dependent part, $\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$ up to a composition term, and the ratio curve
  bends below $q$ only if the floor differs between firms of equal size.  The floor is a plateau
  part plus the climb from the newborn size and the final collapse spread over the life; lives end in
  dips of the field at a memoryless rate that we derive, so at fixed size the floor varies mainly with the
  life length.  Its heterogeneity predicts the measured ratio curve.  The degree keeps the floor of
  large firms large against their noise; on a graph with equal degrees the ratios return close to $q$.
\item A comparison with every MSB fact (Sec.~\ref{sec:facts}) and a structural limit: the
  self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also caps firm size, so the decline spans two
  decades, from the newborn size to the plateau, and the model has no Zipf law.
\end{enumerate}
Derivations, numerical checks and robustness tests are in the appendices.  Every number and figure
is printed or drawn by a notebook of the companion code \cite{code}; the appendix captions name it.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We consider $N$ firms with sizes $x_i$, $i=1,\dots,N$, and call $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ the mean firm.
The firms sit on the nodes of a configuration-model graph with degree law $p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and
mean degree $C$, and their sizes evolve as
\begin{equation}
dx_i=x_i\Big[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\sum_jJ_{ij}\frac{x_j}{m}\Big]dt+\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}\;dW_i ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where the $W_i$ are independent Wiener processes.  The couplings are $J_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$,
with $A$ the adjacency matrix and $z_{ij}$ independent standard Gaussians ($z_{ij}$ and $z_{ji}$ are
uncorrelated); $\mu<0$ means that competition dominates on average.

Since $x_i=0$ is absorbing and Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} has no source, firms would only disappear, so to
keep the economy stationary we replace a firm that reaches zero by a newborn of size $Dm$ with the same
node and couplings.  The number of firms then stays $N$, entries occur at the rate of exits set by the
dynamics, and $Dm$ is the size at which the noise $\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}$ equals $x_i$.  In
ecological models extinct species come back through a constant immigration \cite{altieri2021,bunin2017};
here a firm enters only when and where another one has left.

Apart from the normalisation, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} is the standard GLV: the carrying capacity and the
unit of interaction are both the mean firm, and the noise is the birth--death noise of stochastic
population models \cite{altieri2021}, with unit $Dm$.  Since every term is homogeneous of degree one
in the sizes, the economy has no preferred scale.  The mean firm grows at a rate that the dynamics
selects, and a firm is large or small only compared with the others.  The noise unit grows along with
the economy, and one can read it as an indivisible piece of business, such as a contract or a
customer, whose size scales with the mean firm.

In terms of the relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} becomes
\begin{equation}
dS_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i+O(N^{-1/2}),
\label{eq:onefirm}
\end{equation}
with the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\bar f(t).
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
Here $\bar f$ is the growth rate of the mean firm,
\begin{equation}
\dot m=m\,\bar f,\qquad \bar f=\frac1N\sum_iS_i\Big(1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j\Big)+D\nu(t),
\label{eq:mean}
\end{equation}
up to noise of order $N^{-1/2}$, and
\begin{equation}
\nu(t)=\frac1N\sum_i\sum_k\delta\big(t-t_{i,k}\big)
\label{eq:nu}
\end{equation}
is the number of entries per node and per unit time, where $t_{i,k}$ are the times at which the firm on
node $i$ exits and is replaced.  The $O(N^{-1/2})$ term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}
keeps $\sum_iS_i=N$; in relative units firms exit at $S_i=0$ and enter at $S_i=D$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a logistic equation whose carrying capacity moves in time.  If the
field on a firm stays at a value $\Phi>0$, the firm relaxes at rate $\Phi$ to the plateau $x_i=m\Phi$;
if $\Phi<0$, it shrinks until it exits.  For $C\to\infty$, and if the growth rate self-averages, a
rescaling of size and time maps the relative sizes onto the standard GLV with demographic noise
\cite{altieri2021}, with renormalised parameters (Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  On the complete graph,
and with an immigration floor in place of the noise, it reduces to the chaotic-turnover model of
Refs.~\cite{bunin2017,mallmin2024}.  What the normalisation adds is a growth rate that comes out of the
model, and observables that are measured relative to the mean firm, as they are in firm data.

\paragraph{Numerics and protocol.}  We integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} in relative form by
Euler--Maruyama with step $0.01$, discard $t<100$, and record sizes every $\Delta t=0.5$, which we take
as one year (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}), over a window of length $480$.  As MSB do, we use an
unbalanced panel.  A \emph{life} runs from a birth to the next exit at the same node, and lives cut by
the window are kept.  A life recorded at $n+1$ times has growth rates $g_t=\ln S_{t+1}-\ln S_t$, and we define its
volatility and size as
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|g_t-\bar g\big|,\qquad
\Sb=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}S_t,
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
with $\bar g$ the mean of the $g_t$; the factor $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ makes $v$ the standard deviation for
Gaussian $g$.  We keep
only lives with at least $n_{\min}$ growth rates, with $n_{\min}=20$ unless stated otherwise.  Compustat
firms have $46$ growth rates on average, and MSB find the same results when they keep only firms with at
least $20$ \cite{moran2024}; in the model, lives with few growth rates are mostly newborns
(Sec.~\ref{sec:facts}).  Exponents are least-squares slopes of $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ against $\ln\Sb$ over
$25$ equal-count bins, with all bins included.  The reference point is $\alpha=2.5$, $\sigma=1.70$,
$D=0.1$, $C=200$, $\mu=-1.76$.  The facts of Sec.~\ref{sec:facts} come from eight economies with
$N=32000$; one of them goes through a collective burst and is excluded (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).  The
theory tests use two economies with $N=16000$.

\section{Phase diagram}
\label{sec:phase}

\paragraph{Deterministic limit.}  For $C\to\infty$ the field on a firm of relative degree
$\kappa=k/C$ is Gaussian.  Heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory (HDMFT) replaces the network by
one process per degree class, closed by averages over neighbours, which are drawn in proportion to
their degree \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026}:
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)=1-\bar f(t)+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
\label{eq:hdmft}
\end{equation}
with $\bar B$ the degree-weighted mean size and $\xi$ a Gaussian process whose correlation is the
degree-weighted correlation of sizes.  On the reference graph this rule would link pairs of hubs with
probability above one, and the classes are coupled by the soft-configuration kernel instead
\cite{patil2026} (Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}).  Without noise, the stationary sizes
are clipped Gaussians, and the unique fixed point of the composition loses stability when the Perron
root of $\sigma^2\varphi_pK_{pr}$ reaches one, with $\varphi_p$ the fraction of surviving firms in class $p$
and $K_{pr}$ the class kernel.  On an uncorrelated graph the stability operator has rank one, and to
first order in the degree variance
\begin{equation}
\sigma_c\simeq\sqrt2\Big[1-\frac{\Var\kappa}{2}\Big(1-\frac{\mu^2}{2\pi}\Big)\Big]
\label{eq:sigmac}
\end{equation}
(Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}).  Degree heterogeneity lowers the threshold because hubs feel a field of amplitude
$\propto\sqrt\kappa$.  A mean interaction of either sign raises it: it makes the mean field depend on the
degree, which spreads the classes' distances from extinction, and this spread stabilises the fixed point;
to this order only $\mu^2$ enters.  To the same order the line on which the
economy stops growing moves less: where it meets the instability line, at
$\sigma\simeq\sqrt2$, its shift is about a quarter of that of $\sigma_c$.

\paragraph{Renewal law.}  With noise and turnover, a node whose field is constant is a renewal
process: its successive lives are independent and identically distributed.  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm}
at constant $\Phi=h$ is a one-dimensional diffusion with scale function $s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,du$, where
$s'(u)=\exp[-(2/D)(hu-u^2/2)]$, and by the renewal--reward theorem the fraction of time the node spends
at size $x$ is
\begin{gather}
p(x\mid h)=\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)\,T(D)},\nonumber\\
T(D)=\int_0^\infty\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)}\,dx,
\label{eq:renewal}
\end{gather}
with turnover rate $1/T(D)$.  The law has two parts: a plateau $\propto x^{-1}e^{-(x-h)^2/D}$, Gaussian of
variance $D/2$ around $h$ for $h\gg\sqrt D$, and below $D$ a nearly flat density of newborns drifting to exit.  Establishment probabilities and
exit times follow from the same quadratures and agree with direct simulation to $3\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).

\paragraph{Instability line with noise.}  Replacing the clipped Gaussian by the renewal law in the
static self-consistency gives the mean sizes $\langle S\rangle_h$ and the susceptibility
$\chi=\partial\langle S\rangle_h/\partial h$; the static state is stable while the Perron root of
$\sigma^2{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K$ is below one.  Noise smooths $\chi$ and raises the line almost linearly with
$D$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phaselines}): from $1.460$ to $1.577$ at $\alpha=2.5$ and from $1.399$ to $1.551$ at
$\alpha=6$ between $D=0.003$ and $0.3$; at $D=0.003$ it agrees with the deterministic line to $0.002$
($1.399$ against $1.400$ at $\alpha=6$, $1.413$ against $\sqrt2$ on the complete graph).  The closure
treats each node's field as frozen, so it is controlled only below the line; in simulations at $\alpha=6$ and
$D=0.1$, slow collective fluctuations of the composition set in between $\sigma=1.40$ and $1.50$, around the
predicted $1.451$, as a smooth crossover rather than a sharp transition (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  The same theory
predicts the turnover rate to $1\%$ below $\sigma_c$ and to about $2\%$ above it, where it is no longer
controlled ($\alpha=6$; Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  Turnover is set by
the nodes with $h<0$, where a newborn's life hardly depends on $D$, and is nearly independent of the
noise.  Figure~\ref{fig:phase} shows the phase diagram in the plane of the mean interaction and the
disorder.  On the reference graph competition raises $\sigma_c$ strongly, from $0.83$ at $\mu=0$ to $1.68$ at
$\mu=-2.5$, because its degrees are the most spread, and with them the mean field across classes; on the
complete graph the line does not depend on $\mu$, and at $\alpha=6$ it barely does.  Under strong
competition the zero-growth line is almost the same on the three graphs, within $0.05$ for $\mu\le-1.25$;
under weak competition it is lower on the reference graph ($0.50$ against $0.63$ and $0.64$ at $\mu=0$).  The reference point, $\sigma=1.70$ against $\sigma_c(0.1)=1.497$, lies in the fluctuating phase, close to
the static estimate of the zero-growth line ($1.72$ at $D=0.1$), which is not controlled there.  In
simulation the zero-growth line nevertheless lies within $0.04$ of the static estimate for $\mu$ from $-0.5$ to $-2.5$, and
the region where the facts of Sec.~\ref{sec:facts} hold is a band along it (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).

\section{One firm is enough}
\label{sec:onefirm}

Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is exact; its content is that the field can be treated as given, i.e.\
that a firm's own noise does not feed back on its field through the network.  We test this
directly.  We store the fields $\Phi_i(t)$ of two reference economies, integrate
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} for every node with its measured field and fresh noise, and compare the life
panels.  The exponents agree to $0.01$ and the ratios to $0.05$ for every minimum life length, and the
exit rates agree exactly (Table~\ref{tab:reduction} and Fig.~\ref{fig:reduction}).  The theory of the
size--volatility statistics is therefore a one-firm problem, given the statistics of the field.


The field follows the degree-resolved law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}.  Over the window, a node's mean field
falls with its degree, from $0.5$ at $\kappa\simeq0.4$ to $-2.4$ at $\kappa\simeq2$: hubs compete with more
firms.  Its temporal amplitude grows as $\kappa^{0.54}$, against $\kappa^{1/2}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft},
and decorrelates over about $4.5$ time units.  Within a degree class the mean field is nearly
symmetric (skewness $-0.3$) but fatter-tailed than a Gaussian (excess kurtosis $2.0$).  The degree therefore sets both how long a firm lives and how strongly it
is shaken while alive; both enter the multiscaling (Sec.~\ref{sec:multiscaling}).

Three statistics of the field are enough.  Driving each firm instead by a Gaussian field with its node's
time mean and temporal amplitude and one correlation function for all nodes gives, for lives with at least
$20$ growth rates, $\zeta_1=0.23$ and ratios $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for
the full model on the same economies.  With the amplitude at $c\sqrt\kappa$ and the means reshuffled among
nodes of similar degree the panel changes little ($0.28$; $1.81$, $2.42$, $2.86$), but with the means at their
mean in the degree class the ratios return towards $q$ ($1.93$, $2.79$, $3.56$;
\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).  The essential input is the spread of the mean field among firms
of equal degree, which sets how high a firm's plateau is and how long it lives.

\section{The volatility law}
\label{sec:law}

\paragraph{Decomposition.}  In logs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} reads
$d\ln S=[\Phi-S-D/(2S)]\,dt+\sqrt{D/S}\,dW$.  Write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$, where $y_F$ solves the noise-free
equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ from the same initial size.  To linear order $\delta$ is an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process that relaxes at the local rate $S$ and is driven by noise of variance
$D/S$.  Treating the two parts as independent, the variance of a firm's yearly growth over its life
is
\begin{equation}
\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$ is the growth variance of $y_F$.  The demographic part $B$ tends to $D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle$
for slowly relaxing firms and to $D\langle S^{-2}\rangle$ for fast ones.

\paragraph{Test.}  For every life with at least $20$ growth rates in the two reference economies we
compute $V_F$ by integrating the noise-free equation along the life's measured field, and $B$ from
its sizes.  Equation~\eqref{eq:law} reproduces the measured volatility to within $5\%$ above the lowest decile of $\Sb$, and $9\%$ in
it (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}a; Table~\ref{tab:law}).  The field part $\sqrt{V_F}$ stays at
$0.32$--$0.37$ across sizes, although $\sqrt{\Var\Delta\ln\Phi}$ falls from $0.92$ to $0.44$: a firm does
not follow the fast part of its field, least of all a small firm, whose field is low and therefore
fluctuates strongly in relative terms.

\paragraph{A crossover.}  The fitness part is nearly flat in size,
$\sqrt{V_F}\simeq0.32$--$0.37$, as the sum of a plateau part that falls with size and an entry and exit
part that rises (Sec.~\ref{sec:multiscaling}).  The demographic part $\sqrt B$ falls as $\Sb^{-b/2}$, with $b=0.66$--$0.87$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:multiscaling}; $b=1$ for $B\simeq D\Delta t/\Sb$).  The size--volatility relation is therefore a
crossover,
\begin{equation}
v^2\simeq v_F^2+B(\Sb),
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
from a steep decline where noise dominates to slope $0$ where the floor does.  The two parts are equal
at $\Sb\simeq1.6$.  The decomposition has the two-regime shape reported for French firms that include
very small ones \cite{fontanelli2025}, but the measured life panel does not resolve the two regimes:
its local slopes by fifth of size are $0.26$, $0.17$, $0.14$, $0.23$, $0.27$ (production run, $n_{\min}=5$),
with neither a steep small-firm end nor a flat top.  $\zeta_1\simeq0.2$ is the effective slope of the whole
range.  The crossover scale grows with $D$, so the effective
exponent does too: at $\sigma=1.70$, $\zeta_1=0.20$, $0.23$ and $0.25$ for $D=0.1$, $0.15$ and $0.2$
($n_{\min}=5$; Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).

\section{The multiscaling mechanism}
\label{sec:multiscaling}

\paragraph{An identity.}  Let a firm's squared volatility be the sum of a floor $u_i$ and a part
$B(\Sb)$ common to all firms of the same size, up to an estimation factor $\epsilon_i$ independent of
both: $v_i^2=(u_i+B(\Sb_i))\,\epsilon_i^2$.  Let $b=-d\ln B/d\ln\Sb$ be the local slope of the size-dependent part
and $w=B/(u+B)$ its share of the variance.  Differentiating $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ gives
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q,\qquad
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\,w\mid\Sb\big]}{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb\big]},
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
provided the law of $u$ at fixed size does not change with size (the general case adds a composition
term $c_q$; Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  For the demographic part of Eq.~\eqref{eq:crossover} the measured slope is
$b=0.66$--$0.87$ across the range, below the value $1$ of $B\simeq D\Delta t/\Sb$ because $B$ is dominated by
the low excursions of a life.  The exponent is thus about $0.4$ times the noise share, weighted by
$v^q$: largest where noise dominates ($w\to1$) and zero where the floor does ($w\to0$).

\paragraph{When the curve bends.}  Since $\langle w\rangle_q=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$ with $Y=u+B$, and
$\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is convex in $q$, $\langle w\rangle_q$ is non-increasing in $q$, and constant only if $Y$ is
the same for all firms of the given size.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q,
\label{eq:bend}
\end{equation}
with equality, when the law of the floor does not change with size, if and only if the floor does not
vary between firms of equal size.  With the composition term only one direction survives: a floor
that is the same for every firm of a given size, however it depends on size, gives a volatility law
of fixed shape and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, like a granular model, while the converse needs control of $c_q$,
which on the network adds to the bend (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  The bend requires heterogeneity of the floor, and it is
strongest where the floor dominates, at large sizes, because there the volatilities of firms with
different floors differ most.  The estimation factor $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratios.  If $\ln Y$ is
Gaussian at fixed size with variance $s^2$, the bend is explicit,
\begin{gather}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}\simeq q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\nonumber\\
s^2=\Var\big(\ln(u+B)\mid\Sb\big)\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb),
\label{eq:lognormal}
\end{gather}
the last form for a narrow floor.  A heterogeneous floor bends the curve only where it is not small
against the demographic part.

\paragraph{What the floor is made of.}  The volatility law gives every life its own floor $V_F$, the
growth variance of the noise-free firm along the life's measured field (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}).  A life
with at least $20$ growth rates usually begins as a newborn at $S\simeq D$ and ends at an exit, so its
noise-free path climbs to a plateau, follows the field there and collapses in the final dip.  Splitting
the path where the noise-free size first and last reaches half its median,
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
with $V_{\rm core}$ the growth variance on the plateau and $E_{\rm in}$, $E_{\rm out}$ the sums of squared growth rates
over the climb and the collapse.  For a logistic climb from $S_0$ to a plateau $h$,
$E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$, and for a collapse in a field that has fallen to $-\varphi$,
$E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$: both grow with the plateau (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).
The two parts of the floor move in opposite directions.  The core falls from $0.084$ in the smallest fifth
of sizes to $0.052$ in the largest, while the entry and exit part grows from a quarter of $V_F$ to $62\%$:
large firms climb further from the newborn size and collapse from higher.  The flat floor of
Sec.~\ref{sec:law} is their sum.

\paragraph{Why floors differ at fixed size.}  Within a size bin the floor is broad, $\Var(\ln V_F\mid\Sb)$
from $0.5$ in the smallest fifth to $0.9$ in the largest, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} says why: the same climb
and collapse are spread over lives of very different lengths.  Within size bins the life length explains
$26$--$62\%$ of $\Var\ln V_F$ in the four upper fifths, more than the degree ($19$--$41\%$; $8\%$ against
$14\%$ in the smallest fifth), and $\Var(\ln n\mid\Sb)$ grows from $0.08$ to $0.91$ with size.  The spread of
life lengths follows from how lives end.  An established firm dies in a dip of its field.  Inside the dip
the firm is small, the logistic term is negligible, and the firm is a Feller branching process, whose
extinction probability is exact:
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm kill}=\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{D\,J}\Big),\qquad J=\int\frac{dt}{S_{\rm det}(t)},
\label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}
with $S_{\rm det}$ the noise-free path through the dip.  Over the $446\,554$ dips of established firms in the
two economies, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill} predicts a kill fraction of $0.725$ against $0.722$ observed, with no
fitted parameter.  Dips begin at the downcrossings of zero, which occur at Rice's rate
$\sqrt{-\rho''(0)}\,e^{-u^2/2}/2\pi$, with $u=m/f$ the node's mean field over its temporal amplitude.  Lives
are therefore memoryless, with a mean that grows steeply with $u$, from about $23$ to $220$ growth rates
across deciles of $u$ (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  Small firms sit on nodes whose lives barely exceed the
cut $n\ge20$; large firms on nodes with long exponential lives, whose lengths spread over a decade.

\paragraph{The noise share.}  It falls from $\langle w\rangle_1=0.79$ in the smallest fifth to $0.43$ in the largest, and its fall
with $q$ grows with size: within size bins $\langle w\rangle_4/\langle w\rangle_1=0.96$, $0.92$, $0.86$, $0.79$, $0.62$
(Table~\ref{tab:fifths}).  In the four lower fifths the local exponents of the law agree with
$(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$ to about $10\%$; in the largest the composition term dominates, because the
largest lives also have the largest degrees (mean $\kappa$ $1.01$ in the fourth fifth, $1.51$ in the fifth).

\paragraph{Test.}  We compute the exponents of the same lives under three laws, with the growth
standard deviation as the volatility (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}c).  Measured, $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.77$, $2.35$,
$2.78$ ($1.80$, $2.42$, $2.88$ with the estimator of Sec.~\ref{sec:model}, and $1.78$, $2.35$, $2.76$ in the
production run of Sec.~\ref{sec:facts}).  With each life's own floor and the demographic part replaced by its mean in the life's size
bin, so that the floor is the only source of dispersion, the law predicts $1.86$, $2.56$, $3.09$.  With
the floor replaced instead by its bin mean, the ratios are $2.08$, $3.65$, $6.58$: without the spread of
the floor the curve lies above $q$, because the per-life estimate of the demographic part has its own
dispersion.  The bend of the ratio curve is therefore carried by the heterogeneity of the floor, through
both its spread at fixed size and the shift of its law with size, which this test does not separate.
Splitting the floor as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} and keeping one part at a time at its own value, the other at
its bin mean, the ratios are $1.98$, $2.94$, $3.85$ with only the core heterogeneous and $1.90$, $2.69$, $3.33$
with only the entry and exit part, against $1.85$, $2.55$, $3.07$ with both (same lives, current economies;
\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).  The bend is carried mostly by the climb and the collapse,
through the spread of life lengths.

\paragraph{Ablation.}  On a random regular graph every firm has degree $C$.  The floor has the same
make-up, its entry and exit part growing from a quarter to half of it, and the life length explains up
to half of its spread at fixed size.  But the floor is small, and it falls with size, from $0.07$ to
$0.03$ in mean, so that even the largest lives remain dominated by their own noise
($\langle w\rangle_1=0.86$ to $0.68$).  The dispersion of their total variance stays small, $s^2=0.008$ to
$0.05$ from the smallest to the largest fifth, against $0.02$ to $0.25$ on the power-law graph
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}b), and the
ratio curve is close to $q$: $1.93$, $2.78$, $3.54$ measured, $1.98$, $2.95$, $3.88$ from the floor alone,
with $\zeta_1=0.40$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:lognormal} with the measured $s^2$ gives $\langle w\rangle_4/\langle w\rangle_1$ to
within $0.06$ in every fifth of both graphs.  In $s^2\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln V_F\mid\Sb)$ the two graphs differ in
both factors: in the largest fifth, within size bins, $(1-w)^2$ is about three times larger on the power-law
graph and $\Var(\ln V_F\mid\Sb)$ about twice.  The degree acts on both.  A better connected firm feels a field
of larger amplitude, which keeps its core up, and more frequent killing dips, which shorten its life (the
correlation of $\ln n$ with $\ln\kappa$ within size bins falls from $-0.16$ in the smallest fifth to $-0.49$ in the
largest) and raise its entry
and exit part.  This keeps the floor of the larger firms at $V_F\simeq0.13$ while their noise falls.  The life
length makes the floor differ between firms of equal size; the degree makes the difference large against
the noise.

\paragraph{What it does not.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Short lives.}  The results differ for very short lives.  With all lives of at least two
  growth rates, MSB's baseline sample, in which Compustat firms still have $46$ on average, the
  ratios lie above $q$ ($2.42$, $3.96$, $5.43$): very short lives are newborns dominated by their own
  noise, whose volatility estimates are dispersed most among the smallest firms.  The bend appears
  from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).
\item \emph{Large firms are too volatile and too fat-tailed.}  Volatility falls by a factor $2.2$
  over $1.4$ decades ($2.8$ over two decades for lives with at least five growth rates, which reach
  smaller sizes) against $5.5$ over four in Compustat.  The rescaled growth rates of small firms
  are nearly Gaussian and those of large firms strongly fat-tailed, and multi-year persistence is
  confined to the largest firms.  Small firms are moved by near-Gaussian, mean-reverting birth--death
  noise, large firms by rare and persistent dips of their field.
\item \emph{The tail of the volatility distribution} is too thin: $b\simeq6.8$ against $4.6$, which MSB
  find for all firms and for those with at least $20$ growth rates.  With all lives the model gives
  $b=4.4$, but that is an artefact of short lives: a null with iid Laplace growth and no differences
  between firms gives $4.3$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).
\item \emph{Aggregate volatility} is several times too large and does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$ over the
  sizes we simulate.  Occasionally an economy enters a collective burst, with aggregate volatility
  several times the typical one, in which volatility no longer depends on size ($5$ of the $80$
  economies of our robustness runs; Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).
\item \emph{Sizes and demography.}  The size distribution has no Pareto tail, newborns have extreme
  infant mortality, and small established firms shrink on average.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

An economy of firms that compete on a heterogeneous network and are subject to birth--death noise
reproduces the shape of the size--volatility relation of firms: a monotone decline with effective
exponent $0.20$--$0.25$ and a ratio curve $\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ that bends below $q$.  The theory is a one-firm problem:
a logistic unit driven by a field whose mean falls and whose amplitude grows as the square root of its
degree, plus its own noise.  The exponent is the effective slope of a crossover between a demographic part
that falls with size and a nearly flat fitness floor, the two-regime shape of data that include small
firms \cite{fontanelli2025}; in the model the two regimes appear in the decomposition but are not
resolved in the measured panel.  The multiscaling has a simple origin: a volatility law of the form
floor plus size-dependent part gives $\zeta_q=(q/2)b\langle w\rangle_q$, and the curve bends below $q$ only
when the floor differs between firms of equal size.  Granular models give a fixed shape and ratios
$q$.  In the model the floor differs because lives differ in length: it contains the climb after entry and
the collapse before exit, and lives end at memoryless dips of the field.  The heterogeneity of the network
keeps the floor of the larger, better connected firms large against their noise, which makes these
differences visible.

The identity~\eqref{eq:identity} does not depend on the network model, and the mechanism suggests two
tests on firm data.  First, the dispersion of volatilities among firms of equal size should grow with
size, and the length of a firm's observed life should explain a growing share of it, together with a
measure of its exposure to others, such as its number of customers, suppliers or competitors.  Second,
trimming the years after entry and before exit should move the ratio curve towards $q$ and steepen the
decline: in the model, keeping only the plateau of each life moves the ratios from $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ to
$1.92$, $2.74$, $3.44$ and $\zeta_1$ from $0.25$ to $0.38$ (lives with at least $20$ growth rates;
\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

The model fails where the self-limitation it relies on bites: the range of the decline is two decades
rather than four, sizes have no Pareto tail, and large firms are too volatile.  Open problems are a two-time theory with noise and
turnover, which would give the field's correlation above $\sigma_c(D)$ and with it the floor $v_F$ and
$\zeta_1(\sigma,D)$ without simulation; the rising co-movement with size, which involves the common mode
of a finite economy; and a dynamics that decouples the volatility decline from the size cap.

\section{Relative sizes and the standard model}
\label{app:relative}

Write $x_i=mS_i$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}.  By It\^o's rule, with $\bar f=\dot m/m$ the drift of the
mean firm,
\begin{align}
dS_i={}&S_i\big[F_i-\bar f\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i\nonumber\\
&-\frac{S_i}{N}\sum_j\sqrt{DS_j}\,dW_j+O(N^{-1})\,dt,\nonumber\\
F_i={}&1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j ,
\label{eq:app-rel}
\end{align}
and $\bar f=N^{-1}\sum_iS_iF_i$ is the value that keeps $\sum_iS_i=N$ between entries.  The third term, of order
$N^{-1/2}$, is the share of the aggregate noise that each firm carries.  Each entry adds $D$ to $\sum_iS_i$, i.e.\
$Dm$ to the economy, and multiplies every relative size by $1-D/N$; at a rate $N\nu$ of entries this is a drift
$-D\nu S_i$, so the growth rate of the mean firm is $\bar f=N^{-1}\sum_iS_iF_i+D\nu$, the value used in the field
throughout.  This is a replicator equation
with birth--death noise; with $\Phi_i=F_i+S_i-\bar f$ it is Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}.  If $\bar f$ self-averages to a constant
$f^*$ at large $N$, the substitution $S=ry$, $t'=rt$ with $r=1-f^*$ gives
\begin{equation}
dy_i=y_i\Big[1-y_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}y_j\Big]dt'+\sqrt{(D/r^2)\,y_i}\,dW_i' ,
\end{equation}
the GLV with demographic noise of Ref.~\cite{altieri2021} on the same graph, with noise $D/r^2$ and newborns
of size $D/r$.  The
stationary statistics of relative sizes are therefore those of the standard model with renormalised
noise; the normalisation adds the growth rate $f^*$ as an output, a common mode $\bar f(t)-f^*$ at
finite $N$, and observables measured relative to the mean firm.

\section{Heterogeneous mean-field theory and the deterministic stability line}
\label{app:hdmft}

\paragraph{Degree classes.}  Group the nodes into classes $p$ with fractions $\rho_p$ and degrees
$k_p$, and let $P_{pr}$ be the probability that a node of class $p$ links to one of class $r$, with
$k_p/N=\sum_r\rho_rP_{pr}$.  The uncorrelated choice $P_{pr}=k_pk_r/(NC)$ exceeds one for pairs of hubs
at $\alpha=2.5$; we use the soft-configuration kernel $P_{pr}=h_ph_r/(h_ph_r+C/N)$ with hidden degrees fixed
by the degree constraint \cite{patil2026}, and write $K_{pr}=N\rho_rP_{pr}/C$.

\paragraph{Effective process.}  For $C\to\infty$ the mean part of the interaction field on a node of
class $p$ tends to $\mu B_p$ and, by the dynamical cavity argument
\cite{bunin2017,galla2018,aguirrelopez}, the random part to a Gaussian process of covariance
$\sigma^2q_p(t,t')$, with
\begin{gather}
B_p(t)=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)],\nonumber\\
q_p(t,t')=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\label{eq:app-closure}
\end{gather}
At zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.  Each class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with field
$\Phi_p(t)=1-\bar f(t)+\mu B_p(t)+\sigma\eta_p(t)$, $\E[\eta_p(t)\eta_p(t')]=q_p(t,t')$, closed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} and by $\sum_p\rho_p\E[S_p]=1$, which fixes $\bar f$.  For the bilinear kernel the
closures factorise, $B_\kappa=\kappa\bar B$ and $q_\kappa=\kappa Q$, with $\kappa=k/C$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}: the field's mean falls linearly in $\kappa$ under competition and its amplitude grows as
$\sqrt\kappa$.

\paragraph{Stability line.}  Without noise, a relaxed composition has $S_p^*=\sigma\sqrt{q_p^*}
[\Delta_p+z]_+$ with $\Delta_p=(a+\mu B_p^*)/(\sigma\sqrt{q_p^*})$, $a=1-f^*$ and $z$ standard Gaussian.  With the
truncated moments $w_n(\Delta)=\int_{-\Delta}^\infty(\Delta+z)^n\phi(z)\,dz$, the survival fraction is
$\varphi_p=w_0(\Delta_p)$.  A persistent perturbation of variance $Y_r$ in class $r$ produces in class $p$ a
field of variance $\sigma^2K_{pr}Y_r$, which the survivors convert into $Y_p=\sigma^2\varphi_p\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r$; the
fixed point loses stability when the Perron root of $\sigma^2\varphi_pK_{pr}$ reaches one.  On a regular graph
this gives $\sigma_c=\sqrt2$ at zero reciprocity, independent of $\mu$, the onset of the fluctuating
phase of the GLV \cite{roy2020,mallmin2024}.  For the bilinear kernel the operator has rank one, and
the threshold is
\begin{gather}
\sigma_c^{-2}=\big\langle\kappa^2\PhiN(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle,\qquad \Delta_\kappa=A\kappa^{-1/2}+M\kappa^{1/2},\nonumber\\
\big\langle\kappa^2\Delta_\kappa w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle=0,\qquad M=\mu\big\langle\kappa^{3/2}w_1(\Delta_\kappa)\big\rangle .
\label{eq:app-sigmac}
\end{gather}
Writing $\kappa=1+\epsilon$ and expanding to second order in $\epsilon$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmac}.  At this
order $\mu$ enters only through $\mu^2$: the terms odd in $\mu$, which lower or raise the survival of the hubs,
cancel against the shift of the mean of $\Delta_\kappa$, and what remains is the variance $\mu^2\Var\kappa/(2\pi)$ of
$\Delta_\kappa$ across classes.  Since $\Delta w_1(\Delta)$ is convex at $\Delta=0$ while $w_0$ has no curvature there,
this spread lowers the mean of $\Delta_\kappa$ and with it the Perron root.  Beyond first order the dependence is
not even: at $\alpha=3.5$ the exact relation gives $\sigma_c=1.32$ at $\mu=1$ and $1.29$ at $\mu=-1$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:app-sigmac} agrees with the 60-class HDMFT to $0.002$ at $\alpha=6$ and $0.03$ at $\alpha=3.5$.  The
zero-growth line, obtained by imposing $a=1$, shifts in $\mu$ with the degree variance by $c(\sigma)\Var\kappa$, with
$c$ running from $0$ as $\sigma\to0$ to $5/4-\pi/2\simeq-0.32$ as $\sigma\to\sqrt2$.  There the regular-graph lines meet, at
$\mu=-1$, and the zero-growth line $\mu=\Delta/w_1(\Delta)-1$, $\sigma^2w_2(\Delta)=1$ has slope $d\mu/d\sigma=-\pi/\sqrt2$; converted to a
shift in $\sigma$ its shift is $0.14\Var\kappa$, about a quarter of the shift $(1-1/(2\pi))\Var\kappa/\sqrt2=0.59\Var\kappa$
of $\sigma_c$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:sigmac}.  The full
derivation, the tables against the 60-class theory and the simulated phase diagram of the
deterministic model are in \texttt{notebooks/02\_phase\_diagram.ipynb}.

\section{Demographic noise at constant field}
\label{app:constant}

\paragraph{Quadratures.}  At constant $\Phi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a diffusion with drift $S(\Phi-S)$ and diffusion
coefficient $DS$, absorbed at $0$.  Its scale density is $s'(y)=\exp[-(2/D)(\Phi y-y^2/2)]$, its scale function
$s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,du$ and its speed density $m(y)=2/(Dy\,s'(y))$ \cite{karlintaylor1981}.  A newborn reaches its plateau before exiting with
probability
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm est}=\frac{\int_0^Ds'(y)\,dy}{\int_0^\Phi s'(y)\,dy}\qquad(\Phi>D),
\end{equation}
and the mean time to exit from $S_0$ is
\begin{equation}
T(S_0)=2\int_0^{S_0}s'(y)\int_y^\infty\frac{dz}{Dz\,s'(z)}\,dy .
\end{equation}
For $-\Phi\gg\sqrt D$, $T$ is of order $1/|\Phi|$ and, for a newborn, nearly independent of $D$: near zero the
logistic term is negligible and the Feller survival probability from $S_0=D$ does not contain $D$ ($T=2.32$,
$2.16$, $1.92$ at $\Phi=-0.5$ and $D=0.01$, $0.1$, $0.3$).  At $\Phi=0$ the logistic term cuts the Feller tail
$2/t$ off at $t\sim D^{-1/2}$, and $T$ grows as $\ln(1/D)$ ($6.05$, $3.84$, $2.88$).  For
$\Phi>0$ it grows as $e^{\Phi^2/D}$, so an established firm exits through dips of its field rather than
through noise.  Table~\ref{tab:constant} compares the quadratures with direct simulation.  On the
plateau, linearising at $S=\Phi$ gives an Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process in $\ln S$ with rate $\Phi$, $\Var
S=D/2$ and $\Var g=(D/\Phi^2)(1-e^{-\Phi\Delta t})$, accurate for $\Phi\gtrsim1$.

\paragraph{Renewal law.}  With turnover the lives at a node of constant field are independent and
identically distributed.  By the renewal--reward theorem the fraction of time spent near $x$ is the
expected time one life spends there, the Green's function $G(D,x)=s(\min(x,D))\,m(x)$, divided by the
mean life $T(D)=\int_0^\infty G(D,x)\,dx$; this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:renewal}.  Against direct
simulation of one node at $D=0.1$ and $\Phi=-0.5,\dots,1$ it gives $\langle S\rangle$, $\langle S^2\rangle$ and the
turnover to within $0.5\%$ (\texttt{notebooks/02\_phase\_diagram.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Static closure and phase lines.}  When the field is static each node follows the renewal
law at its $h=a+\mu B_p+\sigma\sqrt{q_p}z$, and the self-consistency reads
\begin{gather}
B_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r},\qquad q_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r}^2,\nonumber\\
\sum_p\rho_p\E\langle S\rangle_{h_p}=1,
\end{gather}
with $\E$ the Gaussian average over $z$.  Counting the temporal fluctuations of $S$ in $q$ as if
quenched moves the lines by less than $0.015$.  Stability is the Perron condition with $\E\chi^2$, where
$\chi=\partial\langle S\rangle_h/\partial h$, in place of the survival fraction (without noise $\chi$ is the
survival step and $\E\chi^2=\varphi$).  Table~\ref{tab:phase} tests the
turnover against simulation.  The share of the field variance that moves in time grows from $0.025$
at $\sigma=1.2$ to $0.138$ at $\sigma=1.7$; the static closure underestimates it below $\sigma_c$, by a factor
of about $2$ at $\sigma=1.2$ and $5$ at $\sigma_c$, because it omits the collective amplification of slow field
fluctuations.  The two-time closure includes it, but at $C=200$ only in part.

\paragraph{Two-time closure.}  At the reference point we solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} in the stationary
state, $q_p(t,t')=q_p(t-t')$, by sampling the effective process \cite{eissfeller1992,roy2019}: draw $\eta_p$ from
$q_p$, integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with exit and re-entry, measure $\E[S_r(t)S_r(t+\tau)]$ and iterate
(\texttt{scripts/two\_time\_dmft.py}).  We use $31$ classes, of equal population in the bulk and of equal degree
mass in the tail; each sampled node carries its own degree, its field is scaled by $k/k_p$, and the
neighbour averages are weighted by degree.  Evaluated with the measured sizes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure}
gives the temporal and frozen parts of the measured field to within $4\%$ and $6\%$ from $\kappa\simeq0.4$ to
$4.7$.  The self-consistent solution reproduces the mean field to within the difference between the two
economies ($0.63$ against $0.59$ and $0.31$ at $\kappa\simeq0.4$), but its temporal amplitude is $0.71$--$0.81$ of
the measured one in every class ($0.96$ against $1.34$ at $\kappa\simeq0.4$), and its correlation is gone by
$\tau=16$, where the measured one is still $0.05$--$0.13$.  The life panel of the solution has $\zeta_1=0.34$
and ratios $1.85$, $2.53$, $3.07$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model.

The loss comes from a spread the closure leaves out.  At $C=200$ a few strongly fluctuating neighbours
dominate the temporal field of a node, so its amplitude varies between nodes of equal degree, with a
coefficient of variation of $0.24$--$0.36$; the closure, exact for $C\to\infty$, replaces this spread by its
mean.  Started from the measured state of one economy, one step of the iteration returns $0.955\pm0.01$ of
the temporal field variance (three replicas).  Driving each node instead with the time-mean field and the
temporal amplitude of a measured node of its class returns $1.02\pm0.03$.  A loss of $4.5\%$ per step
that halves the self-consistent temporal variance means a loop gain of about $0.9$: the temporal field at
the reference point is set close to marginal amplification, which is also why leaving the amplification
out, as the static closure does, costs so much.  Predicting its amplitude needs a closure at finite $C$ that keeps the spread
between nodes, such as the cavity method on a tree solved by population dynamics; in the main text we
take it from simulation.

Supplying the closure with measured inputs shows what else it lacks
(\texttt{scripts/two\_time\_dmft.py measured}, \texttt{measured3}).  With the temporal field taken from
simulation, an amplitude $2.16\sqrt\kappa$ and an exponential correlation with the measured $1/e$ time $4.5$,
and the rest self-consistent, the panel has $\zeta_1=0.26$ and ratios $1.85$, $2.54$, $3.08$, against $0.25$ and
$1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model; the volatility of the smallest and largest bins is $0.71$ and $0.37$,
against $0.70$ and $0.32$.  The frozen spread of the closure is then $12$--$15\%$ low in standard deviation.
Fixing it at its measured value, $2.66\sqrt\kappa$ in every class but the top one, brings it to within $4\%$
and leaves $\zeta_1$ at $0.26$, but moves the ratios only to $1.83$, $2.49$, $2.98$.  The measured mean fields,
reshuffled among nodes of similar degree, give $1.81$, $2.42$, $2.86$, and each node's own measured mean and
amplitude $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  The bend therefore depends on the shape of the
spread of mean fields at equal degree and not only on its variance: its fat tail, which we attribute to the
few neighbours that dominate a field at finite $C$, is what a Gaussian closure cannot produce.  With three
measured numbers the closure reproduces the size--volatility slope and the volatility levels; the bend needs
a closure at finite $C$.

\paragraph{Onset in simulation.}  Since the closure is least accurate near $\sigma_c$, we locate the onset
directly.  For each $\sigma$ we run two replicas on each of two graphs ($\alpha=6$, $\mu=-1.76$, $D=0.1$,
$N=4000$, $C=100$, $T=4200$, the first $200$ discarded) with the same couplings and different initial sizes and
noise, and compare their time-averaged sizes over the last $W$ units of time,
$d(W)=\overline{(\bar S_i^A-\bar S_i^B)^2}/\Var_i\bar S_i$.  Fast noise alone gives $d\propto1/W$ with $Wd$ set by its
correlation time; slow collective motion raises $Wd$, and distinct attractors would keep $d$ finite.  For
$W=125$ to $2000$, $Wd\simeq0.6$ at $\sigma=1.30$ and $1.35$, $0.7$--$1.1$ at $1.40$, $1.3$--$2.9$ at $1.45$, $1.2$--$4.6$ at
$1.50$ and $5$--$8$ at $1.60$ and $1.70$.  The slow component sets in between $1.40$ and $1.50$, steepest
between $1.45$ and $1.50$, in agreement with the predicted $\sigma_c=1.451$.  Up to $\sigma=1.7$, $d$ keeps falling
roughly as $1/W$: the replicas agree on average, and what grows is the correlation time of the
composition, not the number of attractors (\texttt{scripts/phase\_onset.py}).

\paragraph{The identity with a composition term.}  Let $p(u\mid\Sb)$ be the law of the floor at fixed
size and $E_q(\Sb)=\int p(u\mid\Sb)\,(u+B(\Sb))^{q/2}\,du$, so that $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=E_q\,\E[\epsilon^q]$.  Then
\begin{gather}
\zeta_q=-\frac{d\ln E_q}{d\ln\Sb}=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\nonumber\\
c_q=\frac{1}{E_q}\int\frac{\partial p(u\mid\Sb)}{\partial\ln\Sb}\,(u+B)^{q/2}\,du ,
\label{eq:app-identity}
\end{gather}
with $b=-d\ln B/d\ln\Sb$ and $\langle w\rangle_q$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:identity}.  The composition term $c_q$ vanishes if the
law of the floor does not depend on size.  On the network it does not vanish: larger lives have
larger degrees on average, and the floor grows with the degree, so $c_q>0$ and the decline is slower
than the noise share alone implies.  If the law of $\ln u$ only shifts with size, by $\delta(\Sb)$, then
$c_q=(q/2)(1-\langle w\rangle_q)\,d\delta/d\ln\Sb$ and $\zeta_q=(q/2)\,[\,b\langle w\rangle_q-(1-\langle w\rangle_q)\,d\delta/d\ln\Sb\,]$: a
rising floor flattens the decline and, since $\langle w\rangle_q$ falls with $q$, adds to the bend; a floor whose upper
tail spreads with size adds more.  If the floor is the same for all firms of a given size, $E_q=(u+B)^{q/2}$
and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$ exactly, whatever $c_q$: heterogeneity at fixed size is necessary for the bend in
general, and sufficient when $c_q=0$.  The tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:multiscaling} use the measured floors of the lives in
each bin, which include the composition term.

\paragraph{Monotonicity of $\langle w\rangle_q$.}  With $Y=u+B$, $\langle w\rangle_q=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]=
B\exp[h(q-2)-h(q)]$, where $h(q)=\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is the cumulant generating function of $\tfrac12\ln Y$
evaluated at $q$ and therefore convex.  Convexity makes $h(q)-h(q-2)$ non-decreasing, so $\langle w\rangle_q$ is
non-increasing in $q$, strictly unless $Y$ is degenerate at fixed size.

\paragraph{Floor make-up.}  The core of a life runs from the first to the last sample at which the noise-free
size is at least half its median (the whole life if fewer than $10$ growth rates lie between them).  For a
climb in a constant field $h$, $u=S/h$ obeys $\dot u=hu(1-u)$ and $d\ln S/dt=h(1-u)$, so
$\int(d\ln S/dt)^2dt=h\int_{S_0/h}^1(1-u)u^{-1}du=h[\ln(h/S_0)-1+S_0/h]$.  For a collapse in a field $-\varphi$,
$d\ln S/dt=-(\varphi+S)$ and $\int(\varphi+S)^2dt=\int_{S_{\rm end}}^h(\varphi+S)S^{-1}dS=\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h-S_{\rm end}$.
Times $\Delta t$ these are the sums of squared yearly growth rates $E_{\rm in}$ and $E_{\rm out}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.
On the $175\,171$ complete lives (born and exited inside the window) with at least $20$ growth rates, the
exit formula follows the measured sum life by life (log-correlation $0.85$--$0.91$ by fifth of size) and lies
above it by $6$--$40\%$; the entry formula has the right scale, from $45\%$ below the measured mean in the
smallest fifth to $38\%$ above in the largest, but correlates less (log-correlation $0.5$), because a firm
climbs in a field that moves.

\paragraph{Life lengths.}  For $dS=r(t)S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW$ the probability of extinction by time $t$ is
$\exp[-2S_0/(D\int_0^te^{-R(s)}ds)]$, $R=\int_0^tr$, from the Laplace transform of this linear (Feller)
diffusion, whose exponent obeys a Riccati equation; with $S_{\rm det}=S_0e^R$ this is Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill}.  At the bottom of a dip, where $\Phi=S_{\min}$, a saddle point gives
$J\simeq\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}/S_{\min}$: a dip kills when it brings the firm below a size of order $D$ times its
recovery time.  The test takes every downcrossing of zero by the field of a node whose firm has $S\ge0.3$,
restarts the noise-free path at the firm's actual size, and integrates it until it recovers to that size
(at most $30$ time units).  Binned by the predicted probability, the observed kill fractions are $0.015$ at
$0.027$, $0.102$ at $0.147$, $0.418$ at $0.450$, $0.894$ at $0.853$ and $0.986$ at $0.974$: the law is slightly too soft at
both ends, and its log-likelihood per dip is $-0.175$ against $-0.591$ for a constant kill rate.  Rice's
downcrossing rate, computed from each node's mean, standard deviation and lag-$\Delta t$ autocorrelation of
the field, lies $1$--$13\%$ above the observed rate in every decile of $u$ but the lowest ($24\%$, the hubs), in
part because sampling every $\Delta t$ misses brief crossings.  Among complete lives with at least $20$ growth
rates, the coefficient of variation of $n-20$ is $1.05$--$1.22$ in every decile of $u$ but the highest, where the
window cuts the longest lives ($0.91$), and the mean of $n$ grows from $23$ to $222$ (\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{A fitted floor.}  An alternative test, without the noise-free integration, fits
$v^2=A\kappa^g+B_j$ (mean absolute deviation, one $B_j$ per size bin) to the same lives.  It gives
$g=1.00$ and predicts ratios $1.86$, $2.56$, $3.06$ against $1.80$, $2.42$, $2.88$ measured; the same law at
the mean degree gives exactly $2$, $3$, $4$ (\texttt{notebooks/05\_multiscaling.ipynb}).  The
fit assigns all of the top bins' variance to the floor ($B_j\to0$), so its partition differs from the
physical one of Table~\ref{tab:fifths}.

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{The year.}  We sample every $0.5$ time units.  The lag-one autocorrelation of growth within
lives is $+0.13$, $+0.18$ and $0.00$ at steps $0.5$, $1$ and $2$; a step of $2$ would remove it but leaves
too few growth rates per life for the panel.  In the two reference economies, lives with at least five
growth rates give $\zeta_q=0.209$, $0.405$, $0.572$, $0.702$ (ratios $1.94$, $2.74$, $3.36$) over $2.0$
decades at step $0.5$, and $\zeta_q=0.156$, $0.270$, $0.339$, $0.370$ (ratios $1.73$, $2.17$, $2.36$) over
$1.4$ decades at step $2$: volatility per step rises by a factor $1.1$ in the smallest size bin and $2.2$
in the largest, so the decline flattens, and the ratio curve bends further below $q$.  For lives with
at least $20$ growth rates the change is smaller ($\zeta_1=0.254$ and $0.230$, ratios $1.80$, $2.42$,
$2.88$ and $1.75$, $2.27$, $2.60$, although at step $2$ these lives span only $0.9$ decades).  The bend
below $q$ survives the coarser year; the level of $\zeta_1$ for short lives does not
(\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Minimum life length.}  MSB keep every firm with at least two growth rates; their firms have $46$ on
average, and their results do not change for firms with at least $20$.  The model's lives are much shorter:
$38\%$ of the lives with at least two growth rates have exactly two ($N=16000$).  We therefore use
$n_{\min}=20$ for the facts, as for the theory tests.  In the production run $\zeta_q=0.178$, $0.431$, $0.706$, $0.968$
(ratios $2.42$, $3.96$, $5.43$) for $n_{\min}=2$, $0.205$, $0.394$, $0.554$, $0.679$ ($1.92$, $2.70$, $3.31$) for $n_{\min}=5$,
$0.214$, $0.381$, $0.501$, $0.577$ ($1.79$, $2.35$, $2.70$) for $n_{\min}=10$ and $0.249$, $0.443$, $0.587$, $0.688$ ($1.78$, $2.35$,
$2.76$) for $n_{\min}=20$, over $2.1$, $2.0$, $1.8$ and $1.4$ decades: the ratios lie above $q$ for $n_{\min}=2$ and below
from $n_{\min}=5$, and the range shrinks because long lives do not occur at the newborn sizes.

\paragraph{The tail index $b$.}  For lives with at least two growth rates the model gives
$b=4.40$--$4.44$ at every parameter point, close to the Compustat value $4.6$.  A null with iid Laplace
growth rates, no differences between firms and the model's distribution of life lengths gives
$4.34$, so the agreement is an effect of short lives.  For longer lives $b$ grows: $5.5$ and $6.8$ in the production
run for $n_{\min}=10$ and $20$ ($5.7$ and $6.9$ at $N=16000$), against $9.3$ and $12.6$ for the Laplace null and $4.6$ in
Compustat for both samples (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Volatility estimator.}  With the standard deviation instead of $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ times the mean
absolute deviation, $\zeta_1$ is $0.03$ lower and the ratios are $0.03$--$0.3$ higher (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb},
rows SD; production run: $\zeta_1=0.175$, ratios $1.96$, $2.82$, $3.58$ at $n_{\min}=5$).

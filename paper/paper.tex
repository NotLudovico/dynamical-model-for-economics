\title{Interaction heterogeneity and the multiscaling of firm growth volatility}
\author{Ludovico Furlanetto}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The growth volatility of firms falls with their size as $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q}$, and the ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ lie below $q$: the dispersion of volatilities among firms of equal size grows with size.
Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of independent parts, give ratios equal to $q$ when
well-diversified firms dominate the moments and close to $1$ when firms made of a few parts do; the data
lie between.  We show that
whenever a firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size,
$\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$, with $w$ the share of the falling part, and the ratios bend below $q$
only if floors differ between firms of equal size.  We then build a dynamical economy in which they do: firms
interact on a scale-free network through generalised Lotka--Volterra dynamics, with birth--death noise
and turnover.  Each firm reduces to a logistic unit driven by its own field.  Its floor contains the
climb after entry and the collapse before exit, and lives end in dips of the field at a rate we derive,
so floors differ with the length of a life and with the degree of its node.  With equal degrees the
floors barely differ and the ratios return to $q$, as the criterion requires.  Sizes, entries, exits and the co-movement of firms, which grows with size as
in the data, are outputs of the dynamics, not inputs.  The model gives
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$, as in firm data, and a ratio curve below $q$, though less bent than in the data; it
caps firm size and has no Zipf law.  We derive tests of the mechanism on firm data.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large firms grow less erratically than small ones, but much more erratically than a sum of independent
parts would.  If $v$ is the volatility of a firm's growth rate and $\Sb$ its mean size over its life,
the conditional moments in Compustat scale as
\begin{equation}
\E[v^q\mid\Sb]\sim\Sb^{-\zeta_q},
\label{eq:zeta}
\end{equation}
with $\zeta_q=0.20(1)$, $0.39(2)$, $0.51(3)$, $0.58(4)$ for $q=1,\dots,4$ over four decades of size
\cite{stanley1996,amaral1997,sutton2002,moran2024}.  The ratios $\zeta_q/\zeta_1=1.95$, $2.55$, $2.90$ lie
below $q$ \cite{moran2024}.  If a firm's volatility were a function of its size times a random factor
whose law does not depend on size, the ratios would be exactly $q$.  The bend therefore says that firms of
equal size differ more in volatility the larger they are.  Firm sizes themselves follow Zipf's law
\cite{axtell2001}.

Granular models, in which a firm is a sum of subunits with independent shocks
\cite{wyartbouchaud2003,gabaix2011}, give $\zeta_1<1/2$ when the subunit sizes are broad.  Moran, Secchi
and Bouchaud (MSB) derive their higher moments \cite{moran2024}.  Well-diversified firms give
$\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, but large firms made of a few subunits, whose volatility does not fall with size,
dominate every moment of order two and above, which then all fall with the same exponent, about $0.2$:
the ratios would be close to $1$.  The data lie between the two limits.  MSB attribute their departure
from $q$ to subdominant corrections from the tail of the volatility distribution, and show that granular
models also miss the shape of that distribution and the fat tails of growth rates at every size.  They
conclude that granular mechanisms alone cannot account for the data, and point to the co-movement of
firms, which grows with size, as the missing ingredient \cite{moran2024,herskovic2020}: firms made of
independent parts do not co-move.  What
makes the dispersion of volatilities grow with size, and by as much as it does, has not been explained.

We answer in two steps.  The first does not depend on a model (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  The ratio
curve lies below $q$ exactly where the dispersion of the rescaled volatility grows with size.  If a
firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size, as when a firm's own noise is
averaged out by its size, the exponents obey $\zeta_q=(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$, with $b$ the slope of the
falling part and $w$ its share, and the dispersion grows only if the floors differ between firms of
equal size.  The noise of estimating a volatility from a short life acts the other way, because short
lives are concentrated among small firms; this is why the measured bend depends on the shortest lives
kept.

The second step is a dynamical economy of interacting firms in which floors differ
(Secs.~\ref{sec:economy}--\ref{sec:floor}).  A granular model takes the structure of each firm as given and
its shocks as independent.  Here nothing about a firm is put in: its size, its entry and exit, the length
of its life and its co-movement with other firms all follow from one dynamics with a few parameters.  We start
from the disordered generalised Lotka--Volterra (GLV) model, whose strongly interacting phase keeps
fluctuating, each species behaving as a logistic unit driven by a coloured Gaussian field
\cite{roy2020,mallmin2024,arnouxbunin2024}.  We measure interactions and self-limitation in units of the
mean firm, so that the economy has no preferred size, place firms on a scale-free network
\cite{acemoglu2012,carvalho2019}, and replace immigration by birth--death noise with turnover: a firm
that reaches zero exits and a newborn takes its node.  Each firm then reduces to a logistic unit in its
own field, and its growth variance to a floor and a demographic part.  The floor contains the climb
after entry and the collapse before exit, spread over the life, and lives end in dips of the field at a
rate given by Rice's formula and the extinction probability of a branching process.  Floors therefore
differ with the length of a life and with the degree of its node, which sets the amplitude of its field.
On a graph with equal degrees they barely differ, and the ratios return close to $q$.  Anomalous moment
scaling has been derived for GLV communities before: with multiplicative environmental noise and migration,
abundances obey Taylor's law with a single exponent for every order \cite{alhiyasat2026}, through the
Bouchaud--M\'ezard wealth model \cite{bouchaudmezard2000}.  That law concerns the moments of an abundance;
ours concerns the moments of the growth volatility conditioned on size, whose exponents bend below
$q\zeta_1$, and depends on the network and on turnover.

At the minimum life length that matches the data, the model gives a monotone decline with
$\zeta_1\simeq0.2$ and a ratio curve below $q$, though less bent than Compustat's, and
fails elsewhere (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}): the self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also caps
firm size, so the decline spans two decades rather than four and there is no Zipf law.  Its theory is a
one-firm problem given three statistics of the field.  Heterogeneous mean-field theory gives their form,
but not the amplitude of the field's motion, which at finite connectivity is set close to marginal
amplification, or how that amplitude differs between firms of equal degree; we take them from simulation
(Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).

The mechanism leads to tests on firm data (Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions}).  The dispersion of volatilities
among firms of equal size should grow with size; the length of a firm's observed life should explain a
growing share of it; trimming the years after entry and before exit should move the ratio curve towards
$q$ and steepen the decline; and adding short lives should push the curve towards or above $q$.
Derivations, checks and robustness tests are in the appendices, and every number is printed by a
notebook of the companion code \cite{code}.

\section{An economy of interacting firms}
\label{sec:economy}

\paragraph{Model.}  We consider $N$ firms with sizes $x_i$ and call $m=N^{-1}\sum_jx_j$ the mean firm.  The
firms sit on the nodes of a configuration-model graph with degree law $p(k)\propto k^{-\alpha}$ and mean
degree $C$, and their sizes evolve as
\begin{equation}
dx_i=x_i\Big[1-\frac{x_i}{m}+\sum_jJ_{ij}\frac{x_j}{m}\Big]dt+\sqrt{D\,m\,x_i}\;dW_i ,
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
with independent Wiener processes $W_i$.  The couplings are $J_{ij}=A_{ij}(\mu/C+\sigma z_{ij}/\sqrt C)$, with
$A$ the adjacency matrix and $z_{ij}$ independent standard Gaussians, $z_{ij}$ and $z_{ji}$ uncorrelated;
$\mu<0$ means that competition dominates on average.  A firm that reaches zero is replaced by a newborn of
size $Dm$ on the same node, the size at which the noise $\sqrt{Dmx_i}$ equals $x_i$, so entries occur where
and when the dynamics makes firms exit.  Every term is homogeneous of degree one in the sizes: the economy
has no preferred scale, and the mean firm grows at a rate the dynamics selects.  The noise unit grows
with the economy, and can be read as an indivisible piece of business, such as a contract or a customer.

\paragraph{Reading the equation.}  The bracket of Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} is the growth rate of firm $i$.  The $1$
is the rate at which a firm would grow alone.  The term $-x_i/m$ is self-limitation: a firm's growth slows
as it becomes large compared with the mean firm, as when it saturates its own market.  The sum is the effect
of the firms it is linked to, each in proportion to its size.  A negative $J_{ij}$ means that firm $j$ takes
growth from firm $i$, as a competitor does, and a positive one that it feeds it, as a customer or a supplier
does.  The links are fixed and their strengths are random: $\mu$ sets their mean and $\sigma$ their spread,
and the scalings with $C$ make both the mean and the spread of the total effect on a firm of average degree
of order one.  The last term is birth--death noise.  Its variance is proportional to the size of the firm, as
for a sum of independent gains and losses of pieces of business of size $Dm$, so it matters for small firms
and averages out for large ones: its contribution to the variance of the growth rate $dx_i/x_i$ is
$Dm/x_i$ per unit time.  This is the only place where a granular, $1/\text{size}$ effect enters.  The model has five
parameters, $\mu$, $\sigma$, $\alpha$, $C$ and $D$.

\paragraph{A logistic unit in a moving field.}  In relative sizes $S_i=x_i/m$,
\begin{equation}
dS_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]dt+\sqrt{DS_i}\,dW_i+O(N^{-1/2}),
\label{eq:onefirm}
\end{equation}
with the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\bar f(t),
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
where $\bar f$ is the growth rate of the mean firm, including the dilution by entries
(Appendix~\ref{app:relative}).  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is a logistic equation whose carrying capacity
moves in time.  If the field on a firm stays at $\Phi>0$, the firm relaxes at rate $\Phi$ to the plateau
$x_i=m\Phi$; if $\Phi<0$, it shrinks until it exits.  For $C\to\infty$ the relative sizes map onto the
standard GLV with demographic noise \cite{altieri2021}, and on the complete graph, with immigration in place
of the noise, onto the chaotic-turnover model of Refs.~\cite{bunin2017,mallmin2024}.

The field $\Phi_i$ collects everything that acts on firm $i$ except its own self-limitation: it is the rate
at which the firm would grow, relative to the mean firm, if it were very small.  Two firms differ only through
their fields, and the field of a firm is set by its neighbours on the network.  The rest of the paper rests
on this reduction: the statistics of volatility follow from one firm and the statistics of its field.

\paragraph{Phase diagram.}  The model has two regimes.  If the interactions are weak, the economy relaxes: every
field settles to a constant, every firm sits on its plateau or is replaced again and again by newborns that
fail, and only the birth--death noise moves sizes.  If they are strong, no such state is stable: the fields
keep moving because the firms that make them keep rising and falling, and the composition of the economy
changes for ever.  The facts we are after need the second regime, since a firm's floor comes from the motion
of its field.  A second question is whether the mean firm grows or shrinks, which the dynamics decides: the
growth rate $\bar f$ is an output.  The phase diagram answers both, in the plane of the mean interaction $\mu$
and the disorder $\sigma$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}).

For $C\to\infty$ heterogeneous dynamical mean-field theory (HDMFT) replaces the
network by one process per degree class, closed by averages over neighbours drawn in proportion to their
degree \cite{aguirrelopez,park2024,poley2025,patil2026}.  A node of relative degree $\kappa=k/C$ feels
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t)=1-\bar f+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t),
\label{eq:hdmft}
\end{equation}
with $\bar B$ the degree-weighted mean size and $\xi$ a Gaussian process whose correlation is the
degree-weighted correlation of sizes (Appendix~\ref{app:hdmft}).  A better connected firm has a lower mean
field under competition, because it competes with more firms, and a field of larger amplitude, because it
sums more random terms.  In the relaxing regime $\xi$ is constant in time, and the theory reduces to a static
self-consistency.  The static solution loses stability at a line $\sigma_c(\mu)$, where the response of the
sizes to a small static change of the fields diverges.  Without noise this line and its dependence on the
degree law are known \cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026}: hubs feel a field of larger amplitude and
lower it.  Here the noise and the turnover change the static solution.  Each node follows the exact
stationary law of one firm at constant field, a renewal process of successive lives, which also predicts
the turnover to $1$--$2\%$ (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  Noise raises the line almost linearly in $D$, as
demographic noise stabilises the unique state on the complete graph \cite{altieri2021}.  The same solution
gives the zero-growth line, on which $\bar f=0$.

In simulation the onset of the fluctuating regime follows the predicted line to within its
width, $0.1$--$0.2$ in $\sigma$: it rises strongly with competition on a scale-free graph and stays flat on a
graph with a light degree tail, with nothing fitted (Appendix~\ref{app:linetest},
Fig.~\ref{fig:instability}).  The zero-growth line of the static solution lies within $0.04$ of the
simulated one for $\mu$ from $-0.5$ to $-2.5$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}a).  Our reference point, $\alpha=2.5$,
$\sigma=1.70$, $D=0.1$, $C=200$, $\mu=-1.76$, lies in the fluctuating phase ($\sigma_c=1.497$ at $D=0.1$),
close to the line on which the mean firm stops growing.

\section{Comparison with firm data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  We integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} in relative form by Euler--Maruyama with step
$0.01$, discard $t<100$, and record sizes every $\Delta t=0.5$, which we take as one year, over a window of
length $480$.  A \emph{life} runs from a birth to the next exit at the same node; lives cut by the window
are kept, as in MSB's unbalanced panel.  A life recorded at $n+1$ times has growth rates
$g_t=\ln S_{t+1}-\ln S_t$, and its volatility and size are
\begin{equation}
v=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\big|g_t-\bar g\big|,\qquad
\Sb=\frac1{n+1}\sum_{t=1}^{n+1}S_t,
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
the factor making $v$ the standard deviation for Gaussian growth.  Exponents are least-squares slopes of
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ against $\ln\Sb$ over $25$ equal-count bins (Fig.~\ref{fig:fits}).  We compare with data on lives with at
least five growth rates, the cut that matches MSB's, and test the theory on lives with
at least $20$, long enough to estimate each life's floor.  The comparison with data uses seven
economies with $N=32000$; the theory tests use two with $N=16000$.

We compare seven economies at the reference point ($N=32000$; an eighth, in a collective burst, is
excluded) with the facts of MSB, using their estimator, the mean absolute deviation of a life's growth
rates.  Which minimum life length matches theirs needs care.  MSB's growth rates are annual differences
rolled over quarters, so their robustness cut of $20$ growth rates spans about six years, or five
non-overlapping annual growth rates; their full sample has $46$ per firm on average.  Our $n_{\min}=5$ is
the like-for-like cut, and we compare at it (Fig.~\ref{fig:facts}).  Table~\ref{tab:facts} also gives
$n_{\min}=20$, the cut of the theory tests of Secs.~\ref{sec:onefirm} and \ref{sec:floor}.

\paragraph{What the model gets.}  At the like-for-like cut, $\zeta_1=0.209\pm0.024$ against $0.20(1)$.  The
decline is monotone, with no hump at small sizes, and the ratio curve lies below $q$ at both cuts.  Growth
rates have exponential, tent-shaped tails, fatter than a Laplace law of the same volatility, as in
Compustat (Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}a, c).  Rescaled by each firm's volatility, they stay fat-tailed among the
largest firms, with an excess kurtosis of $5.7$ against $5.7$ in Compustat and $0.1$ for a Gaussian null
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}d): there is no Gaussianisation, which granular models predict.  The
co-movement of a firm with the aggregate and with firms of similar size rises with size, as in
Compustat.  This is the feature MSB single out as the missing ingredient of granular models, whose firms
do not co-move \cite{moran2024}; here it comes from the interactions.  A natural reading is that small
firms are moved mostly by their own noise and large ones by their field, which they share with their
neighbours, but we have no quantitative theory of it.  Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} sets these facts against the main
classes of models of firm growth: to our knowledge no other model gets all rows but the last together.

\paragraph{Where the facts hold.}  The reference point is one point of a band (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}).  Near
the zero-growth line, $\zeta_1$ and the ratio curve depend on a single variable, the depth $\sigma-\sigma_c(\mu)$
of the economy in the fluctuating phase: for $\mu\le-1$ and lives with at least five growth rates,
$\zeta_1=0.49-1.34\,(\sigma-\sigma_c)$, with a scatter of $0.014$ over $27$ points.  The deeper the economy, the
more its field moves, the larger the floor of large firms and the flatter the decline.  Depths from $0.18$
to $0.25$ give $\zeta_1=0.15$--$0.25$.  For $\mu$ from $-1.25$ to $-2.25$ this band contains economies whose
mean firm grows by between $-0.08$ and $+0.16$ per unit time, so an economy that competes moderately and
grows slowly sits in it; slow growth alone does not, since along the zero-growth line $\zeta_1$ falls from
$0.40$ under weak competition to $0.13$ under strong competition.  The band is about $0.07$ wide in $\sigma$.

\paragraph{What is fitted.}  One fact is fitted: the depth sets $\zeta_1$, and we place the reference point at
the depth that gives $\zeta_1\simeq0.2$.  The others are outputs.  Across the band of depths that gives
$\zeta_1=0.15$--$0.25$ the ratio curve stays below $q$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:strip}c and Table~\ref{tab:robust}), and the
tails of growth, their survival among large firms, the co-movement and the serial correlation of growth come
from the same point.

\paragraph{What it misses.}  The decline spans two decades rather than four, and volatility falls by a
factor $2.8$ against $5.5$.  The tail of the volatility distribution is too thin, $b=5.2$ against $4.6$, and thinner for longer
lives; with all lives the model gives $b=4.4$, but a null with iid Laplace growth and no differences
between firms gives $4.3$, so that value is an artefact of short lives.  The rescaled growth rates of small
firms have tails that are too thin: their excess kurtosis is $3.0$ against $4.2$, and a Gaussian null with
the same lives gives $2.5$ from the rescaling of short lives alone.  The model's tails are also asymmetric, the
negative one fatter, and multi-year persistence is confined to the largest firms: small firms are moved by
near-Gaussian, mean-reverting birth--death noise, large firms by rare and persistent dips of their field,
which make their fat tails and the collapses before exit.  Aggregate volatility is several times too large
and does not fall as $N^{-1/2}$ over the sizes we simulate.  Five of the $80$ economies of our robustness
runs, one of the eight at the reference point, enter a collective burst: aggregate volatility per year above
$0.3$, against $0.04$--$0.26$ in the others, higher turnover, and a volatility that hardly depends on size
($\zeta_1=0.08$).  We exclude economies above this threshold; with the burst economy the pooled exponents at the
reference point are $\zeta_1=0.170$ and ratios $1.79$, $2.30$, $2.52$, because it contributes the largest number
of lives (Appendix~\ref{app:robust}).  What triggers a burst is open.  The year is a choice.  At our step
of $0.5$ time units the serial correlation of growth rises with size, from about zero in the smallest fifth
to $+0.46$ in the largest, as reported for firm data \cite{coad2007}, though probably too strongly among
large firms; at a step of $2$ it vanishes at every size.  The bend survives the coarser step, but $\zeta_1$
for lives with at least five growth rates falls to $0.15$ (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}).  The size distribution has no
Pareto tail, newborns have extreme infant mortality, and small established firms shrink on average.  The
missing tail and the short range come from the self-limitation: a firm whose field stays at $\Phi>0$
relaxes to the plateau $x=m\Phi$, a few times the mean firm, so sizes are capped near the highest plateaus,
and the decline runs from a few times the newborn size $D$, below which lives are too short to measure, to
the plateau.

\section{When the ratio curve bends}
\label{sec:criterion}

\paragraph{What the exponents measure.}  The exponent $\zeta_1$ says how fast the typical volatility falls with
size.  The higher exponents say how the whole distribution of volatilities at a given size changes with size.
Suppose first that it does not change, apart from its scale: $v=A(\Sb)\,X$, with the law of $X$ the same at
every size.  Then $\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=A(\Sb)^q\,\E[X^q]$ and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$ for every $q$.  Ratios
$\zeta_q/\zeta_1$ equal to $q$ therefore mean that firms of different sizes have the same distribution of
volatilities up to a scale factor.  High moments weigh the most volatile firms of each size.  Ratios below $q$
mean that these fall with size more slowly than the typical firm does: among large firms, some stay much more
volatile than the rest.  The rest of this section makes this precise, in two steps.  The first is an identity
that holds for any panel.  The second assumes that a firm's growth variance has two parts, and identifies
which dispersion is responsible.  The derivations are in Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}.



\paragraph{Dispersion at fixed size.}  Write a firm's volatility as its conditional mean times a rescaled
volatility, $v=m(\Sb)\,X$ with $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$, and let $K_q(\Sb)=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln m+K_q$, and since $K_1=0$,
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general}
\end{equation}
$K_q$ is convex in $q$ and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so for $q>1$ it measures the dispersion of $X$,
weighted more towards its upper tail as $q$ grows.  The ratio curve lies below $q$ where this dispersion
grows with size, on $q$ where it does not change, and above $q$ where it shrinks.  For a lognormal $X$,
with $\sigma^2(\Sb)$ the variance of $\ln X$, $K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$ and
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{q(q-1)}{2}\,\frac{d\sigma^2}{d\ln\Sb}.
\label{eq:general-lognormal}
\end{equation}
Granular models show both limits \cite{moran2024}.  When well-diversified firms set the moments, the law
of $X$ does not depend on size and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.  When firms made of a few subunits, whose volatility
does not fall with size, set them, the upper tail of $X$ stretches with size so fast that $\zeta_q$ no longer
depends on $q$ for $q\ge2$.  A model of the bend has to say why the dispersion grows, and by how much.

In Compustat, $\zeta_2-2\zeta_1=-0.01$, within the uncertainties of the exponents, while
$\zeta_3-3\zeta_1=-0.09$ and $\zeta_4-4\zeta_1=-0.22$ \cite{moran2024}.  The second moment of $X$ hardly
changes with size; the third and fourth grow.  The dispersion that grows with size is therefore in the
upper tail of the rescaled volatility, and the collapse of its distribution that MSB report can only be
approximate there.

\paragraph{A floor and a falling part.}  Suppose that a firm's squared volatility is a floor $u_i$ plus a
part $B(\Sb)$ common to all firms of the same size, up to an estimation factor $\epsilon_i$:
\begin{equation}
v_i^2=\big(u_i+B(\Sb_i)\big)\,\epsilon_i^2 .
\label{eq:twopart}
\end{equation}
Let $b=-d\ln B/d\ln\Sb$ be the local slope of the falling part and $w=B/(u+B)$ its share of the variance.
If $\epsilon$ is independent of $u$ and of size, differentiating $\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]$ gives
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\qquad
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\,w\mid\Sb\big]}{\E\big[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb\big]},
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
where the composition term $c_q$ vanishes when the law of the floor at fixed size does not change with
size (Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  The exponent is the slope of the falling part times its share of
the variance, weighted by $v^q$: largest where the falling part dominates and zero where the floor does.

\paragraph{The criterion.}  With $Y=u+B$, $\langle w\rangle_q=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$.  Since
$\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]$ is convex in $q$, $\langle w\rangle_q$ does not increase with $q$, and it is constant only if
$Y$ is the same for all firms of equal size.  When $c_q=0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q,
\label{eq:bend}
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if the floor does not vary between firms of equal size.  When $c_q\neq0$ one
direction survives: a floor that is the same for every firm of a given size, however it depends on size,
gives $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$, like a granular model of well-diversified firms.  If $\ln Y$ is Gaussian at fixed size with variance $s^2$,
\begin{gather}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}\simeq q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\nonumber\\
s^2=\Var\big(\ln(u+B)\mid\Sb\big)\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb),
\label{eq:lognormal}
\end{gather}
the last form for a narrow floor.  This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:general} at work: as the falling part
shrinks with size the floors show through, $w$ falls and $s^2$ grows.  A heterogeneous floor bends the
curve only where it is not small against the falling part, which is among the large firms.

\paragraph{Estimation noise.}  The factor $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratios only if its law does not depend
on size.  A volatility estimated from $n$ growth rates by their mean absolute deviation has
$\Var\ln\epsilon\simeq(\pi/2-1)/n$ for Gaussian growth, and by their standard deviation about $1/(2n)$;
fat tails raise both.  Wherever small firms live shorter, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and, by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}, pushes the ratio curve above $q$.  Short lives can therefore hide a bend
or reverse it.  In the model, all lives with at least two growth rates give ratios $2.83$, $5.00$, $7.30$
with the standard deviation as the volatility, and the bend appears as the shortest lives are dropped (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).  In firm data the same
effect makes the ratio curve depend on the minimum life length, which should be reported with it.

\paragraph{Three cases.}  Equations~\eqref{eq:general}--\eqref{eq:lognormal} account for the three panels of
the model: on a scale-free network the dispersion at fixed size grows from $s^2=0.02$ in the smallest
fifth of sizes to $0.26$ in the largest and the ratios bend; on a graph with equal degrees it stays below
$0.05$ and the ratios are close to $q$; with all lives the estimation noise dominates and the ratios lie
above $q$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).

\paragraph{The shape of the bend.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:general} reads the row $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$ of
Table~\ref{tab:facts} as the growth with size of the moments of the rescaled volatility.  In Compustat the
second moment hardly grows and the fourth does: the upper tail spreads.  At the like-for-like cut the
model gets the second moment but its upper tail spreads too little ($-0.15$ against $-0.22$).  At
$n_{\min}=20$ its dispersion grows already in the bulk ($-0.06$ against $-0.01$) and its tail at least as
much as in Compustat.  The difference between the two cuts is the estimation noise of short lives, which
shrinks with size and offsets part of the floors' spread (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}).  Neither cut gives
Compustat's shape: the floors of the model spread the whole distribution of volatilities at fixed size,
while in Compustat the spread is concentrated in the upper tail.

\section{One firm in its field}
\label{sec:onefirm}

The economy of Sec.~\ref{sec:economy} has $N$ coupled firms, but Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} describes each of them
as one logistic unit driven by its field.  This section uses that description in three steps.  We first check
that the field can be treated as an external input, so that the problem is one of a single firm.  We then ask
which statistics of the field matter.  Last, we split the growth variance of a firm into the part that comes
from the motion of its field and the part that comes from its own birth--death noise.  The first part is what
Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} called the floor.



\paragraph{The field can be taken as given.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:onefirm} is exact; its content is that a
firm's own noise does not feed back on its field through the network.  We store the fields of the two
reference economies, integrate Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} for every node with its measured field and fresh
noise, and compare the life panels: the exponents agree to $0.01$ and the ratios to $0.06$ for every
minimum life length, and the exit rates agree exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}).  The size--volatility
statistics are a one-firm problem, given the statistics of the field.

\paragraph{Three statistics of the field.}  A node's mean field falls with its degree, from $0.5$ at
$\kappa\simeq0.4$ to $-2.3$ at $\kappa\simeq2$: hubs compete with more firms.  Among nodes of equal degree the
mean field has a spread of $2.66\sqrt\kappa$ in standard deviation, close to Gaussian (excess kurtosis
$0.3$), and around its mean the field moves with amplitude $2.15\sqrt\kappa$ (as $\kappa^{0.54}$ across degree
classes), which differs between nodes of equal degree by about $30\%$, and decorrelates over about $4$ time
units.  These three statistics are enough.  Driving each firm by a Gaussian field with its node's time mean,
its temporal amplitude and one correlation function gives $\zeta_1=0.23$ and ratios $1.78$, $2.37$, $2.81$,
against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$ for the full model.  Two spreads among firms of equal degree carry
the bend.  Setting the amplitudes to their value in the degree class raises $\zeta_1$ and weakens the bend
(\todo{$0.28$; $1.84$, $2.51$, $3.03$}), and setting the means to their class value as well sends the ratios
back towards $q$ ($1.93$, $2.79$, $3.56$); the shape of the spread of the means does not matter, since a
Gaussian spread of the same variance gives the same panel.  HDMFT, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, gives the form of
these statistics, and its closure holds on the measured sizes to $4$--$6\%$.  Solved self-consistently with a
Gaussian field, it gets the mean but not the temporal amplitude, which comes out $20$--$30\%$ low, or its
spread at equal degree; at $C=200$ both depend on the few neighbours that dominate a firm's field, close to a
marginal amplification (Appendix~\ref{app:closure}).  We therefore take
the field's statistics from simulation (\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The volatility law.}  In logs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} reads
$d\ln S=[\Phi-S-D/(2S)]\,dt+\sqrt{D/S}\,dW$.  Write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$, where $y_F$ solves the noise-free
equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ from the same initial size.  To linear order $\delta$ is an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process that relaxes at the local rate $S$ and is driven by noise of variance $D/S$.
Treating the two parts as independent, the variance of a firm's yearly growth over its life is
\begin{equation}
\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$, the floor, is the growth variance of $y_F$.  For every life with at least $20$ growth rates,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:law} reproduces the measured volatility to within $5\%$ above the lowest decile of size and
$9\%$ in it (Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}a), where the linearisation is worst: it needs $S\gg\sqrt{D/2}\simeq0.22$.
The floor is nearly flat, $\sqrt{V_F}=0.32$--$0.38$, although $\sqrt{\Var\Delta\ln\Phi}$ falls from $0.92$ to
$0.44$ with size: a firm does not follow the fast part of its field.  The demographic part falls as
$\Sb^{-b}$ with $b=0.65$--$0.79$, below the value $1$ of $B\simeq D\Delta t/\Sb$ because $B$ is dominated by the
low excursions of a life, and the two parts are equal at $\Sb\simeq1.6$.  Equation~\eqref{eq:law} is
therefore the two-part law of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}, with $u=V_F$.  The decomposition has the two-regime
shape reported for data that include very small firms \cite{fontanelli2025}, but the measured panel does not
resolve the two regimes, and $\zeta_1$ is the effective slope of the whole range.

\section{Where the floors differ}
\label{sec:floor}

Section~\ref{sec:onefirm} gives every life a growth variance $V_F+B$: a floor $V_F$, the growth variance of
the noise-free firm along the life's own field, and a demographic part $B$ that falls with size.  This
is the two-part law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:twopart} with $u=V_F$.  Here we ask what the floor is made of, why
it differs between lives of equal size, and whether Eqs.~\eqref{eq:identity}--\eqref{eq:lognormal}
account for the panel.  Throughout, the volatility is the growth standard deviation, and the lives are
those with at least $20$ growth rates in the two reference economies, unless stated otherwise
(\texttt{notebooks/05\_multiscaling.ipynb} and \texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The floor is mostly entry and exit.}  A life of this length usually begins as a newborn at
$S\simeq D$ and ends at an exit, so its noise-free path climbs to a plateau, follows the field there,
and collapses in the final dip.  Splitting the path where the noise-free size first and last reaches
half its median,
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},
\label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
with $V_{\rm core}$ the growth variance on the plateau and $E_{\rm in}$, $E_{\rm out}$ the sums of squared
growth rates over the climb and the collapse.  For a logistic climb from $S_0$ to a plateau $h$,
$E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$, and for a collapse in a field that has fallen to $-\varphi$,
$E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$: both grow with the plateau
(Appendix~\ref{app:multiscaling}).  The two parts move in opposite directions.  The core falls from
$0.084$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $0.052$ in the largest, while the share of entry and exit grows
from a quarter of $V_F$ to $62\%$: large firms climb further from the newborn size and collapse from
higher.  Their sum is the nearly flat floor of Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}.

\paragraph{Lives end in dips of the field.}  An established firm dies when its field dips.  Inside the
dip the firm is small, the logistic term is negligible, and the firm is a Feller branching process,
whose extinction probability is exact:
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm kill}=\exp\Big(-\frac{2}{D\,J}\Big),\qquad J=\int\frac{dt}{S_{\rm det}(t)},
\label{eq:kill}
\end{equation}
with $S_{\rm det}$ the noise-free path through the dip.  Over the $446\,554$ dips of established firms,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill} predicts a kill fraction of $0.725$ against $0.722$ observed, with no fitted
parameter.  Dips begin at the downcrossings of zero, which occur at Rice's rate
$\sqrt{-\rho''(0)}\,e^{-u^2/2}/2\pi$, with $u$ the node's mean field over its temporal amplitude.  Lives
are therefore memoryless, with a mean that grows steeply with $u$, from about $23$ to $220$ growth rates
across deciles of $u$.

\paragraph{Floors differ with life length and degree.}  By Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} the same climb and
collapse are spread over lives of very different lengths, and the lengths are broad because lives end
at memoryless dips.  Within size bins, $\Var(\ln V_F\mid\Sb)$ grows from $0.5$ in the smallest fifth to
$0.9$ in the largest, and the spread of life lengths, $\Var(\ln n\mid\Sb)$, from $0.08$ to $0.91$.  In
the four upper fifths the life length explains $26$--$62\%$ of $\Var\ln V_F$ within bins, more than the
degree ($19$--$41\%$; $8\%$ against $14\%$ in the smallest fifth).  The degree acts through both parts
of the floor.  A better connected firm feels a field of larger amplitude, which keeps its core up, and
more frequent killing dips, which shorten its life (the correlation of $\ln n$ with $\ln\kappa$ within
size bins falls from $-0.16$ in the smallest fifth to $-0.49$ in the largest) and raise its entry and
exit part.  This keeps the floor of the larger firms at $V_F\simeq0.13$ while their demographic part
falls.

\paragraph{The criterion on the panel.}  The noise share falls from $\langle w\rangle_1=0.80$ in the
smallest fifth to $0.44$ in the largest, and the dispersion at fixed size, with $B$ at its bin mean,
grows from $s^2=0.021$ to $0.264$.  Within size bins $\langle w\rangle_4/\langle w\rangle_1=0.96$, $0.92$,
$0.87$, $0.79$, $0.61$ by fifth, against $0.97$, $0.93$, $0.89$, $0.83$, $0.67$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:lognormal}.  In the four lower fifths the local exponents agree with
$(q/2)\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q$ to within $13\%$; in the largest the composition term dominates, because the
largest lives also have the largest degrees (mean $\kappa$ from $1.00$ to $1.53$), and the local
exponents are $0.13$, $0.16$, $0.10$, $-0.03$ against $0.17$, $0.29$, $0.37$, $0.41$.  Keeping each life's
own floor and replacing $B$ by its bin mean, so that the floor is the only source of dispersion, gives
ratios $1.85$, $2.55$, $3.07$ against $1.75$, $2.30$, $2.70$ measured.  Replacing instead the floor by its
bin mean and keeping each life's own estimate of $B$ gives $2.08$, $3.68$, $6.61$, above $q$: without the
spread of the floor only estimation noise is left, as in Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}.  Keeping one part of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor} at its own value and the other at its bin mean gives $1.98$, $2.94$, $3.85$ with
only the core heterogeneous and $1.90$, $2.69$, $3.33$ with only the entry and exit part.  The bend is
carried mostly by the climb and the collapse, through the spread of life lengths.

\end{figure*}

\section{Predictions for firm data}
\label{sec:predictions}

The criterion of Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion} holds for any panel; the mechanism of Sec.~\ref{sec:floor} is a
claim about where the dispersion at fixed size comes from.  It leads to four tests.  The model's numbers
below are for the two reference economies, with the panel's estimator (\texttt{notebooks/07\_floor\_theory.ipynb}).

\paragraph{1. The dispersion at fixed size grows with size.}  For lives with at least $20$ growth rates the
within-bin variance of $\ln v$ grows from $0.048$ in the smallest fifth of sizes to $0.162$ in the largest.
Compustat's exponents imply that its dispersion grows mainly in the upper tail (Sec.~\ref{sec:data});
measuring $K_q(\Sb)$ by size directly would show where, and whether the growth is concentrated among the
largest firms, as the criterion requires when it comes from floors.

\paragraph{2. Life length and exposure explain a growing share of it.}  Within size bins, the length of a
life explains $5\%$ of $\Var\ln v$ in the smallest fifth and $61\%$ in the largest, and the degree $4\%$ and
$40\%$.  In firm data the life is the observed span from listing to delisting, and the degree a measure
of exposure to other firms, such as the number of major customers or suppliers a firm discloses.  The
prediction is not that either matters, but that their share of the dispersion grows with size.

\paragraph{3. Trimming entry and exit years moves the curve towards $q$.}  Dropping the first $k$ years
after a birth and the last $k$ before an exit, and keeping lives that still have at least $20$ growth
rates, steepens the decline and weakens the bend: for $k=0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, $5$, $\zeta_1=0.253$, $0.255$,
$0.272$, $0.291$, $0.320$ and $\zeta_4/\zeta_1=2.80$, $2.96$, $3.08$, $3.17$, $3.23$.  Keeping only the
plateau of each life, which data cannot locate, goes further in the same direction, to $\zeta_1=0.38$ and
$\zeta_4/\zeta_1=3.44$.  Compustat firms enter at listing, already large, so the climb from the newborn size has no
counterpart there and the years before delisting should carry the effect.  With shorter lives the test is
not clean: at $n_{\min}=5$ trimming also shortens lives and adds estimation noise, and the sequence is not
monotone.

\paragraph{4. Short lives push the curve towards or above $q$.}  With all lives the model's ratios are
$2.42$, $4.01$, $5.64$, and the bend appears from $n_{\min}\simeq5$ ($1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$).  When short lives are kept, the
dispersion at fixed size is not monotone in size: for $n_{\min}=5$ the within-bin variance of $\ln v$ falls
from $0.18$ in the smallest fifth to $0.10$ and rises again to $0.17$ in the largest, estimation noise
dominating among small firms and floors among large ones.  Panels that include very small firms, as in
Ref.~\cite{fontanelli2025}, should therefore show ratios close to or above $q$ unless short lives
are dropped, and ratios that fall below $q$ as the minimum life length grows.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The ratio curve of firm volatility bends below $q$ exactly when the dispersion of volatilities among firms
of equal size grows with size.  When a firm's growth variance is a floor plus a part that falls with size,
this happens only if floors differ between firms of equal size; the noise of estimating a volatility from
a short life works the other way.  The criterion does not depend on a model.  Granular models sit at its
two ends: well-diversified firms give a rescaled volatility whose law does not depend on size, on the
boundary, and firms made of a few subunits stretch its upper tail so much that the ratios fall towards
$1$.  Firm data lie between, and so does the economy we build.  The corrections that MSB invoke to bring
a granular model between the limits would act through the same growth of $K_q$ with size, but they give
the length of a life, and the years after entry and before exit, no role beyond estimation noise, which
separates them from our mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions}).

In an economy of interacting firms the floors differ for two reasons.  A firm's floor contains the climb
after its entry and the collapse before its exit, spread over its life, and lives end at memoryless dips
of the field, so their lengths are broad.  And the degree of a firm's node sets the amplitude of its field,
which keeps the floors of the larger, better connected firms large against their noise and makes their
differences visible.  Removing the degree heterogeneity removes the bend, and keeping short lives reverses
it; one criterion accounts for the three.  None of this is put in by hand.  A granular model takes the
structure of firms and the independence of their shocks as given; here the floors, the life lengths that
spread them, entries and exits, and the co-movement of large firms all come from one dynamics of
interacting firms.

Two limits are clear.  The theory is a one-firm problem given three statistics of the field, but at finite
connectivity a Gaussian closure predicts neither the amplitude of the field's motion, set close to a
marginal amplification, nor its spread between firms of equal degree, which carries part of the bend; a
closure that keeps the
differences between firms of equal degree, such as the cavity method solved by population dynamics, is
open.  And the model spreads the whole distribution of volatilities at fixed size, while in Compustat only
its upper tail spreads; the self-limitation that makes volatility depend on size also caps firm size, so
the range is two decades rather than four and there is no Zipf law.  A dynamics that decouples the decline
of volatility from the size cap, and a theory of the co-movement of large firms, are the next steps.  The
tests of Sec.~\ref{sec:predictions} do not depend on these limits: they ask whether firm data show the
dispersion, the role of life length and the response to trimming that the mechanism implies.

\section{Relative sizes and the one-firm equation}
\label{app:relative}

This appendix goes from the model, Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, to the equation of one firm in its field,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm}, and to the standard model it maps onto.  Throughout, $S_i=x_i/m$ is the relative size, so that
$\sum_iS_i=N$; $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is an average over degree classes or nodes, $\E$ an average over the disorder or the
noise, and an overbar a time or life average; $\phi$ and $\PhiN$ are the standard normal density and distribution
function, and $\phi_0=\phi(0)=1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.

\subsection{The mean firm}

Write $F_i=1-S_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j$, so that the drift of $x_i$ is $x_iF_i$ (because $x_j/m=S_j$).  Summing
Eq.~\eqref{eq:model} over $i$ and dividing by $N$,
\begin{equation}
\dd m=\frac1N\sum_jmS_jF_j\,\dd t+\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{D\,m\cdot mS_j}\,\dd W_j
=m\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],
\label{eq:app-dm}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
\fb_0=\frac1N\sum_jS_jF_j,\qquad \dd Z=\frac1N\sum_j\sqrt{DS_j}\,\dd W_j,\qquad
(\dd Z)^2=\frac{1}{N^2}\sum_jDS_j\,\dd t=\frac DN\,\dd t ,
\label{eq:app-Z}
\end{equation}
where the last equality uses $\sum_jS_j=N$.  The common noise $Z$ has quadratic variation of order
$1/N$.

\subsection{It\^o's rule for the quotient}

For $S_i=x_i/m$ It\^o's rule for $f(x,m)=x/m$ gives
\begin{equation}
\dd S_i=\frac{\dd x_i}{m}-\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}+\frac{x_i\,(\dd m)^2}{m^3}-\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}.
\end{equation}
Term by term:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd x_i}{m}&=S_iF_i\,\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i,\\
\frac{x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=S_i\big[\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big],\\
\frac{x_i(\dd m)^2}{m^3}&=S_i\,(\dd Z)^2=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t,\\
\frac{\dd x_i\,\dd m}{m^2}&=\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i\cdot\frac1N\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i=\frac{DS_i}{N}\,\dd t ,
\end{align}
where the last line uses $\dd W_i\dd W_j=\delta_{ij}\dd t$.  The two It\^o corrections cancel exactly, and
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[F_i-\fb_0\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-S_i\,\dd Z .}
\label{eq:app-rel}
\end{equation}
This is exact between entries.

\paragraph{Conservation.}  Summing Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel},
$\sum_i\dd S_i=\big(\sum_iS_iF_i-N\fb_0\big)\dd t+\sum_i\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i-N\,\dd Z=0$
by the definitions of $\fb_0$ and $Z$.  So $\sum_iS_i=N$ is preserved between entries.

\paragraph{Size of the common-noise term.}  $S_i\,\dd Z$ has variance $S_i^2D\,\dd t/N$, against $DS_i\,\dd t$
for the own noise: relative size $S_i/N$.  It is of order $N^{-1/2}$ in amplitude and is dropped in the
one-firm equation.  It is the only place where the finite size of the economy enters the single-firm
dynamics directly.

\subsection{Entry dilution}

An exit at $S_i=0$ removes nothing from $m$.  The newborn adds $Dm$ to $\sum_jx_j$, so
$m\to m'=m(1+D/N)$, every incumbent's relative size is multiplied by $1/(1+D/N)$, and the newborn's
relative size is $D/(1+D/N)=D+O(1/N)$.  If entries happen at a rate $N\nu$ per unit time ($\nu$ per
node), then over $\dd t$
\begin{equation}
\frac{m(t+\dd t)}{m(t)}=(1+D/N)^{N\nu\,\dd t}\big[1+\fb_0\,\dd t+\dd Z\big]
\;\Rightarrow\; \fb=\fb_0+D\nu ,
\end{equation}
and each incumbent picks up the drift $-D\nu S_i$.  So the growth rate of the mean firm is
\begin{equation}
\fb(t)=\frac1N\sum_iS_iF_i+D\nu(t),
\label{eq:app-fbar}
\end{equation}
and in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-rel} $\fb_0$ is replaced by $\fb$.  Here $\nu(t)$ is the empirical entry rate, which fluctuates at finite $N$.  (At the reference point $D\nu\simeq0.06$.)

\subsection{The field and the one-firm equation}

Collect everything except the firm's own self-limitation into the field
\begin{equation}
\Phi_i(t)=F_i+S_i-\fb=1+\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j(t)-\fb(t).
\label{eq:app-field}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\dd S_i=S_i\big[\Phi_i(t)-S_i\big]\dd t+\sqrt{DS_i}\,\dd W_i+O(N^{-1/2}),}
\label{eq:app-onefirm}
\end{equation}
with exit at $S_i=0$ and entry at $S_i=D$.  This is exact as a rewriting; the content of the
``one firm is enough'' result is that $\Phi_i$ may be treated as an external input, i.e.\ that firm $i$'s own
noise does not feed back on $\Phi_i$ through its neighbours.  The feedback is of order
$J_{ij}J_{ji}\sim A_{ij}(\mu^2/C^2+\sigma^2\E[z_{ij}z_{ji}]/C)$, so at zero reciprocity it is $\mu^2/C^2$ per
neighbour and $O(1/C)$ in total after summing over $k\sim C$ neighbours.  The approximation is controlled in $1/C$ and is tested in Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}.

\paragraph{Plateau.}  At constant $\Phi>0$ the noise-free equation $\dot S=S(\Phi-S)$ has the stable fixed
point $S=\Phi$ ($\partial_S[S(\Phi-S)]=\Phi-2S=-\Phi<0$ there) and relaxes to it at rate $\Phi$.  For
$\Phi<0$, $S=0$ is the only non-negative fixed point and is attracting.  In absolute units the plateau is
$x_i=m\Phi$.

\subsection{Logarithmic form and ``size is mean field''}

By It\^o, $\dd\ln S=\dd S/S-\tfrac12(\dd S)^2/S^2$ with $(\dd S)^2=DS\,\dd t$:
\begin{equation}
\dd\ln S_i=\Big[\Phi_i-S_i-\frac{D}{2S_i}\Big]\dd t+\sqrt{\frac{D}{S_i}}\,\dd W_i .
\label{eq:app-log}
\end{equation}
Integrate over a window $[t_1,t_2]$ of length $T$ with $\ln S_i(t_2)=\ln S_i(t_1)$ and divide by $T$:
\begin{equation}
\Sb_i=\bar\Phi_i-\frac D2\,\overline{S_i^{-1}}+\frac1T\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{D/S_i}\,\dd W_i .
\end{equation}
Without noise, $\Sb_i=\bar\Phi_i$ exactly: a firm's mean size is its mean field.  With noise the
stochastic integral has zero mean for a window fixed in advance, but not for a window chosen by the path
(a life selected for its length), which is why the identity holds per life only to about 5\%.

\section{Heterogeneous mean-field theory}
\label{app:hdmft}

This appendix summarises the mean-field description of the network, Eq.~\eqref{eq:hdmft}, in the
notation we use.  It follows Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,poley2025,patil2026}, where the static solution without
noise and its stability are derived; Appendix~\ref{app:constant} adds the birth--death noise and the turnover.

\subsection{Degree classes and kernels}

Group the nodes into classes $p$ with population fractions $\rho_p$ and degrees $k_p$.  Let $P_{pr}$ be the
probability that a given node of class $p$ is linked to a given node of class $r$.  The expected number
of class-$r$ neighbours of a class-$p$ node is $N\rho_rP_{pr}$, and the degree constraint is
$\sum_rN\rho_rP_{pr}=k_p$.  Define
\begin{equation}
K_{pr}=\frac{N\rho_rP_{pr}}{C}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sum_rK_{pr}=\kappa_p .
\end{equation}
\emph{Uncorrelated (bilinear) kernel:} $P_{pr}=k_pk_r/(NC)$, so $K_{pr}=\rho_r\kappa_p\kappa_r$; it satisfies
the constraint because $\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r=1$.  It is a probability only if $k_pk_r\le NC$; at $\alpha=2.5$
the largest degrees violate this (structural cutoff $k\sim\sqrt{NC}$).  \emph{Soft configuration} \cite{patil2026}:
$P_{pr}=h_ph_r/(h_ph_r+C/N)$, always in $[0,1]$, with hidden degrees $h_p$ chosen so that the constraint
holds; for $h_ph_r\ll C/N$ it reduces to the bilinear form with $h_p\propto k_p$.

\subsection{The mean part of the interaction field}

The interaction term on node $i$ in class $p$ is
$\sum_jJ_{ij}S_j=\frac\mu C\sum_jA_{ij}S_j+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt C}\sum_jA_{ij}z_{ij}S_j$.
The first sum has $k_i\sim C$ terms; by the law of large numbers over neighbours drawn with the kernel,
\begin{equation}
\frac\mu C\sum_jA_{ij}S_j\;\to\;\frac\mu C\sum_rN\rho_rP_{pr}\,\E[S_r]=\mu\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r]\equiv\mu B_p ,
\end{equation}
with fluctuations of relative order $k^{-1/2}$, i.e.\ an absolute correction $O(C^{-1/2})$.

\subsection{The random part: cavity argument}

Remove node $0$ (the cavity) and let the rest of the system evolve; the neighbours' sizes $S_j^{\setminus0}(t)$
are then independent of the couplings $z_{0j}$.  Reinserting node $0$ has two effects.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Direct field.}  $\eta_0(t)=C^{-1/2}\sum_jA_{0j}z_{0j}S_j^{\setminus0}(t)$ is a sum of $k_0$
  independent zero-mean terms conditional on the cavity trajectories, so by the central limit theorem it is a
  Gaussian process with covariance
  \begin{equation}
  \E[\eta_0(t)\eta_0(t')]=\frac1C\sum_jA_{0j}S_j(t)S_j(t')\;\to\;\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')]\equiv q_p(t,t') .
  \end{equation}
\item \emph{Reaction.}  Node $0$ perturbs each neighbour $j$ by $J_{j0}S_0$; the response of $j$ is
  $\chi_j J_{j0}S_0$ and feeds back on $0$ through $J_{0j}$.  The total is
  $\sum_jJ_{0j}J_{j0}\chi_jS_0$, whose mean is $\sum_jA_{0j}(\mu^2/C^2+\sigma^2\E[z_{0j}z_{j0}]/C)\chi_j=O(\mu^2/C)+0$
  at zero reciprocity.  It vanishes for $C\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
So for $C\to\infty$ the field on a class-$p$ node is
\begin{equation}
\Phi_p(t)=1-\fb(t)+\mu B_p(t)+\sigma\eta_p(t),\qquad \E[\eta_p(t)\eta_p(t')]=q_p(t,t'),
\label{eq:app-effproc}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B_p(t)=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)],\qquad q_p(t,t')=\sum_rK_{pr}\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\label{eq:app-closure}
\end{equation}
Each class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} with this field; Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} closes the problem, and
$\fb(t)$ is fixed by $\sum_p\rho_p\E[S_p(t)]=1$ (which is $\sum_iS_i=N$, not an extra approximation).
This is the standard dynamical cavity derivation
\cite{bunin2017,galla2018,aguirrelopez} with degree classes.

\subsection{Bilinear kernel: the field law}

With $K_{pr}=\rho_r\kappa_p\kappa_r$, both closures factorise:
\begin{equation}
B_p=\kappa_p\bar B,\quad \bar B=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r];\qquad q_p=\kappa_pQ,\quad Q(t,t')=\sum_r\rho_r\kappa_r\E[S_r(t)S_r(t')] .
\end{equation}
$\bar B$ and $Q$ are degree-weighted averages (a random neighbour is drawn in proportion to its degree).
Writing $\eta_p=\sqrt{\kappa_p}\,\xi$ with $\E[\xi(t)\xi(t')]=Q(t,t')$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Phi_\kappa(t)=1-\fb(t)+\mu\kappa\bar B+\sigma\sqrt\kappa\,\xi(t).}
\label{eq:app-fieldlaw}
\end{equation}
Under competition ($\mu<0$, $\bar B>0$) the field's mean falls linearly in $\kappa$; its amplitude grows as
$\sqrt\kappa$.  (On the reference graph the bilinear kernel is not a probability for the hubs,
so the factorisation is approximate there; the measured amplitude exponent $0.54$ against $0.5$ is measured.)

\section{One firm with birth--death noise at constant field, and the instability line with noise}
\label{app:constant}

With noise a firm in a constant field no longer sits at a fixed size: it fluctuates around its plateau, or fails
and is replaced.  This appendix solves that problem exactly, uses the solution in place of the clipped Gaussian
of the noise-free theory \cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} to obtain the phase lines with noise, and tests the instability line in simulation.
Local stationary laws with demographic noise have been derived on sparse graphs with immigration, which keeps
every species present \cite{machado2025}; here zero is absorbing, and a newborn replaces the firm that exits.

\subsection{Scale function, speed measure and boundaries}

At constant $\Phi$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-onefirm} is the one-dimensional diffusion
$\dd S=b(S)\,\dd t+\sqrt{a(S)}\,\dd W$ with $b(y)=y(\Phi-y)$ and $a(y)=Dy$, generator
$\mathcal L=\tfrac12Dy\,\partial_y^2+y(\Phi-y)\partial_y$.  The scale density and speed density
\cite{karlintaylor1981} are
\begin{equation}
s'(y)=\exp\Big[-\int_0^y\frac{2b(u)}{a(u)}\dd u\Big]=\exp\Big[-\frac2D\Big(\Phi y-\frac{y^2}2\Big)\Big],\qquad
m(y)=\frac{2}{a(y)s'(y)}=\frac{2}{Dy\,s'(y)},
\end{equation}
since $2b/a=2(\Phi-u)/D$.  Let $s(y)=\int_0^ys'(u)\,\dd u$.  Properties:
\begin{itemize}
\item Near $0$, $s(y)\simeq y$ and $m(y)\simeq2/(Dy)$; $\int_0s(y)m(y)\,\dd y<\infty$, so $0$ is reached in finite time
  (an exit boundary in Feller's classification).
\item $s'(y)\sim e^{y^2/D}$ for large $y$, so $s(\infty)=\infty$: $\infty$ is never reached, and the process started anywhere
  hits $0$ with probability one.  Every life ends, so at a node with constant field the lives form a proper
  renewal sequence.
\item $\int^\infty m(z)\,\dd z<\infty$ (Gaussian decay).
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Mean exit time}

$T(x)$ solves $\mathcal LT=-1$ with $T(0)=0$ and no flux at infinity.  Since $s''/s'=-2b/a$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd y}\Big(\frac{T'}{s'}\Big)=\frac{T''+(2b/a)T'}{s'}=\frac{2\,\mathcal LT}{a\,s'}=-\frac{2}{a\,s'}=-m(y).
\end{equation}
Integrating from $y$ to $\infty$ with $T'/s'\to0$ at infinity, $T'(y)=s'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z$, and
\begin{equation}
T(x)=\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty m(z)\,\dd z\,\dd y=2\int_0^xs'(y)\int_y^\infty\frac{\dd z}{Dz\,s'(z)}\,\dd y .
\label{eq:app-T}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Green's function and the renewal law}

Exchanging the order of integration in Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} (Fubini; all integrands positive),
\begin{equation}
T(x_0)=\int_0^\infty m(z)\int_0^{\min(x_0,z)}s'(y)\,\dd y\,\dd z=\int_0^\infty\underbrace{s(\min(x_0,z))\,m(z)}_{G(x_0,z)}\dd z .
\end{equation}
$G(x_0,z)$ is the Green's function: the expected time spent near $z$, per unit $z$, before absorption, starting at $x_0$.

\paragraph{Renewal--reward.}  With replacement at $D$, the successive lives at a node are i.i.d.\ copies of the
process started at $D$.  For any set $\mathcal A$, the long-run fraction of time spent in $\mathcal A$ equals
$\E[\text{time in }\mathcal A\text{ during one life}]/\E[\text{life length}]$.  So the stationary density of sizes at the node is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{p(x\mid h)=\frac{G(D,x)}{T(D)}=\frac{2\,s(\min(x,D))}{D\,x\,s'(x)\,T(D)},\qquad T(D)=\int_0^\infty G(D,x)\,\dd x,}
\label{eq:renewal}
\end{equation}
and by the elementary renewal theorem the turnover rate is $1/T(D)$ per node per unit time.

\paragraph{Shape.}  For $x>D$, $s(\min(x,D))=s(D)$ is constant and
$1/(xs'(x))=x^{-1}\exp[(2/D)(hx-x^2/2)]=x^{-1}e^{h^2/D}e^{-(x-h)^2/D}$: a Gaussian factor of variance $D/2$ around
$h$ times $x^{-1}$.  For $x<D$ and $x\ll\sqrt D$, $s(x)\simeq x$ and $s'(x)\simeq1$, so $p\simeq2/(DT(D))$: flat.


\subsection{Two limits of the exit time}

Two limits are used below.  For a negative field, $-\Phi\gg\sqrt D$, the rescaling $y=D\tilde y$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-T} and the limit $D\to0$ give the exit time of a newborn as the Feller value
$T(D)\to|\Phi|^{-1}\int_0^{2|\Phi|}e^tE_1(t)\,\dd t$, with $E_1$ the exponential integral: it does not depend
on $D$.  For a firm on its plateau, $\Phi>0$, Laplace's method on the peak of $m(z)$ at $z\simeq\Phi$ gives
$T\simeq\sqrt{\pi D}\,\Phi^{-2}e^{\Phi^2/D}$ to exponential order.  An established firm therefore exits
through dips of its field rather than through its own noise.

\subsection{The static closure with noise}

When the field is static, each node follows the renewal law \eqref{eq:renewal} at its own $h=a+\mu B_p+\sigma\sqrt{q_p}\,z$, with $a=1-f^*$, $f^*$ the stationary growth rate of the mean firm, and $z$ a frozen standard Gaussian.
Let $\langle S\rangle_h=\int xp(x\mid h)\,\dd x$.  The static closure is Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} with time averages:
\begin{equation}
B_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r},\qquad q_p=\sum_rK_{pr}\E\langle S\rangle_{h_r}^2,\qquad \sum_p\rho_p\E\langle S\rangle_{h_p}=1,
\end{equation}
with $\E$ the Gaussian average over $z$.  Using $\langle S\rangle^2_h$ in $q_p$ counts only the static part of the neighbours'
sizes in the field covariance, i.e.\ the temporal fluctuations of $S$ produced by the noise are taken as not
feeding the field.  (Using $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ instead, ``as if quenched'', moves the lines by less than $0.015$.)

\subsection{The susceptibility and the instability line}
\label{app:susceptibility}

The response argument of Refs.~\cite{opperdiederich1992,bunin2017,aguirrelopez,patil2026} carries over with one change.  Shift the growth rate of every
node by a small static amount $\delta h_i$, independent between nodes, of zero mean and variance $\varsigma^2$.  The
time-averaged size of a node responds to the shift of its own field and to the shifts of its neighbours' mean
sizes,
\begin{equation}
\delta\langle S_i\rangle=\chi_i\Big(\delta h_i+\sum_jJ_{ij}\,\delta\langle S_j\rangle\Big),\qquad
\chi=\frac{\partial\langle S\rangle_h}{\partial h},
\end{equation}
where $\chi_i$ is evaluated at the node's own field.  By the cavity argument the sum is a Gaussian of variance
$\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r$ for a node of class $p$, independent of $\chi_i$, with $Y_r=\E[\delta\langle S\rangle_r^2]$.
Squaring and averaging,
\begin{equation}
Y_p=\E[\chi_p^2]\Big(\varsigma^2+\sigma^2\sum_rK_{pr}Y_r\Big)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Y=\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2\,\varsigma^2 .
\end{equation}
The mean squared response per unit variance of the shift is the spin-glass susceptibility,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\chi_{\rm SG}=\frac1{\varsigma^2}\sum_p\rho_pY_p=\rho^{\sf T}\big(\mathbb{I}-\sigma^2\,{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K\big)^{-1}\E\chi^2 .}
\label{eq:chisg}
\end{equation}
It is finite while the Perron root $\Lambda$ of $\sigma^2{\rm diag}(\E\chi^2)K$ is below one and diverges as
$\Lambda\to1$, which defines $\sigma_c(\mu,D)$.  Without noise $\langle S\rangle_h=[h]_+$, $\chi=\theta(h)$, $\chi^2=\chi$ and
$\E\chi^2=\varphi$: the deterministic criterion of Refs.~\cite{aguirrelopez,patil2026} is recovered.  With noise $\chi$ is a smooth function of $h$ in place of a step, and the line moves up, almost linearly in $D$ (below).

\paragraph{Numerical solution.}  We tabulate $\langle S\rangle_h$, $\langle S^2\rangle_h$ and $\ln T(D;h)$ once per $D$ on a grid of fields,
by quadrature of Eq.~\eqref{eq:renewal} in logarithms, with a logarithmic grid below $D$, and obtain $\chi$ by
differentiation.  The closure is iterated with damping on $(B_p,q_p)$, the normalisation being solved for $a$ at
every step, with $60$ degree classes of equal population built from the realised degree sequence and the
soft-configuration kernel.  The instability line is the root in $\sigma$ of $\Lambda=1$, and the zero-growth line the
root of $a=1$.

\paragraph{Turnover.}  $\sum_p\rho_p\E[1/T(D;h_p)]$ per node per unit time.  Nodes with $h<0$ dominate it, and by
the first limit above their newborns' lives are nearly $D$-independent: turnover is nearly independent of the noise.

\subsection{The instability line in simulation}
\label{app:linetest}

\paragraph{Protocol.}  Equation~\eqref{eq:chisg} can be measured directly.  For each point we run three economies with
the same couplings and the same realisation of the noise ($D=0.1$, total time $700$, the first $200$ discarded,
$N=4000$, $C=100$): a base run; a run in which the growth rate of every firm is shifted by $\varsigma\eta_i$, with
$\eta_i$ independent standard Gaussians and $\varsigma=0.01$; and a run from other initial sizes.  The mean squared
difference between the time-averaged sizes of the first two, divided by $\varsigma^2$, is $\chi_{\rm SG}$; using the
same noise removes most of the sampling error.  The third run tells whether the composition is slaved to the
noise.  Below the line two economies with the same noise forget their initial sizes and follow each other;
above it the composition has a motion of its own and they separate.  We measure their distance
$d=\overline{(S_i^A-S_i^B)^2}/(2\Var S)$ over the last $100$ samples, which is $1$ for independent economies.  For
each graph we take nine values of $\sigma$ around the predicted line: $\alpha=2.5$ at $\mu=0$, $-0.5$, $-1$, $-1.76$ and
$-2.5$, and $\alpha=6$ at $\mu=0$, $-1.76$ and $-2.5$, two graphs per point.  The theory uses the degree classes of the
same graphs, so its lines differ from those of Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}, which are for the reference graph ($N=32000$,
$C=200$): at $\mu=0$ and $\alpha=2.5$, $0.95$ here against $0.83$ there.

field as frozen.

below the line ($+0.19$ against $+0.15$ and $0.160$ against $0.148$ at $\sigma=0.70$; $+0.72$ against $+0.56$ at
$\sigma=0.88$).  There the hubs survive, and $60$ degree classes at $C=100$ may not resolve them; we have not
tested this explanation.

\section{Two-time closure}
\label{app:closure}

Each degree class obeys Eq.~\eqref{eq:onefirm} with a Gaussian field closed by Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure}; at
zero reciprocity there is no reaction term.  We solve the closure in the stationary state at the reference
point by sampling the effective process \cite{eissfeller1992,roy2019} (\texttt{scripts/two\_time\_dmft.py}),
with $31$ degree classes.  Evaluated on the measured sizes, Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-closure} gives the temporal
and frozen parts of the measured field to within $4\%$ and $6\%$.  Solved self-consistently, it gets the
mean field, but its temporal amplitude is $0.71$--$0.81$ of the measured one in every class, and its life
panel has $\zeta_1=0.34$ and ratios $1.85$, $2.53$, $3.07$, against $0.25$ and $1.79$, $2.37$, $2.80$.

The cause is the finite connectivity.  At $C=200$ a few strongly fluctuating neighbours dominate the
temporal field of a node, so its amplitude varies between nodes of equal degree, with a coefficient of
variation of $0.24$--$0.36$, and the closure replaces this spread by its mean.  Started from the measured
state of one economy, one step of the iteration returns $0.955\pm0.01$ of the temporal field variance.  A
loss of $4.5\%$ per step that halves the self-consistent variance means a loop gain of about $0.9$: the
temporal field is set close to marginal amplification.  With the temporal field taken from simulation, an
amplitude $2.15\sqrt\kappa$ and an exponential correlation of $1/e$ time $4.5$, the panel has $\zeta_1=0.26$
and ratios $1.85$, $2.54$, $3.08$; the rest of the gap is the spread of amplitudes at equal degree
(Sec.~\ref{sec:onefirm}).  Closures that keep this spread, such as the dynamic cavity method solved by
population dynamics \cite{tarabolo2025,tonolo2025}, are the natural next step.

\subsection{Derivation of the volatility law}

Start from Eq.~\eqref{eq:app-log}.  Let $y_F$ solve the noise-free equation $\dot y_F=\Phi-e^{y_F}$ along the same field and from
the same initial size, and write $\ln S=y_F+\delta$.  Subtracting,
\begin{equation}
\dd\delta=-e^{y_F}\big(e^{\delta}-1\big)\dd t-\frac{D}{2S}\,\dd t+\sqrt{\frac DS}\,\dd W .
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
\item Linearise in $\delta$: $-e^{y_F}(e^\delta-1)\simeq-S_F\delta$ with $S_F=e^{y_F}\simeq S$.
\item Freeze the coefficients over the relaxation time $1/S$ (adiabatic): $\delta$ is an OU process with rate $k=S$ and noise
  intensity $D/S$, stationary variance $v_\delta=(D/S)/(2S)=D/(2S^2)$.
\item The It\^o drift $-D/2S$ shifts the mean of $\delta$, not its fluctuations, to this order.
\end{itemize}
The increment over $\Delta t$ has variance $2v_\delta(1-e^{-k\Delta t})$:
\begin{equation}
\Var(\Delta\delta)=\frac{D}{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big).
\end{equation}
The growth rate is $g=\Delta y_F+\Delta\delta$.  Taking the two parts as uncorrelated (the noise $W$ is independent of the field,
and $y_F$ is a functional of the field only) and averaging the local variance over the life:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Var g\simeq V_F+B,\qquad B=\Big\langle\frac D{S^2}\big(1-e^{-S\Delta t}\big)\Big\rangle_{\rm life},}
\end{equation}
where $V_F$ is the growth variance of $y_F$ over the life.  Limits: $S\Delta t\ll1$ gives $B\to D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle$; $S\Delta t\gg1$ gives
$B\to D\langle S^{-2}\rangle$..

\paragraph{Conditions.}  (1) $\delta$ small: $v_\delta=D/(2S^2)\ll1$, i.e.\ $S\gg\sqrt{D/2}\simeq0.22$ at $D=0.1$ (on the plateau, a simulation of one node at constant field gives a variance error of about $2D/S^2$, i.e.\ $20\%$ at $S=1$; along a life the relevant $S$ is the instantaneous one, and
the error is partly averaged out over a life that also visits larger sizes).  (2) $S$ varies slowly on the time $1/S$.  (3) The cross term $2\Cov(\Delta y_F,\Delta\delta)$ is small: $\delta$
relaxes towards $y_F$, so the deviation and the path are not exactly independent once the linearisation fails.  All three are
worst for small firms, consistent with the 9\% error in the lowest decile ($\Sb=0.26$) against 5\% elsewhere.

\paragraph{Why the filter matters.}  If a firm followed its field exactly it would sit on $S=\Phi$, with $\Var g=\Var\Delta\ln\Phi$ plus unfiltered noise
$D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle$.  The noise part changes little (at $\Delta t=0.5$, $\sqrt{D\Delta t\langle S^{-1}\rangle}$ is within 6\% of $\sqrt B$),
but $\Var\Delta\ln\Phi$ is much larger than $V_F$ for small firms: $\ln\Phi$ moves violently when $\Phi$ is small, while $y_F$
low-pass filters the field at rate $S$.  (Numbers: $\sqrt{\Var\Delta\ln\Phi}$ from $0.92$ to $0.44$, $\sqrt{V_F}$ flat at $0.32$--$0.37$.)

\section{The criterion, the floor and the length of a life}
\label{app:multiscaling}

This appendix derives the results of Secs.~\ref{sec:criterion} and \ref{sec:floor}: the general relation between the
ratio curve and the dispersion at fixed size, the identity for a growth variance made of a floor and a falling
part, the conditions under which the curve bends, the make-up of the floor, and the law of life lengths.

\subsection{The general criterion}
\label{app:cumulant}

Write $v=m(\Sb)\,X$ with $m(\Sb)=\E[v\mid\Sb]$, so that $\E[X\mid\Sb]=1$.  Then
$\ln\E[v^q\mid\Sb]=q\ln m(\Sb)+K_q(\Sb)$ with $K_q=\ln\E[X^q\mid\Sb]$, and $K_1=0$.  Differentiating in $\ln\Sb$, the
case $q=1$ gives $\zeta_1=-d\ln m/d\ln\Sb$, and the general case
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q=q\,\zeta_1-\frac{dK_q}{d\ln\Sb},
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:general}.  No assumption has been made.  $K_q$ is the cumulant generating function of $\ln X$
at fixed size, $K_q=\ln\E[e^{q\ln X}]=\sum_{n\ge1}c_nq^n/n!$ with $c_n(\Sb)$ the cumulants of $\ln X$.  It is convex in
$q$ (H\"older's inequality) and vanishes at $q=0$ and $q=1$, so it is negative between them and positive for
$q>1$.  Since $K_1=\sum_nc_n/n!=0$, subtracting $q$ times this from $K_q$ removes the mean $c_1$:
\begin{equation}
\zeta_q-q\zeta_1=-\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{q^n-q}{n!}\,\frac{dc_n}{d\ln\Sb}
=-\frac{q(q-1)}2\,\frac{d\Var(\ln X\mid\Sb)}{d\ln\Sb}-\frac{q(q^2-1)}{6}\,\frac{dc_3}{d\ln\Sb}-\dots
\end{equation}
To second order the curve lies below $q$ if and only if the variance of $\ln v$ at fixed size grows with size, and
above $q$ if it shrinks.  For a lognormal $X$ only $c_2=\sigma^2(\Sb)$ is left and the mean of $\ln X$ is $-\sigma^2/2$, so
$K_q=q(q-1)\sigma^2/2$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal}.  If the law of $X$ does not depend on size,
every $c_n$ is constant and $\zeta_q=q\zeta_1$.

\subsection{The identity for a floor and a falling part}

Assume $v_i^2=(u_i+B(\Sb_i))\,\epsilon_i^2$, with $\epsilon_i$ independent of $u_i$ and of $\Sb_i$ (estimation noise), and $B$ a function
of size alone.  Let $p(u\mid\Sb)$ be the law of the floor at fixed size and
\begin{equation}
E_q(\Sb)=\int p(u\mid\Sb)\,(u+B(\Sb))^{q/2}\,\dd u,\qquad \E[v^q\mid\Sb]=E_q(\Sb)\,\E[\epsilon^q].
\end{equation}
Since $\E[\epsilon^q]$ does not depend on size, $\zeta_q=-\dd\ln E_q/\dd\ln\Sb$.  Differentiate under the integral; there are two
contributions, from $B(\Sb)$ inside the power and from $p$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\dd E_q}{\dd\ln\Sb}=\int p\,\frac q2(u+B)^{q/2-1}\frac{\dd B}{\dd\ln\Sb}\,\dd u+\int\frac{\partial p}{\partial\ln\Sb}(u+B)^{q/2}\dd u .
\end{equation}
With $\dd B/\dd\ln\Sb=-bB$ and $(u+B)^{q/2-1}B=(u+B)^{q/2}w$, $w=B/(u+B)$, the first term is $-\tfrac q2bE_q\langle w\rangle_q$, where
\begin{equation}
\langle w\rangle_q=\frac{\E[(u+B)^{q/2}w\mid\Sb]}{\E[(u+B)^{q/2}\mid\Sb]} .
\end{equation}
Dividing by $-E_q$:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\zeta_q=\frac q2\,b\,\langle w\rangle_q-c_q,\qquad c_q=\frac1{E_q}\int\frac{\partial p(u\mid\Sb)}{\partial\ln\Sb}(u+B)^{q/2}\,\dd u .}
\label{eq:app-identity}
\end{equation}
$c_q=0$ when the law of the floor does not depend on size.
The composition term does not vanish on the network: larger lives have larger degrees on average, and the floor grows
with the degree, so $c_q>0$ and the decline is slower than the noise share alone implies.

\paragraph{Floor whose law shifts with size.}  If $\ln u=\delta(\Sb)+\xi$ with the law of $\xi$ fixed, then the size dependence of $p$
is entirely through $\delta$, and $\partial_{\ln\Sb}$ at fixed $B$ acts on $(e^{\delta+\xi}+B)^{q/2}$ as
$\tfrac q2(u+B)^{q/2-1}u\,\delta'$.  So
\begin{equation}
c_q=\frac q2\big(1-\langle w\rangle_q\big)\delta',\qquad
\zeta_q=\frac q2\Big[b\langle w\rangle_q-\big(1-\langle w\rangle_q\big)\delta'\Big],\qquad \delta'=\frac{\dd\delta}{\dd\ln\Sb}.
\end{equation}
A rising floor ($\delta'>0$) flattens the decline.  It also adds to the bend: write $\zeta_q=\tfrac q2[(b+\delta')x_q-\delta']$ with
$x_q=\langle w\rangle_q$.  For $x_q<x_1$ and $\zeta_q,\zeta_1>0$,
\begin{equation}
\frac{(b+\delta')x_q-\delta'}{(b+\delta')x_1-\delta'}<\frac{x_q}{x_1}\iff -x_1\delta'<-x_q\delta'\iff x_q<x_1 ,
\end{equation}
so the ratio is below the $c_q=0$ value $qx_q/x_1$.

\paragraph{Homogeneous floor.}  If $u$ is the same for all firms of a given size, however it depends on size, then
$E_q=Y(\Sb)^{q/2}$ with $Y=u+B$ and $\zeta_q=-\tfrac q2\dd\ln Y/\dd\ln\Sb=q\zeta_1$ exactly, whatever $c_q$.

\subsection{Monotonicity of $\langle w\rangle_q$ and the bound $\zeta_q/\zeta_1\le q$}

With $Y=u+B$ at fixed size, $\langle w\rangle_q=\E[Y^{q/2}\cdot B/Y]/\E[Y^{q/2}]=B\,\E[Y^{q/2-1}]/\E[Y^{q/2}]$.  Let
$h(q)=\ln\E[Y^{q/2}]=\ln\E[e^{q\cdot\frac12\ln Y}]$, the cumulant generating function of $\tfrac12\ln Y$; it is convex (H\"older).  Then
$\langle w\rangle_q=B\exp[h(q-2)-h(q)]$.  For a convex function the secant slope $[h(q)-h(q-2)]/2$ is non-decreasing in $q$, so
$\langle w\rangle_q$ is non-increasing, and strictly decreasing unless $h$ is linear, i.e.\ unless $\ln Y$ is a.s.\ constant at fixed size.
With $c_q=0$ (and $b$ the same for all $q$, as it is the slope of $B$),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}\le q\qquad(q\ge1),
\end{equation}
with equality iff the floor is the same for all firms of equal size.  $\epsilon$ cancels in the ratio.

\subsection{The lognormal case}

If $\ln Y\mid\Sb\sim\mathcal N(m,s^2)$, then $\E[Y^a]=e^{am+a^2s^2/2}$ and
\begin{equation}
\langle w\rangle_q=B\exp\Big[\Big(\frac q2-1\Big)m+\Big(\frac q2-1\Big)^2\frac{s^2}2-\frac q2m-\Big(\frac q2\Big)^2\frac{s^2}2\Big]
=B\,e^{-m}\,e^{(1-q)s^2/2},
\end{equation}
since $(q/2-1)^2-(q/2)^2=1-q$.  Hence
\begin{equation}
\frac{\langle w\rangle_q}{\langle w\rangle_1}=e^{-(q-1)s^2/2},\qquad \frac{\zeta_q}{\zeta_1}=q\,e^{-(q-1)s^2/2}\quad(c_q=0),
\qquad\frac{\langle w\rangle_4}{\langle w\rangle_1}=e^{-3s^2/2}.
\end{equation}
Exact for a lognormal $Y$; but $Y=u+B\ge B$, so $Y$ cannot be exactly lognormal and the formula is an approximation, good
when $s$ is small or $B$ is small compared with the typical $Y$.

\paragraph{Narrow floor.}  With $u=\bar ue^{\xi}$ and $\xi$ small, $\ln(u+B)\simeq\ln(\bar u+B)+\frac{\bar u}{\bar u+B}\xi$, so
$s^2\simeq(1-w)^2\Var(\ln u\mid\Sb)$ with $w$ at $\bar u$.  A heterogeneous floor bends the curve only where it is not small against
$B$: at large sizes.


\paragraph{Scope.}  The statement that the curve bends only if floors differ holds within the two-part law
$v^2=(u+B(\Sb))\epsilon^2$, with $B$ a function of size alone and $\epsilon$ independent of size.  Outside it the general
criterion of Sec.~\ref{app:cumulant} applies: any dispersion of $\ln v$ that grows with size bends the curve, and any
dispersion that shrinks with size pushes it above $q$.  The second case occurs in two places below: the
estimation noise of short lives, and the per-life estimate of $B$, whose dispersion is largest among the smallest
firms.

\subsection{Estimation noise}

Let a life have $n$ growth rates, independent and Gaussian with standard deviation $s$.  With the mean absolute
deviation, $v=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,n^{-1}\sum_t|g_t-\bar g|$.  For a Gaussian $\E|g-\bar g|\simeq s\sqrt{2/\pi}$, so $\E v\simeq s$,
and $\Var|g|=s^2(1-2/\pi)$, so
\begin{equation}
\Var\ln\epsilon\simeq\frac{\Var v}{(\E v)^2}=\frac1n\,\frac{1-2/\pi}{2/\pi}=\frac{\pi/2-1}{n}.
\end{equation}
With the standard deviation, $v^2$ is $s^2$ times a chi-squared variable over its number of degrees of freedom,
$\Var(v^2)/s^4\simeq2/n$, and $\Var\ln\epsilon=\tfrac14\Var\ln v^2\simeq1/(2n)$.  Both are leading orders in $1/n$, and fat
tails raise both.  If the mean number of growth rates grows with size, $\Var\ln\epsilon$ falls with size and by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-lognormal} adds $+\tfrac{q(q-1)}2\,|d\Var\ln\epsilon/d\ln\Sb|$ to $\zeta_q-q\zeta_1$: the ratios move up.

\subsection{What the floor is made of}

\paragraph{Splitting a life.}  The floor of a life is the variance of the growth rates $g^F_t$ of its noise-free path
$y_F$ (Appendix~\ref{app:reduction}),
$V_F=n^{-1}\sum_t(g^F_t)^2-(\bar g^F)^2$.  A complete life starts at the newborn size and ends at an exit, so its
mean growth rate $\bar g^F=n^{-1}\ln(S_{\rm end}/S_0)$ is small and we drop its square.  The core of a life runs
from the first to the last sample at which the noise-free size is at least half its median; before it is the
climb and after it the collapse.  Splitting the sum,
\begin{equation}
V_F\simeq\frac{n_{\rm core}}{n}\,V_{\rm core}+\frac{E_{\rm in}+E_{\rm out}}{n},\qquad
E_{\rm in}=\sum_{\rm climb}(g^F_t)^2,\quad E_{\rm out}=\sum_{\rm collapse}(g^F_t)^2 ,
\end{equation}
and for the long lives considered $n_{\rm core}\simeq n$, which gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.  For a path that changes
slowly over one step, $g^F_t\simeq(d\ln S/dt)\,\Delta t$ and a sum of squares over a stretch is
$\sum_t(g^F_t)^2\simeq\Delta t\int(d\ln S/dt)^2\,dt$.

\paragraph{The climb.}  In a constant field $h$ the noise-free equation is $\dot S=S(h-S)$.  With $u=S/h$,
$\dot u=hu(1-u)$ and $d\ln S/dt=h(1-u)$.  Changing variable from $t$ to $u$, $dt=du/[hu(1-u)]$, and from the
initial size $S_0$ to the plateau,
\begin{equation}
\int\Big(\frac{d\ln S}{dt}\Big)^2dt=\int_{S_0/h}^1\frac{h^2(1-u)^2}{hu(1-u)}\,du=h\int_{S_0/h}^1\frac{1-u}{u}\,du
=h\Big[\ln\frac h{S_0}-1+\frac{S_0}h\Big].
\end{equation}
Times $\Delta t$ this is $E_{\rm in}\simeq\Delta t\,h[\ln(h/S_0)-1]$ for $S_0\ll h$.

\paragraph{The collapse.}  In a field that has fallen to $-\varphi<0$, $\dot S=-S(\varphi+S)$ and $d\ln S/dt=-(\varphi+S)$.
With $dt=-dS/[S(\varphi+S)]$, from the plateau $h$ down to $S_{\rm end}$,
\begin{equation}
\int\Big(\frac{d\ln S}{dt}\Big)^2dt=\int_{S_{\rm end}}^h\frac{\varphi+S}{S}\,dS=\varphi\ln\frac h{S_{\rm end}}+h-S_{\rm end}.
\end{equation}
Times $\Delta t$ this is $E_{\rm out}\simeq\Delta t\,[\varphi\ln(h/S_{\rm end})+h]$.  Both terms grow with the plateau $h$,
and both are divided by the length of the life in Eq.~\eqref{eq:floor}.

\paragraph{Test.}  On the $175\,171$ complete lives with at
least $20$ growth rates, the exit formula follows the measured sum life by life (log-correlation
$0.85$--$0.91$ by fifth) and lies above it by $6$--$40\%$; the entry formula has the right scale but
correlates less (log-correlation $0.5$), because a firm climbs in a field that moves.

\subsection{How long a firm lives}

\paragraph{A firm in a dip is a branching process.}  An established firm sits near its plateau, where its own
noise cannot kill it: the exit time grows as $e^{\Phi^2/D}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:constant}).  It dies when its field dips
below zero.  Inside the dip the firm is small and the logistic term $-S^2$ is negligible, so
\begin{equation}
dS=r(t)\,S\,dt+\sqrt{DS}\,dW,
\end{equation}
with $r(t)$ the field less the self-limitation along the noise-free path.  This is Feller's branching diffusion
with a time-dependent rate \cite{feller1951}.  Because independent pieces of the firm evolve independently, the
Laplace transform of its size is exponential in the initial size, $\E[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-S_0u_0}$, where $u_0$ is
the value at time $0$ of the solution of the backward equation $-\partial_su=r(s)\,u-\tfrac12Du^2$ with $u(t)=\lambda$.
The equation is linear in $1/u$, $\partial_s(1/u)=r/u-D/2$, and with $R(t)=\int_0^tr(s)\,ds$ its solution is
\begin{equation}
\frac1{u_0}=\frac{e^{-R(t)}}{\lambda}+\frac D2\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds .
\end{equation}
The probability that the firm is extinct by time $t$ is the limit $\lambda\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}
\Pr(S_t=0)=\exp\Big[-\frac{2S_0}{D\int_0^te^{-R(s)}\,ds}\Big].
\end{equation}
The noise-free path through the dip is $S_{\rm det}(t)=S_0e^{R(t)}$, so $S_0^{-1}\int e^{-R}ds=\int dt/S_{\rm det}$, which
gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:kill}: $P_{\rm kill}=\exp[-2/(DJ)]$ with $J=\int dt/S_{\rm det}$.  For a constant rate $r>0$ and
$t\to\infty$ it reduces to the classical survival probability $1-e^{-2rS_0/D}$.

\paragraph{The bottom of the dip.}  $J$ is dominated by the times at which the noise-free path is smallest.  At its
minimum $\dot y_F=\Phi-S_{\min}=0$, and nearby $\ln S_{\rm det}\simeq\ln S_{\min}+\tfrac12\Phi't^2$, with $\Phi'$ the slope of
the field as it recovers.  A Gaussian integral gives
\begin{equation}
J\simeq\frac1{S_{\min}}\int e^{-\Phi't^2/2}\,dt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}}{S_{\min}} .
\end{equation}
A dip kills when it brings the firm below a size of order $D$ times its recovery time $\sqrt{2\pi/\Phi'}$.

\paragraph{Test of the kill law.}  For every downcrossing of zero by the field of a node whose firm is established
($S\ge0.3$), we restart the noise-free path at the firm's actual size, integrate until it recovers to that size,
and compare $P_{\rm kill}$ with whether the firm died.  Over the $446\,554$ dips the predicted kill fraction is
$0.725$ against $0.722$ observed.  Binned by the predicted probability, the observed kill fractions are $0.015$ at
$0.027$, $0.102$ at $0.147$, $0.418$ at $0.450$, $0.894$ at $0.853$ and $0.986$ at $0.974$, and the log-likelihood per
dip is $-0.175$ against $-0.591$ for a constant kill rate.  No parameter is fitted.

\paragraph{The rate of dips.}  For a stationary Gaussian field of mean $\bar\Phi$, standard deviation $f$ and
autocorrelation $\rho(\tau)$, downcrossings of zero occur at Rice's rate \cite{rice1944}
\begin{equation}
\nu=\frac{\sqrt{-\rho''(0)}}{2\pi}\,e^{-u^2/2},\qquad u=\frac{\bar\Phi}{f},
\end{equation}
the product of the density of the field at zero and the mean downward speed there.  We estimate $\rho''(0)$ from
the lag-$\Delta t$ autocorrelation, $\rho(\Delta t)\simeq1+\tfrac12\rho''(0)\Delta t^2$.  Rice's rate, from each node's own mean,
standard deviation and autocorrelation, lies $1$--$13\%$ above the observed rate in every decile of $u$ but the
lowest; sampling every $\Delta t$ misses brief crossings.

\paragraph{Lives are memoryless.}  The hazard of an established firm is the rate of dips times the mean kill
probability of a dip, $\nu\,\overline{P_{\rm kill}}$.  Successive dips are nearly independent, so the life of an
established firm is close to exponential, with a mean that grows as $e^{u^2/2}$: steeply with the mean field over
its amplitude.  Among complete lives the coefficient of variation of $n-20$ is $1.05$--$1.22$ in every decile of
$u$ but the highest, where the window cuts the longest lives; an exponential law gives $1$.  The logarithm of an
exponential variable has variance $\pi^2/6\simeq1.6$, which is the scale of the spread of $\ln n$ among the largest
firms ($0.91$ at fixed size, Sec.~\ref{sec:floor}).

\section{Protocol}
\label{app:protocol}

\paragraph{The year.}  We sample every $0.5$ time units.  The lag-one autocorrelation of growth, demeaned within
each life, for lives with at least five growth rates of the two reference economies, is $-0.05$, $+0.30$,
$+0.36$, $+0.40$ and $+0.46$ by fifth of size at step $0.5$; $+0.13$, $+0.31$, $+0.32$, $+0.32$ and $+0.31$ at
step $1$; and $+0.02$, $+0.06$, $+0.05$, $+0.02$ and $-0.01$ at step $2$.  Demeaning biases each value down by
about $1/n$, most among the short lives of small firms, so the rise with size is robust and the sign in the
smallest fifth is not.  At step $0.5$ the serial correlation rises with size, as in firm data
\cite{coad2007}; at step $2$ it is gone at every size, and a step of $2$ also leaves few growth rates per life.  In the two reference economies, lives with at least five
growth rates give $\zeta_q=0.208$, $0.403$, $0.572$, $0.711$ (ratios $1.94$, $2.75$, $3.41$) over $2.0$
decades at step $0.5$, and $\zeta_q=0.154$, $0.259$, $0.316$, $0.333$ (ratios $1.68$, $2.06$, $2.16$) over
$1.4$ decades at step $2$: volatility per step rises by a factor $1.1$ in the smallest size bin and $2.2$
in the largest, so the decline flattens, and the ratio curve bends further below $q$.  For lives with
at least $20$ growth rates the change is smaller ($\zeta_1=0.253$ and $0.247$, ratios $1.79$, $2.37$,
$2.80$ and $1.75$, $2.28$, $2.62$, although at step $2$ these lives span only $0.9$ decades).  The bend
below $q$ survives the coarser year; the level of $\zeta_1$ for short lives does not
(\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The factor in the volatility.}  If
$X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,s^2)$, then $\E|X-\mu|=s\int|z|\phi(z)\,\dd z=2s\int_0^\infty z\phi(z)\,\dd z=2s\phi_0=s\sqrt{2/\pi}$,
so $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\E|X-\mu|=s$.  For Gaussian growth $v$ estimates the standard deviation; for fat-tailed
growth it is a robust scale that is smaller than the standard deviation.

\paragraph{Minimum life length.}  MSB keep every firm with at least two growth rates; their firms have $46$ on
average, and their results do not change for firms with at least $20$.  Their growth rates are annual
differences rolled over quarters, $g_t=\ln S_{t+4}-\ln S_t$, so $46$ of them span about twelve years and $20$
about six, or five non-overlapping annual growth rates.  The model's lives are much shorter: $38\%$ of the
lives with at least two growth rates have exactly two ($N=16000$).  We therefore compare with data at
$n_{\min}=5$ and test the theory at $n_{\min}=20$, where each life is long enough to estimate its floor.  In
the production run $\zeta_q=0.180$, $0.434$, $0.708$, $0.965$ (ratios $2.41$, $3.94$, $5.36$) for $n_{\min}=2$, $0.209$,
$0.400$, $0.561$, $0.685$ ($1.92$, $2.69$, $3.28$) for $n_{\min}=5$, $0.218$, $0.388$, $0.508$, $0.582$ ($1.78$, $2.33$,
$2.67$) for $n_{\min}=10$ and $0.254$, $0.452$, $0.598$, $0.701$ ($1.78$, $2.35$, $2.75$) for $n_{\min}=20$, over $2.1$,
$2.0$, $1.8$ and $1.4$ decades: the ratios lie above $q$ for $n_{\min}=2$ and below from $n_{\min}=5$, as the
estimation noise of short lives requires (Sec.~\ref{sec:criterion}), and the range shrinks because long lives
do not occur at the newborn sizes.

$q\le2$ the residuals stay within $0.1$ in log units; for $q=3$ and $4$ the end bins lie above the lines.

\paragraph{The tail index $b$.}  For lives with at least two growth rates the model gives
$b=4.39$--$4.44$ at every parameter point, close to the Compustat value $4.6$.  A null with iid Laplace
growth rates, no differences between firms and the model's distribution of life lengths gives
$4.34$, so the agreement is an effect of short lives.  For longer lives $b$ grows: $5.2$, $5.5$ and $6.6$ in the production
run for $n_{\min}=5$, $10$ and $20$ ($5.7$ and $6.9$ at $N=16000$ for $10$ and $20$), against $9.3$ and $12.6$ for the Laplace null and $4.6$ in
Compustat for both samples (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{Volatility estimator.}  With the standard deviation instead of $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ times the mean
absolute deviation, $\zeta_1$ is $0.03$ lower and the ratios are $0.03$--$0.3$ higher (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb},
rows SD; production run: $\zeta_1=0.178$, ratios $1.95$, $2.81$, $3.54$ at $n_{\min}=5$).

\paragraph{Rescaled growth.}  MSB rescale each growth rate by its firm's leave-one-out mean and volatility,
$\hat g_t=(g_t-\bar g_{-t})/\sigma_{-t}$, so that a large rate does not shrink its own scale, and find densities
that hardly depend on size \cite{moran2024}.  We rescale the lives with at least five growth rates of the two
reference economies the same way (Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}).  Compustat's densities, kernel estimates in $25$ size
bins, are read from the vector paths of MSB's figures (\texttt{scripts/msb\_curves.py}); we use the plotted
curves because we could not reconcile the fitted parameters of their Table~1 with the plotted fit.  The
curves stop at a density of $10^{-4}$ on $|z|\le8$, so we compare the excess kurtosis on that window.  By fifth
of size the model gives $3.0$, $3.7$, $4.3$, $4.8$, $5.7$ and Compustat $4.2$, $5.6$, $5.1$, $5.4$, $5.7$
($3.5$--$4.2$ in its three smallest bins).  A Gaussian null with the same life lengths gives $2.5$, $2.4$, $1.6$,
$0.4$, $0.1$: among small firms most of the model's kurtosis is the rescaling of short lives.  The full fourth
moments are dominated by rare events beyond $|z|=8$, outside the range of the Compustat curves; rescaling
instead by each life's own volatility, lives with at least $10$ growth rates give $1.3$ to $23$ by fifth in the
production run (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).

\paragraph{The tail of the growth rates.}  The tails of the pooled growth rates of Fig.~\ref{fig:tails}a
are exponential, not power laws.  Over the $1.8\times10^7$ growth rates of lives with at least five, divided
by their common volatility, the Hill index of $|z|$ rises with the depth of the tail, $2.3$, $3.0$, $3.5$,
$4.3$ and $5.2$ for the top $10\%$, $3\%$, $1\%$, $0.3\%$ and $0.1\%$, as for a Laplace law ($3.1$ to $7.8$)
and unlike a power law (Student $t_3$: $2.5$ to $3.0$).  The slope of $\ln P(|z|>x)$ against $x$ is
$-0.53$, $-0.47$ and $-0.48$ over the three decades of probability from $10^{-2}$ to $10^{-5}$: an exponential
tail, fatter than the Laplace law of the same volatility (\texttt{notebooks/06\_facts.ipynb}).
